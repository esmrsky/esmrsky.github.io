Received

Rejection, acceptance, assumptions, triggers and irritation feel like five problems. They run on one thing: a need you keep bringing to people that only God can meet. Under the need sits a fear of being seen and turned away. He has seen you. He has already met it.

Start with the smallest one. Someone leaves you on read. A coworker takes the credit. Someone you love uses that tone. Nothing big happened, and you are irritated for an hour.

Follow the irritation down. It goes in steps.

  1. Irritation“That bothered me.”
  2. Expectation“I expected them to answer.”
  3. Demand“They should have.” An expectation you will punish someone for missing.
  4. Need“Because if they had, it would mean I matter to them.”

Every step is ordinary. The last one is the real one. Followed far enough, nearly every irritation ends at the same need: to be wanted, valued, taken in. The trouble is not the need. God made you with it. The trouble is where you take it.

A demand is a need with the wrong address on it.

Two pages on this site sit right beside this one. Measured found the verdict hiding under comparing and people-pleasing. The Gap showed how assuming works. This page asks the question under both: what are you asking people to give you?

01 · One need, five faces

The five words are what one need does

Once you see the need, the five words stop being five things. They are the same need at different points in the day. Pick one.

Rejection

The need refused, or the fear that it will be.

Sounds like
“They don’t want me.” “I’m too much.” “I’m not enough.”
Asks people
Want me.
Scripture
Though my father and mother forsake me, the Lord will receive me. Psalm 27:10

Assumption

Reading the refusal before it comes, so it can’t surprise you.

Sounds like
“They did that on purpose.” “I know what that look meant.”
Asks people
Prove me wrong, without my having to ask.
Scripture
To answer before listening— that is folly and shame. Proverbs 18:13

Trigger

Anything that touches the need. Usually small. Often old.

Sounds like
“Here we go again.” “Why does this always happen to me?”
Asks people
Don’t remind me.
Scripture
The tempter came to him and said, “If you are the Son of God, tell these stones to become bread.” Matthew 4:3

Irritation

The need unmet in a small way, felt as heat. Anger is the same thing, louder.

Sounds like
“Why is everyone like this?” “I’m fine.”
Asks people
Count me more than you just did.
Scripture
Do not be quickly provoked in your spirit, for anger resides in the lap of fools. Ecclesiastes 7:9

Acceptance

The need met at the root. It runs both ways: you are received, so you can receive.

Sounds like
“I don’t need this from you. So I can give you something.”
Asks people
Nothing they can’t give.
Scripture
Accept one another, then, just as Christ accepted you, in order to bring praise to God. Romans 15:7

They make a loop

  1. Need You bring it to people and it becomes a demand.
  2. Assumption A demand watches for refusal, so you start reading faces for it.
  3. Trigger Watching makes small things look like refusal. A pause, a tone, a seat taken.
  4. Irritation Each small refusal stings. You get sharper, quieter, or colder.
  5. Rejection Sharp, quiet and cold are hard to be near. People pull back a little.
  6. Back to the start That proves the need was right to be afraid, and the demand gets louder.

No better crowd breaks this. A new friend, a new church, a new job only restarts it. The way out is the need being met somewhere people can’t reach.

02 · Afraid

Under the need, a fear

The need to be received has a guard on it. Fear. Not of spiders or heights, but of being seen as you are and turned away.

The first time fear shows up in the Bible, it is this fear. Read what the first two people do in the garden. Most of this page is already in these four verses.

Then the eyes of both of them were opened, and they realized they were naked; so they sewed fig leaves together and made coverings for themselves. Then the man and his wife heard the sound of the Lord God as he was walking in the garden in the cool of the day, and they hid from the Lord God among the trees of the garden. But the Lord God called to the man, “Where are you?” He answered, “I heard you in the garden, and I was afraid because I was naked; so I hid.”

Genesis 3:7-10NIV

Count the moves. They feel exposed, so they cover. They hear him coming, so they hide. Two verses later, asked what happened, the man blames: The man said, “The woman you put here with me—she gave me some fruit from the tree, and I ate it.” Genesis 3:12 He blames her, and with “you put here,” he blames God too.

Fear does three things. It hides, covers and blames.

Every one of the three is still around. They just have new names.

Hide

So no one can turn you away

Pulling back. Numbing. Staying busy. Scrolling. Leaving before you can be left.

Now watch what God does. He doesn’t open with a charge. He opens with a question: “Where are you?” Genesis 3:9 He knew where they were. The question was an invitation to come out.

And before they leave the garden, he replaces their covering with his: The Lord God made garments of skin for Adam and his wife and clothed them. Genesis 3:21

Fig leaves are a covering you make. The garments were one they were given.

The fear of people

Proverbs names it: Fear of man will prove to be a snare, but whoever trusts in the Lord is kept safe. Proverbs 29:25 A snare is a trap you walk into because you didn’t see it. Saul walked into this one and said so: “I was afraid of the men and so I gave in to them.” 1 Samuel 15:24

Peter shows how small the trigger can be. The night Jesus was arrested, it wasn’t a soldier who scared him.

A servant girl saw him seated there in the firelight. She looked closely at him and said, “This man was with him.” But he denied it. “Woman, I don’t know him,” he said.

Luke 22:56-57NIV

A servant girl’s look was enough. Afterward Jesus didn’t lecture him. He asked him a question three times, one for each denial: “Simon son of John, do you love me?” John 21:15-17 Then he gave him work to do.

What the research sees

Psychology has looked hard at the fear of being judged. Two things stand out: it hits the body hard, and it guesses wrong.

  • Fear

    Being judged is one of the hardest stresses on the body

    A review of 208 lab studies found that stress tasks with an audience judging you, especially when you couldn’t control how it went, caused the biggest rise in the stress hormone cortisol and the slowest recovery. These were lab tasks, not daily life.

    Dickerson & Kemeny, 2004 · doi

    The fear of people is not a soft fear. A servant girl’s look was enough.

  • Fear

    We expect a harsher verdict than we get

    After an embarrassing slip, people predicted that onlookers would judge them more harshly than the onlookers actually did. Four studies, mostly in the lab.

    Savitsky, Epley & Gilovich, 2001 · doi

    Fear predicts a verdict. The real one is usually gentler.

  • Fear

    Fewer people are watching than you think

    People overestimate how much others notice their looks and their slips. In one study, students made to wear an embarrassing T-shirt guessed that more people noticed it than really had. The finding comes mostly from one research group.

    Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky, 2000 · doi

    Much of the spotlight you hide from is in your own head.

Jesus’ own answer to the fear of people is about worth:

Are not five sparrows sold for two pennies? Yet not one of them is forgotten by God. Indeed, the very hairs of your head are all numbered. Don’t be afraid; you are worth more than many sparrows.

Luke 12:6-7NIV

And John puts the cure in one line: There is no fear in love. But perfect love drives out fear, because fear has to do with punishment. The one who fears is not made perfect in love. 1 John 4:18 Fear has to do with punishment. It is waiting for a verdict. Love that has already given the verdict leaves it nothing to wait for.

03 · Where fights come from

“You do not have because you do not ask God”

What causes fights and quarrels among you? Don’t they come from your desires that battle within you? You desire but do not have, so you kill. You covet but you cannot get what you want, so you quarrel and fight. You do not have because you do not ask God.

James 4:1-2NIV

James asks the question this whole page is about, and answers it in one breath.

Fights come from wanting. A want turns into a fight when you can’t get it from the person in front of you. Then comes the line almost nobody quotes: you do not have because you do not ask God.

Read it in order. The want is not condemned. The quarrels come from taking it to the wrong place. People can’t fill it, so you push, and pushing is what a quarrel is. James’s fix is not “want less.” It is “ask God.”

Verse 3 keeps it honest. Even prayer can be a demand in disguise, asked “with wrong motives,” as a way to get from God what you still plan to spend on being seen by people. The question is not only who you ask. It is what you want it for.

Irritation is a small demand going unmet. A fight is a big one.

04 · Three angry men

God answers anger with a question

Three times in Scripture a man is angry because someone else was received. Three times God, or a father, asks him why. Not a lecture. A question, and an offer.

Cain

Genesis 4:3-8

God looks with favor on Abel’s offering and not on Cain’s. Cain reads favor on his brother as rejection of himself. “So Cain was very angry, and his face was downcast.”

Then the Lord said to Cain, “Why are you angry? Why is your face downcast? If you do what is right, will you not be accepted? But if you do not do what is right, sin is crouching at your door; it desires to have you, but you must rule over it.”

Genesis 4:6-7NIV

Acceptance is still on offer, in the same sentence as the warning. Then the picture: something crouching at the door. Anger is a door, and something waits on the other side of it. Cain never answers the question. He invites his brother out to the field.

Jonah

Jonah 4

God spares Nineveh, and Jonah is angry enough to want to die. He even says why: “I knew that you are a gracious and compassionate God.” His demand was that mercy stay inside Jonah’s lines.

“Is it right for you to be angry?”

God asks it twice Jonah 4:4, 9. The second time is about a plant that gave Jonah shade and then died overnight. That is the point. Big anger and small irritation run on the same wire. A man furious about a city is also furious about a plant. The book ends with God’s question still hanging, unanswered, which leaves it with the reader.

The older brother

Luke 15:25-32

The father throws a feast for the son who wasted everything. The older brother “became angry and refused to go in.” So the father goes out to him.

Listen to the complaint: But he answered his father, ‘Look! All these years I’ve been slaving for you and never disobeyed your orders. Yet you never gave me even a young goat so I could celebrate with my friends. Luke 15:29 He never asked for one.

“ ‘My son,’ the father said, ‘you are always with me, and everything I have is yours.

Luke 15:31NIV

He had it the whole time. He had been working for what was already his, and resenting anyone who got it free. Luke never tells us whether he went in.

05 · What the research sees

The pieces fit, even if no one study holds them all

No single study tests this whole loop. What psychology has is findings that fit together, step by step. Here are the ones that hold up when checked, with the limits stated.

  • Assumption

    Expecting rejection changes what you see

    Some people expect rejection, notice it quickly in unclear moments, and react strongly. Researchers call this rejection sensitivity.

    Downey & Feldman, 1996 · doi

    That is the assumption step. The demand sets the eyes.

  • Trigger

    Being left out makes neutral things look hostile

    In lab studies, people who had just been excluded read neutral or ambiguous behavior as more hostile, and then treated even uninvolved strangers more harshly.

    DeWall, Twenge, Gitter & Baumeister, 2009 · doi; Twenge et al., 2001 · doi

    That is the trigger step. After a refusal, the next small thing looks like another one.

  • Demand

    Anger tracks how much you think you count

    One well-tested account says anger rises when someone seems to give your interests less weight than you expected. The pattern held across six cultures in scenario experiments.

    Sell et al., 2017 · doi; Sell, Tooby & Cosmides, 2009 · doi

    That is the demand, measured: anger fires at the gap between how much you expected to count and how much you seem to.

  • Back to rejection

    The loop can make itself come true

    In dating couples, women high in rejection sensitivity behaved more negatively after conflict, and their partners then responded with more rejection. The samples were small, and the effect was clearer for women than for men.

    Downey, Freitas, Michaelis & Khouri, 1998 · doi

    That is the step from irritation back to rejection.

  • The need

    Worth that rides on approval swings with it

    When your sense of worth depends on other people’s approval, good and bad news in that area moves your mood and self-esteem more.

    Crocker & Wolfe, 2001 · doi; Crocker, Karpinski, Quinn & Chase, 2003 · doi

    The need with the wrong address on it, in a researcher’s words.

  • Received

    Feeling valued changes what tomorrow looks like

    Spouses who felt less valued read more rejection into stressful days, felt more hurt, and behaved worse the next day. Spouses who felt valued drew closer instead.

    Murray, Bellavia, Rose & Griffin, 2003 · doi

    The same day, read by a received person and an unreceived one, is a different day.

  • Assumption

    You are probably liked more than you think

    After a conversation, people tend to underestimate how much the other person liked them.

    Boothby, Cooney, Sandstrom & Clark, 2018 · doi

    Your assumption is usually harsher than the facts.

  • The need

    Exclusion hurts even when it shouldn’t

    Being ignored or left out lowers people’s sense of belonging, worth, control and meaning, even when it comes from a computer or from a group they despise. Across more than a hundred experiments the effect is large.

    Zadro, Williams & Richardson, 2004 · doi; Gonsalkorale & Williams, 2007 · doi; Hartgerink et al., 2015 · doi

    The need is real and built in. That is why it can’t be argued away, only met.

What this page is not claiming

  • That rejection uses the same brain circuit as physical pain. It is often said. It is disputed, and a large review of the brain-scan studies did not find it reliably.
  • That one study has proven the whole loop. The steps are each supported; the loop is our way of putting them together.
  • That irritation is “the rejection alarm at low volume.” That is this page’s reading of the anger research, not a finding.
  • That passive aggression is a well-studied everyday behavior. The research on it is thin. Section 10 leans on nearby findings about shame, blame and the silent treatment.
  • That contempt is the number-one predictor of divorce, or any “94%” figure. Those numbers come from prediction models that did much worse when tested on new couples.
  • That being left out makes people binge or reach for vices. One set of lab studies pointed that way and has not been repeated. Section 12 rests on Scripture, with two small findings beside it.
  • That “fawning” is an established finding. It is a therapy term without a tested measure. This page says people-pleasing instead.
  • That faith makes an insecure person secure as a measured fact. Believers often describe God as a safe place to come back to, and some experiments fit that. Nobody has shown it fixes attachment.
06 · Assumptions

Asked, or assumed

An assumption is a demand’s early warning system. It reads the refusal before it arrives, so it can hurt less. It almost always costs more than it saves. Here are two stories with the same setup and opposite endings.

Assumed

Hanun and the envoys

The king of Ammon dies, and David means nothing but kindness by it. Hanun’s advisers supply another reading:

David thought, “I will show kindness to Hanun son of Nahash, just as his father showed kindness to me.” So David sent a delegation to express his sympathy to Hanun concerning his father. When David’s men came to the land of the Ammonites, the Ammonite commanders said to Hanun their lord, “Do you think David is honoring your father by sending envoys to you to express sympathy? Hasn’t David sent them to you only to explore the city and spy it out and overthrow it?”

2 Samuel 10:2-3NIV

Nobody asks. Hanun shaves off half of each man’s beard, cuts their robes at the hip and sends them home.

A war follows that no one needed.

Asked

The altar by the Jordan

The tribes east of the Jordan build a huge altar at the river. The rest of Israel assumes rebellion and gathers to go to war Joshua 22:12. But first they send Phinehas to ask. The answer turns everything:

On the contrary, it is to be a witness between us and you and the generations that follow, that we will worship the Lord at his sanctuary with our burnt offerings, sacrifices and fellowship offerings. Then in the future your descendants will not be able to say to ours, ‘You have no share in the Lord.’

Joshua 22:27NIV

The altar was built out of fear of being rejected later. Both sides were acting from the same fear.

Asking ended it. “They were glad to hear the report and praised God” Joshua 22:33.

Proverbs puts the courtroom inside the head: In a lawsuit the first to speak seems right, until someone comes forward and cross-examines. Proverbs 18:17 Your assumption is always the first speaker. It sounds right because nobody has answered it yet.

Ecclesiastes gives the cross-examination: don’t listen to every word people say about you, for you know in your heart that many times you yourself have cursed others. Ecclesiastes 7:22 You have said careless things about people you love. They probably have too, and it meant about as much.

And love has a default for the unclear moment: It always protects, always trusts, always hopes, always perseveres. 1 Corinthians 13:7 Trust is not pretending nothing happened. It is choosing the kinder reading until you have asked.

For the machinery of assuming, why the mind fills gaps and how to hold a reading loosely, read The Gap.

07 · The person-lens

Once you’ve filed someone

Some assumptions are about a moment. This one is about a person.

You know their politics, their history, “how she is.” So you’ve filed them. Now everything they say gets read through the file, and every reading proves the file right. A joke is a dig. A question is a test. Silence is a message.

Jesus pointed this out about the people of his own day:

For John came neither eating nor drinking, and they say, ‘He has a demon.’ The Son of Man came eating and drinking, and they say, ‘Here is a glutton and a drunkard, a friend of tax collectors and sinners.’ But wisdom is proved right by her deeds.”

Matthew 11:18-19NIV

Opposite behavior, same verdict. John fasted, so he was strange. Jesus feasted, so he was loose. When the verdict comes first, any evidence will do.

Simon’s file

At a dinner, a woman known for her sins washes Jesus’ feet with her tears. The host, a Pharisee named Simon, doesn’t see what she is doing. He sees what she is: When the Pharisee who had invited him saw this, he said to himself, “If this man were a prophet, he would know who is touching him and what kind of woman she is—that she is a sinner.” Luke 7:39

Jesus answers with a question that is the whole cure:

Then he turned toward the woman and said to Simon, “Do you see this woman? I came into your house. You did not give me any water for my feet, but she wet my feet with her tears and wiped them with her hair.

Luke 7:44NIV

Simon had been looking at her all evening. He hadn’t seen her once.

Kept the file

Eliab

David brings food to his brothers at the front and asks a question about Goliath. His oldest brother has had him filed for years.

When Eliab, David’s oldest brother, heard him speaking with the men, he burned with anger at him and asked, “Why have you come down here? And with whom did you leave those few sheep in the wilderness? I know how conceited you are and how wicked your heart is; you came down only to watch the battle.” “Now what have I done?” said David. “Can’t I even speak?”

1 Samuel 17:28-29NIV

“I know how conceited you are.” The file answered before David finished his sentence.

Eliab missed the giant-killer standing in front of him.

Came and saw

Nathanael

Philip tells Nathanael they have found the one Moses wrote about, from Nazareth. Nathanael has the whole town filed.

“Nazareth! Can anything good come from there?” Nathanael asked. “Come and see,” said Philip.

John 1:46NIV

Philip doesn’t argue with the file. He asks Nathanael to look for himself. Nathanael goes, and a few lines later he is calling Jesus the Son of God John 1:49.

“Come and see” is how a file gets lifted.

A file doesn’t only misread people. It limits what can happen between you. When Jesus went home to Nazareth, they had known him since he was a boy. “Isn’t this the carpenter?” they said, “And they took offense at him.” Then Mark adds a startling line: He could not do any miracles there, except lay his hands on a few sick people and heal them. Mark 6:5

Why build a file at all? Because it feels safe. If you already know what they mean, they can’t surprise you or hurt you again. It is the assumption from section 06, made permanent. And Jesus says where to look first: You hypocrite, first take the plank out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to remove the speck from your brother’s eye. Matthew 7:5 A file usually says more about the filer’s need than about the person in it.

What the research sees

There is no study of “filing” a person by that name. There is good research on its parts.

  • Person-lens

    Unhappy couples read each other in the worst light

    A major review found that dissatisfied spouses explain a partner’s behavior in ways that make the partner look worse. A later study followed newlyweds over eight rounds and found those explanations shifted as satisfaction shifted. They were not a fixed trait.

    Bradbury & Fincham, 1990 · doi; Karney & Bradbury, 2000 · doi

    The file follows how it is going between you. That means it can change.

  • Person-lens

    What they meant and what you read often differ

    156 couples watched a video of their own upsetting conversation. What each person thought the partner meant matched the partner’s own account only weakly to moderately.

    Waldinger & Schulz, 2006 · doi

    The file says what they meant. Often they meant something else.

  • Person-lens

    Same act, different verdict

    Fourth graders heard the same small provocations and judged them more hostile when a classmate they disliked did it than when another classmate did. That held after accounting for reputation. These were children and imagined scenes; no clean adult version turned up.

    Peets, Hodges, Kikas & Salmivalli, 2007 · doi

    Once someone is filed, the file reads the act before you do.

Paul says being received changes how you see everyone else: So from now on we regard no one from a worldly point of view. Though we once regarded Christ in this way, we do so no longer. 2 Corinthians 5:16 If God didn’t keep you in your file, you can take someone else out of theirs.

For how lenses form in general and how to hold a reading loosely, read The Gap.

08 · Triggers

Every trigger has an “if” in it

A trigger is any cue that touches the need. It is usually small and often old: a tone that sounds like a parent, a silence that sounds like a breakup. The size of the reaction is set by the need, not by the cue. That is why other people can’t understand why something so small got to you.

Watch the order in Matthew. At the river, before Jesus has preached a sermon or done a miracle, a voice says:

And a voice from heaven said, “This is my Son, whom I love; with him I am well pleased.”

Matthew 3:17NIV

The very next scene, in the desert, the tempter’s first word is “if.” The tempter came to him and said, “If you are the Son of God, tell these stones to become bread.” Matthew 4:3

That is what a trigger does. It reopens a question that was already answered. Jesus does not argue for who he is, and he does not prove it with bread. He answers from what God has said.

Listen for the if in your own: if they respected me, if I mattered, if I were enough.

Saul and the song

Saul’s trigger is a song. After David kills Goliath, the women come out dancing and singing: “Saul has slain his thousands, and David his tens of thousands.” A victory chant. Saul hears a ranking.

Saul was very angry; this refrain displeased him greatly. “They have credited David with tens of thousands,” he thought, “but me with only thousands. What more can he get but the kingdom?” And from that time on Saul kept a close eye on David.

1 Samuel 18:8-9NIV

The whole loop is in two verses. The trigger is the song. “What more can he get but the kingdom?” is the assumption. “Kept a close eye” is the watching that never switches off, and it runs the rest of Saul’s life.

The root shows three chapters earlier, when Saul is caught disobeying God. His excuse: Then Saul said to Samuel, “I have sinned. I violated the Lord’s command and your instructions. I was afraid of the men and so I gave in to them. 1 Samuel 15:24 And his request: Saul replied, “I have sinned. But please honor me before the elders of my people and before Israel; come back with me, so that I may worship the Lord your God.” 1 Samuel 15:30 A king asking the crowd for what only God could give him. The crowd was the only court he had left, and a crowd votes every day.

09 · Irritation

Small, and telling

Irritation is the most common of the five and the easiest to wave off. It is small. But small is not meaningless. It is where the need shows first, before it is big enough to name.

Scripture does not call the feeling itself sin. “In your anger do not sin”: Do not let the sun go down while you are still angry, and do not give the devil a foothold. Ephesians 4:26-27 The feeling is a signal. The foothold is what you let it build overnight.

So go slow with it: My dear brothers and sisters, take note of this: Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak and slow to become angry, because human anger does not produce the righteousness that God desires. James 1:19-20 And left alone, it grows. Hebrews calls it a bitter root that troubles many Hebrews 12:15.

A person’s wisdom yields patience; it is to one’s glory to overlook an offense.

Proverbs 19:11NIV

Overlooking an offense is only possible for someone who doesn’t need the apology. That is why Proverbs calls it glory. It costs something only a secure person can pay.

The most useful thing to do with irritation is to answer God’s question to Cain and Jonah honestly. Why are you angry? Not “because they were rude.” What did you expect, and what would it have meant if you’d gotten it? The tool in section 16 walks through exactly that.

10 · Hidden anger

Anger that won’t say its name

Not all anger shouts. Most of it sighs, goes quiet, runs late, or says “I’m fine.”

Passive aggression is anger that is afraid to be seen. Read it through this page and it is a demand you are too scared to say out loud. You want something from them. Asking feels too risky, because they could say no. So the demand leaks out sideways, where you can always deny it.

Scripture is full of people who kept it in. Each one leaked it a different way.

Absalom

2 Samuel 13
Silence

And Absalom never said a word to Amnon, either good or bad; he hated Amnon because he had disgraced his sister Tamar.

2 Samuel 13:22NIV

His anger was just. His silence was not peace. Two years later he had Amnon killed 2 Samuel 13:23-29.

Ahab

1 Kings 21
Sulking

So Ahab went home, sullen and angry because Naboth the Jezreelite had said, “I will not give you the inheritance of my ancestors.” He lay on his bed sulking and refused to eat.

1 Kings 21:4NIV

A king, sulking over a vineyard. The sulk worked. His wife saw it and arranged Naboth’s death for him.

Michal

2 Samuel 6
Sarcasm

As the ark of the Lord was entering the City of David, Michal daughter of Saul watched from a window. And when she saw King David leaping and dancing before the Lord, she despised him in her heart.

2 Samuel 6:16NIV

“In her heart” first. When it came out, it came out as sarcasm: “How the king of Israel has distinguished himself today” 2 Samuel 6:20.

Martha

Luke 10:38-42
Complaining sideways

She doesn’t ask her sister for help. She asks Jesus to make her sister help, in front of her sister: “Lord, don’t you care that my sister has left me to do the work by myself? Tell her to help me!” Luke 10:40

Jesus doesn’t scold her. He answers what is under it: “Martha, Martha,” he says, “you are worried and upset about many things” Luke 10:41.

Haman

Esther 5
Resentment

He has wealth, sons, and a private dinner with the king and queen. One man at the gate won’t bow to him.

But all this gives me no satisfaction as long as I see that Jew Mordecai sitting at the king’s gate.”

Esther 5:13NIV

All of it, cancelled by one man who wouldn’t show respect.

The older brother

Luke 15:28
Refusing to go in

Section 04 met him. Look again at what he does. He doesn’t storm in and say it. He stands outside the party and won’t come in. That is a sulk.

His father does what the sulk was asking for. He comes out to him.

What the research sees

“Passive aggression” itself has thin research. The studied pieces around it point the same way.

  • Hidden anger

    Shame turns into blame

    In two student studies, people prone to shame were also more prone to anger, suspicion, resentment, blaming others and indirect hostility. People prone to guilt, which is about what you did rather than who you are, went the other way. The data are self-reports and correlations.

    Tangney, Wagner, Fletcher & Gramzow, 1992 · doi

    Hide, then blame. Section 02, in a questionnaire.

  • Hidden anger

    The silent treatment looks different from each side

    When people described times they gave or got the silent treatment, those on the receiving end reported a threat to belonging and resentment. Those who used it often called it a useful tactic. These were recalled stories, not observed behavior.

    Sommer, Williams, Ciarocco & Baumeister, 2001 · doi

    The one who goes quiet calls it a tool. The one who gets the quiet calls it a wall.

  • Hidden anger

    Venting doesn’t drain it

    People who hit a punching bag while thinking about someone who had angered them ended up angrier and more aggressive than people who were distracted or did nothing. This compared venting to distraction, not to bottling it up.

    Bushman, 2002 · doi

    Sighs and sarcasm are small vents. They keep the anger warm. The answer below is not to bottle it up either.

What God says to do instead

“ ‘Do not hate a fellow Israelite in your heart. Rebuke your neighbor frankly so you will not share in their guilt.

Leviticus 19:17NIV

Look at what the verse puts side by side. Hate kept in the heart, or a frank word said out loud. It treats the frank word as the loving one.

Jesus puts it ahead of worship. Contempt gets named in the same breath as murder: “anyone who says, ‘You fool!’ will be in danger of the fire of hell.” Matthew 5:22 Then:

“Therefore, if you are offering your gift at the altar and there remember that your brother or sister has something against you, leave your gift there in front of the altar. First go and be reconciled to them; then come and offer your gift.

Matthew 5:23-24NIV

And the way to do it is small and private: “just between the two of you” Matthew 18:15. Not a hint. Not a post. Not a sigh. A sentence, to their face, about the thing.

Passive aggression asks them to guess. Love tells them.

Saying it plainly is hard for the same reason overlooking is. It risks a no. Only someone whose need is already met can afford that risk. That is why Paul puts the letting go and the kindness in one sentence, and grounds both in what was already done for you: Get rid of all bitterness, rage and anger, brawling and slander, along with every form of malice. Be kind and compassionate to one another, forgiving each other, just as in Christ God forgave you. Ephesians 4:31-32

11 · People-pleasing

Earning a yes you already have

People-pleasing is the fig leaf with good manners. It covers you by keeping everyone happy, so no one ever has a reason to turn you away.

It looks like kindness. The test is what happens when someone is upset with you. Kindness can live with that. Pleasing can’t. It has to fix it, because their mood has become your verdict.

Leah’s four sons

Leah is married to Jacob, who loves her sister. She has four sons, and she names each one after what she hopes it will win.

  1. Son 1

    Reuben

    “Surely my husband will love me now.”

    Genesis 29:32Aimed at Jacob
  2. Son 2

    Simeon

    “Because the Lord heard that I am not loved, he gave me this one too.”

    Genesis 29:33Still about Jacob
  3. Son 3

    Levi

    “Now at last my husband will become attached to me, because I have borne him three sons.”

    Genesis 29:34Aimed at Jacob
  4. Son 4

    Judah

    “This time I will praise the Lord.”

    Genesis 29:35Turned to God

Three times the hope points at Jacob. The fourth time it turns. Nothing about Jacob has changed. Leah has. And the son she named with praise is the one Jesus’ family line runs through Matthew 1:2-3.

What pleasing costs

Pleasing is not harmless. In each of these, someone else paid for it.

  • Aaron

    The crowd wanted a god they could see, and Aaron wanted the crowd. Then he told Moses the calf just happened: So I told them, ‘Whoever has any gold jewelry, take it off.’ Then they gave me the gold, and I threw it into the fire, and out came this calf!” Exodus 32:24

  • Herod

    He didn’t want John dead. He wanted to keep face at his own party. The king was greatly distressed, but because of his oaths and his dinner guests, he did not want to refuse her. Mark 6:26

  • Pilate

    He asked the crowd himself, “What crime has he committed?” Mark 15:14 Then: Wanting to satisfy the crowd, Pilate released Barabbas to them. He had Jesus flogged, and handed him over to be crucified. Mark 15:15

  • Peter, again

    Years after the servant girl, at Antioch, Paul watched him do it again: For before certain men came from James, he used to eat with the Gentiles. But when they arrived, he began to draw back and separate himself from the Gentiles because he was afraid of those who belonged to the circumcision group. Galatians 2:12 Others followed him, even Barnabas.

  • The leaders who believed

    They believed in Jesus and kept quiet about it, for they loved human praise more than praise from God. John 12:43

What the research sees

The research on people-pleasing is young, but the cost shows up clearly.

  • People-pleasing

    Giving to keep the peace costs more

    In a two-week daily study of dating couples, giving something up out of love went with better well-being and a better relationship. Giving something up to avoid conflict or to avoid disappointing a partner went with worse, and over time it was particularly harmful.

    Impett, Gable & Peplau, 2005 · doi

    Same sacrifice, different reason. Pleasing gives to avoid a verdict. Love gives because it already has one.

  • People-pleasing

    Smiling through it costs you both

    Hiding what you feel while giving something up went with lower well-being and relationship quality for both partners, and more thoughts of breaking up three months later. In a separate lab study of women who had just met, hiding emotion in a conversation raised the other person’s blood pressure.

    Impett et al., 2012 · doi; Butler et al., 2003 · doi

    Pleasing hides. The person you are hiding from can feel it.

  • People-pleasing

    It only just got a proper measure

    A ten-item people-pleasing scale was published in 2026, tested in two German samples. A 2025 study of 2,203 Chinese university students linked more people-pleasing with lower well-being. No one has yet tested whether pleasing is a search for acceptance.

    Blötner, 2026 · doi; Kuang et al., 2025 · doi

    So this section rests mostly on Scripture. The research is starting to catch up.

Paul gives the test in one question:

Am I now trying to win the approval of human beings, or of God? Or am I trying to please people? If I were still trying to please people, I would not be a servant of Christ.

Galatians 1:10NIV

This is not “stop caring what people feel.” Paul cared enough to cry over his churches 2 Corinthians 2:4. The difference is whose approval he was living from: On the contrary, we speak as those approved by God to be entrusted with the gospel. We are not trying to please people but God, who tests our hearts. 1 Thessalonians 2:4 Approved first. Then free to serve, and free to say no.

Measured follows people-pleasing down to the verdict underneath it.

12 · Broken cisterns

What we drink instead

When the need goes unmet long enough, people stop asking and start numbing. The habits are different. The thirst is the same.

“My people have committed two sins: They have forsaken me, the spring of living water, and have dug their own cisterns, broken cisterns that cannot hold water.

Jeremiah 2:13NIV

Two mistakes, not one. Leaving the spring, and then digging. A cistern is work. You dig it, you line it, and then it leaks. That is a fair picture of most escapes: a lot of effort for something that won’t hold.

What the cisterns look like now

Each one fakes a piece of acceptance for a little while.

  • ScrollingA stream of small yeses. Seen by many, known by none.
  • OverworkWorth you can count. Until the next week starts at zero.
  • A drink, a pill, a bingeAn hour off from the question.
  • PornWanted, with no risk of being turned down.
  • GossipBelonging, paid for with someone who isn’t in the room.
  • CynicismYou can’t be let down if you never hope.
  • Leaving firstYou can’t be left if you go before they do.
  • WithdrawingYou can’t be refused if you never ask.

What the research sees

Whether being left out drives people to these habits has barely been tested. Two findings sit close.

  • Escapes

    Loneliness and heavy screen use feed each other, a little

    A 2025 review of 26 long-term studies, covering 24,798 people, found that loneliness predicted later problem use of media, and problem use predicted later loneliness. In the stronger analyses both links were small.

    Fam & Männikkö, 2025 · doi

    Scrolling is a leaky cistern: a little relief now, a little more thirst later.

  • Escapes

    Bonding over someone who isn’t there

    Strangers who found they shared a dislike of a third person felt closer than strangers who shared a liking, at least when the dislike was mild. They didn’t expect that to happen.

    Bosson, Johnson, Niederhoffer & Swann, 2006 · doi; Weaver & Bosson, 2011 · doi

    That is why gossip works as a cistern. It really does feel like being let in. Someone else pays for it.

Thirsty people in Scripture

Babel and Abram want the same thing. The builders say, “let us build ourselves a city, with a tower that reaches to the heavens, so that we may make a name for ourselves” Genesis 11:4. One chapter later, God says to Abram, “I will make your name great” Genesis 12:2. One name is dug. The other is given.

Esau trades his birthright for one bowl of stew, because the stew is right now: “Look, I am about to die,” he says. “What good is the birthright to me?” Genesis 25:32 Every escape makes that trade.

The younger son spends everything in the far country and ends up feeding pigs. Luke’s saddest line is a line about acceptance: He longed to fill his stomach with the pods that the pigs were eating, but no one gave him anything. Luke 15:16

The woman at the well

She comes for water at noon, by herself. She has had five husbands, and the man she lives with now is not her husband John 4:18. Jesus asks her for a drink, and then offers her one:

Jesus answered, “Everyone who drinks this water will be thirsty again, but whoever drinks the water I give them will never thirst. Indeed, the water I give them will become in them a spring of water welling up to eternal life.”

John 4:13-14NIV

He tells her everything she has done, and he doesn’t leave. Watch what she does next: Then, leaving her water jar, the woman went back to the town and said to the people, “Come, see a man who told me everything I ever did. Could this be the Messiah?” John 4:28-29

She left the jar. And she went to the people.

Fully known and not turned away, she doesn’t need to hide from the town anymore. Zacchaeus goes the same way. Jesus picks him out of the crowd and says, “I must stay at your house today” Luke 19:5, before Zacchaeus has changed a thing. Then he gives away half of what he owns Luke 19:8. Received first. Then given.

“Come, all you who are thirsty, come to the waters; and you who have no money, come, buy and eat! Come, buy wine and milk without money and without cost. Why spend money on what is not bread, and your labor on what does not satisfy? Listen, listen to me, and eat what is good, and you will delight in the richest of fare.

Isaiah 55:1-2NIV
13 · How Jesus lived it

He did not hide, cover or blame

Jesus wanted people. The night he was arrested he asked his friends, “Stay here and keep watch with me” Matthew 26:38. But he never took the need to the crowd to be settled. It was settled at the river, before he had done anything.

Watch what that freed him to do.

Didn’t hide

He let himself be seen

He wept in front of a crowd John 11:35. He said what he wanted, out loud. He asked people plain questions, even obvious ones: “What do you want me to do for you?” Jesus asked him. The blind man said, “Rabbi, I want to see.” Mark 10:51

Didn’t cover

He didn’t need the crowd’s verdict

But Jesus would not entrust himself to them, for he knew all people. He did not need any testimony about mankind, for he knew what was in each person. John 2:24-25 When they told him, “Everyone is looking for you!”, he left for the next towns Mark 1:37-38.

Didn’t blame

He let people go, and forgave

He looked at the rich young man and loved him, and let him walk away Mark 10:21-22. When many followers turned back, he asked the Twelve, “You do not want to leave too, do you?” John 6:67 On the cross: “Father, forgive them” Luke 23:34.

What he taught about it

Jesus talked about the crowd’s approval more than you might expect. He treated it as a real reward, paid on the spot:

“So when you give to the needy, do not announce it with trumpets, as the hypocrites do in the synagogues and on the streets, to be honored by others. Truly I tell you, they have received their reward in full.

Matthew 6:2NIV

“Received their reward in full.” Applause pays, and then it is spent. So he points to another audience, the one people-pleasing was looking for all along: But when you pray, go into your room, close the door and pray to your Father, who is unseen. Then your Father, who sees what is done in secret, will reward you. Matthew 6:6

He also said the crowd’s approval gets in the way of faith itself:

How can you believe since you accept glory from one another but do not seek the glory that comes from the only God?

John 5:44NIV

And he named the whole project of covering: He said to them, “You are the ones who justify yourselves in the eyes of others, but God knows your hearts. What people value highly is detestable in God’s sight. Luke 16:15 God knows your heart. That sounds like a threat until you remember he knows it and still came for you.

He was received at the river. Everything after that was given.

14 · Already yours

He was not received, so you could be

Here is where the loop breaks. Not by needing less. By the need being met where people can’t reach it.

Jesus took refusal all the way down. His town tried to throw him off a cliff. His friends ran. His nation handed him over. Isaiah saw it centuries ahead: He was despised and rejected by mankind, a man of suffering, and familiar with pain. Like one from whom people hide their faces he was despised, and we held him in low esteem. Isaiah 53:3 On the cross he cried out the words of a man forsaken Mark 15:34.

He came to that which was his own, but his own did not receive him. Yet to all who did receive him, to those who believed in his name, he gave the right to become children of God—

John 1:11-12NIV

The rejection was carried, so the welcome could be given. Peter puts both halves in one line: As you come to him, the living Stone—rejected by humans but chosen by God and precious to him— 1 Peter 2:4 In the next breath he says the same of everyone who comes to him.

What is already said

The need has a short list of questions. Each one already has an answer.

  • Am I wanted?

    All those the Father gives me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never drive away. John 6:37

  • Am I enough?

    to the praise of his glorious grace, which he has freely given us in the One he loves. Ephesians 1:6 Older translations put it “accepted in the beloved.”

  • Am I still on trial?

    Who will bring any charge against those whom God has chosen? It is God who justifies. Who then is the one who condemns? No one. Christ Jesus who died—more than that, who was raised to life—is at the right hand of God and is also interceding for us. Romans 8:33-34

  • What if the people closest to me leave?

    Though my father and mother forsake me, the Lord will receive me. Psalm 27:10

  • What about what I still need?

    And my God will meet all your needs according to the riches of his glory in Christ Jesus. Philippians 4:19

Go back to the older brother one more time. The father did not argue with him or shame him. He told him what he owned: “everything I have is yours.” The demand ends when you believe the inventory.

And go back to James. “You do not have because you do not ask God.” Asking is the practical shape of all of this. When the need shows up as irritation, take it to the one who has already answered. If you, then, though you are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father in heaven give good gifts to those who ask him! Matthew 7:11

15 · Living from it

“So he got up”

Jesus knew that the Father had put all things under his power, and that he had come from God and was returning to God; so he got up from the meal, took off his outer clothing, and wrapped a towel around his waist. After that, he poured water into a basin and began to wash his disciples’ feet, drying them with the towel that was wrapped around him.

John 13:3-5NIV

John gives the reason before the towel. Jesus knew where he came from, where he was going, and what was in his hands. So he got up and washed feet, including the feet of the man who would sell him that night.

You can’t wash Judas’s feet while you still need something from Judas.

Security came first. Service came out of it. Received people become the ones who receive. We love because he first loved us. 1 John 4:19 The loop runs the other way.

Four moves when it fires

None of these work as techniques on their own. Each one is possible only for someone who isn’t demanding the thing anymore.

Ask

When you are reading a refusal into something unclear

Do what Phinehas did. Go and find out before you act. “If your brother or sister sins, go and point out their fault, just between the two of you. If they listen to you, you have won them over. Matthew 18:15

Overlook

When it was small, and you are secure enough to let it go

Not pretending. Choosing. It is “to one’s glory to overlook an offense” Proverbs 19:11, and “love keeps no record of wrongs” 1 Corinthians 13:5.

Entrust

When it was real, and you can’t fix it

When they hurled their insults at him, he did not retaliate; when he suffered, he made no threats. Instead, he entrusted himself to him who judges justly. 1 Peter 2:23 Hand the case to the Judge.

Receive

When someone is hard to accept

Give them what you were given, before they earn it. Accept one another, then, just as Christ accepted you, in order to bring praise to God. Romans 15:7
