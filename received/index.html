Start with the smallest one. Someone leaves you on read. A coworker takes the credit. Someone you love uses that tone. Nothing big happened, and you are irritated for an hour.

Follow the irritation down. It goes in steps.

  1. Irritation“That bothered me.”
  2. Expectation“I expected them to answer.”
  3. Demand“They should have.” An expectation you will punish someone for missing.
  4. Need“Because if they had, it would mean I matter to them.”

Every step is ordinary. The last one is the real one. Followed far enough, nearly every irritation ends at the same need: to be wanted, valued, taken in. The trouble is not the need. God made you with it. The trouble is where you take it.

A demand is a need with the wrong address on it.

Two pages on this site sit right beside this one. Measured found the verdict hiding under comparing and people-pleasing. The Gap showed how assuming works. This page asks the question under both: what are you asking people to give you?

01 · One need, five faces

The five words are what one need does

Once you see the need, the five words stop being five things. They are the same need at different points in the day. Pick one.

Rejection

The need refused, or the fear that it will be.

Sounds like
“They don’t want me.” “I’m too much.” “I’m not enough.”
Asks people
Want me.
Scripture
Though my father and mother forsake me, the Lord will receive me. Psalm 27:10

Assumption

Reading the refusal before it comes, so it can’t surprise you.

Sounds like
“They did that on purpose.” “I know what that look meant.”
Asks people
Prove me wrong, without my having to ask.
Scripture
To answer before listening— that is folly and shame. Proverbs 18:13

Trigger

Anything that touches the need. Usually small. Often old.

Sounds like
“Here we go again.” “Why does this always happen to me?”
Asks people
Don’t remind me.
Scripture
The tempter came to him and said, “If you are the Son of God, tell these stones to become bread.” Matthew 4:3

Irritation

The need unmet in a small way, felt as heat. Anger is the same thing, louder.

Sounds like
“Why is everyone like this?” “I’m fine.”
Asks people
Count me more than you just did.
Scripture
Do not be quickly provoked in your spirit, for anger resides in the lap of fools. Ecclesiastes 7:9

Acceptance

The need met at the root. It runs both ways: you are received, so you can receive.

Sounds like
“I don’t need this from you. So I can give you something.”
Asks people
Nothing they can’t give.
Scripture
Accept one another, then, just as Christ accepted you, in order to bring praise to God. Romans 15:7

They make a loop

  1. Need You bring it to people and it becomes a demand.
  2. Assumption A demand watches for refusal, so you start reading faces for it.
  3. Trigger Watching makes small things look like refusal. A pause, a tone, a seat taken.
  4. Irritation Each small refusal stings. You get sharper, quieter, or colder.
  5. Rejection Sharp, quiet and cold are hard to be near. People pull back a little.
  6. Back to the start That proves the need was right to be afraid, and the demand gets louder.

No better crowd breaks this. A new friend, a new church, a new job only restarts it. The way out is the need being met somewhere people can’t reach.

02 · Where fights come from

“You do not have because you do not ask God”

What causes fights and quarrels among you? Don’t they come from your desires that battle within you? You desire but do not have, so you kill. You covet but you cannot get what you want, so you quarrel and fight. You do not have because you do not ask God.

James 4:1-2NIV

James asks the question this whole page is about, and answers it in one breath.

Fights come from wanting. A want turns into a fight when you can’t get it from the person in front of you. Then comes the line almost nobody quotes: you do not have because you do not ask God.

Read it in order. The want is not condemned. The quarrels come from taking it to the wrong place. People can’t fill it, so you push, and pushing is what a quarrel is. James’s fix is not “want less.” It is “ask God.”

Verse 3 keeps it honest. Even prayer can be a demand in disguise, asked “with wrong motives,” as a way to get from God what you still plan to spend on being seen by people. The question is not only who you ask. It is what you want it for.

Irritation is a small demand going unmet. A fight is a big one.

03 · Three angry men

God answers anger with a question

Three times in Scripture a man is angry because someone else was received. Three times God, or a father, asks him why. Not a lecture. A question, and an offer.

Cain

Genesis 4:3-8

God looks with favor on Abel’s offering and not on Cain’s. Cain reads favor on his brother as rejection of himself. “So Cain was very angry, and his face was downcast.”

Then the Lord said to Cain, “Why are you angry? Why is your face downcast? If you do what is right, will you not be accepted? But if you do not do what is right, sin is crouching at your door; it desires to have you, but you must rule over it.”

Genesis 4:6-7NIV

Acceptance is still on offer, in the same sentence as the warning. Then the picture: something crouching at the door. Anger is a door, and something waits on the other side of it. Cain never answers the question. He invites his brother out to the field.

Jonah

Jonah 4

God spares Nineveh, and Jonah is angry enough to want to die. He even says why: “I knew that you are a gracious and compassionate God.” His demand was that mercy stay inside Jonah’s lines.

“Is it right for you to be angry?”

God asks it twice Jonah 4:4, 9. The second time is about a plant that gave Jonah shade and then died overnight. That is the point. Big anger and small irritation run on the same wire. A man furious about a city is also furious about a plant. The book ends with God’s question still hanging, unanswered, which leaves it with the reader.

The older brother

Luke 15:25-32

The father throws a feast for the son who wasted everything. The older brother “became angry and refused to go in.” So the father goes out to him.

Listen to the complaint: But he answered his father, ‘Look! All these years I’ve been slaving for you and never disobeyed your orders. Yet you never gave me even a young goat so I could celebrate with my friends. Luke 15:29 He never asked for one.

“ ‘My son,’ the father said, ‘you are always with me, and everything I have is yours.

Luke 15:31NIV

He had it the whole time. He had been working for what was already his, and resenting anyone who got it free. Luke never tells us whether he went in.

04 · What the research sees

The pieces fit, even if no one study holds them all

No single study tests this whole loop. What psychology has is findings that fit together, step by step. Here are the ones that hold up when checked, with the limits stated.

  • Expecting rejection changes what you see

    Some people expect rejection, notice it quickly in unclear moments, and react strongly. Researchers call this rejection sensitivity.

    Downey & Feldman, 1996 · doi

    That is the assumption step. The demand sets the eyes.

  • Being left out makes neutral things look hostile

    In lab studies, people who had just been excluded read neutral or ambiguous behavior as more hostile, and then treated even uninvolved strangers more harshly.

    DeWall, Twenge, Gitter & Baumeister, 2009 · doi; Twenge et al., 2001 · doi

    That is the trigger step. After a refusal, the next small thing looks like another one.

  • Anger tracks how much you think you count

    One well-tested account says anger rises when someone seems to give your interests less weight than you expected. The pattern held across six cultures in scenario experiments.

    Sell et al., 2017 · doi; Sell, Tooby & Cosmides, 2009 · doi

    That is the demand, measured: anger fires at the gap between how much you expected to count and how much you seem to.

  • The loop can make itself come true

    In dating couples, women high in rejection sensitivity behaved more negatively after conflict, and their partners then responded with more rejection. The samples were small, and the effect was clearer for women than for men.

    Downey, Freitas, Michaelis & Khouri, 1998 · doi

    That is the step from irritation back to rejection.

  • Worth that rides on approval swings with it

    When your sense of worth depends on other people’s approval, good and bad news in that area moves your mood and self-esteem more.

    Crocker & Wolfe, 2001 · doi; Crocker, Karpinski, Quinn & Chase, 2003 · doi

    The need with the wrong address on it, in a researcher’s words.

  • Feeling valued changes what tomorrow looks like

    Spouses who felt less valued read more rejection into stressful days, felt more hurt, and behaved worse the next day. Spouses who felt valued drew closer instead.

    Murray, Bellavia, Rose & Griffin, 2003 · doi

    The same day, read by a received person and an unreceived one, is a different day.

  • You are probably liked more than you think

    After a conversation, people tend to underestimate how much the other person liked them.

    Boothby, Cooney, Sandstrom & Clark, 2018 · doi

    Your assumption is usually harsher than the facts.

  • Exclusion hurts even when it shouldn’t

    Being ignored or left out lowers people’s sense of belonging, worth, control and meaning, even when it comes from a computer or from a group they despise. Across more than a hundred experiments the effect is large.

    Zadro, Williams & Richardson, 2004 · doi; Gonsalkorale & Williams, 2007 · doi; Hartgerink et al., 2015 · doi

    The need is real and built in. That is why it can’t be argued away, only met.

What this page is not claiming

  • That rejection uses the same brain circuit as physical pain. It is often said. It is disputed, and a large review of the brain-scan studies did not find it reliably.
  • That one study has proven the whole loop. The steps are each supported; the loop is our way of putting them together.
  • That irritation is “the rejection alarm at low volume.” That is this page’s reading of the anger research, not a finding.
  • That faith makes an insecure person secure as a measured fact. Believers often describe God as a safe place to come back to, and some experiments fit that. Nobody has shown it fixes attachment.
05 · Assumptions

Asked, or assumed

An assumption is a demand’s early warning system. It reads the refusal before it arrives, so it can hurt less. It almost always costs more than it saves. Here are two stories with the same setup and opposite endings.

Assumed

Hanun and the envoys

The king of Ammon dies, and David means nothing but kindness by it. Hanun’s advisers supply another reading:

David thought, “I will show kindness to Hanun son of Nahash, just as his father showed kindness to me.” So David sent a delegation to express his sympathy to Hanun concerning his father. When David’s men came to the land of the Ammonites, the Ammonite commanders said to Hanun their lord, “Do you think David is honoring your father by sending envoys to you to express sympathy? Hasn’t David sent them to you only to explore the city and spy it out and overthrow it?”

2 Samuel 10:2-3NIV

Nobody asks. Hanun shaves off half of each man’s beard, cuts their robes at the hip and sends them home.

A war follows that no one needed.

Asked

The altar by the Jordan

The tribes east of the Jordan build a huge altar at the river. The rest of Israel assumes rebellion and gathers to go to war Joshua 22:12. But first they send Phinehas to ask. The answer turns everything:

On the contrary, it is to be a witness between us and you and the generations that follow, that we will worship the Lord at his sanctuary with our burnt offerings, sacrifices and fellowship offerings. Then in the future your descendants will not be able to say to ours, ‘You have no share in the Lord.’

Joshua 22:27NIV

The altar was built out of fear of being rejected later. Both sides were acting from the same fear.

Asking ended it. “They were glad to hear the report and praised God” Joshua 22:33.

Proverbs puts the courtroom inside the head: In a lawsuit the first to speak seems right, until someone comes forward and cross-examines. Proverbs 18:17 Your assumption is always the first speaker. It sounds right because nobody has answered it yet.

Ecclesiastes gives the cross-examination: don’t listen to every word people say about you, for you know in your heart that many times you yourself have cursed others. Ecclesiastes 7:22 You have said careless things about people you love. They probably have too, and it meant about as much.

And love has a default for the unclear moment: It always protects, always trusts, always hopes, always perseveres. 1 Corinthians 13:7 Trust is not pretending nothing happened. It is choosing the kinder reading until you have asked.

For the machinery of assuming, why the mind fills gaps and how to hold a reading loosely, read The Gap.

06 · Triggers

Every trigger has an “if” in it

A trigger is any cue that touches the need. It is usually small and often old: a tone that sounds like a parent, a silence that sounds like a breakup. The size of the reaction is set by the need, not by the cue. That is why other people can’t understand why something so small got to you.

Watch the order in Matthew. At the river, before Jesus has preached a sermon or done a miracle, a voice says:

And a voice from heaven said, “This is my Son, whom I love; with him I am well pleased.”

Matthew 3:17NIV

The very next scene, in the desert, the tempter’s first word is “if.” The tempter came to him and said, “If you are the Son of God, tell these stones to become bread.” Matthew 4:3

That is what a trigger does. It reopens a question that was already answered. Jesus does not argue for who he is, and he does not prove it with bread. He answers from what God has said.

Listen for the if in your own: if they respected me, if I mattered, if I were enough.

Saul and the song

Saul’s trigger is a song. After David kills Goliath, the women come out dancing and singing: “Saul has slain his thousands, and David his tens of thousands.” A victory chant. Saul hears a ranking.

Saul was very angry; this refrain displeased him greatly. “They have credited David with tens of thousands,” he thought, “but me with only thousands. What more can he get but the kingdom?” And from that time on Saul kept a close eye on David.

1 Samuel 18:8-9NIV

The whole loop is in two verses. The trigger is the song. “What more can he get but the kingdom?” is the assumption. “Kept a close eye” is the watching that never switches off, and it runs the rest of Saul’s life.

The root shows three chapters earlier, when Saul is caught disobeying God. His excuse: Then Saul said to Samuel, “I have sinned. I violated the Lord’s command and your instructions. I was afraid of the men and so I gave in to them. 1 Samuel 15:24 And his request: Saul replied, “I have sinned. But please honor me before the elders of my people and before Israel; come back with me, so that I may worship the Lord your God.” 1 Samuel 15:30 A king asking the crowd for what only God could give him. The crowd was the only court he had left, and a crowd votes every day.

07 · Irritation

Small, and telling

Irritation is the most common of the five and the easiest to wave off. It is small. But small is not meaningless. It is where the need shows first, before it is big enough to name.

Scripture does not call the feeling itself sin. “In your anger do not sin”: Do not let the sun go down while you are still angry, and do not give the devil a foothold. Ephesians 4:26-27 The feeling is a signal. The foothold is what you let it build overnight.

So go slow with it: My dear brothers and sisters, take note of this: Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak and slow to become angry, because human anger does not produce the righteousness that God desires. James 1:19-20 And left alone, it grows. Hebrews calls it a bitter root that troubles many Hebrews 12:15.

A person’s wisdom yields patience; it is to one’s glory to overlook an offense.

Proverbs 19:11NIV

Overlooking an offense is only possible for someone who doesn’t need the apology. That is why Proverbs calls it glory. It costs something only a secure person can pay.

The most useful thing to do with irritation is to answer God’s question to Cain and Jonah honestly. Why are you angry? Not “because they were rude.” What did you expect, and what would it have meant if you’d gotten it? The tool in section 10 walks through exactly that.

08 · Already yours

He was not received, so you could be

Here is where the loop breaks. Not by needing less. By the need being met where people can’t reach it.

Jesus took refusal all the way down. His town tried to throw him off a cliff. His friends ran. His nation handed him over. Isaiah saw it centuries ahead: He was despised and rejected by mankind, a man of suffering, and familiar with pain. Like one from whom people hide their faces he was despised, and we held him in low esteem. Isaiah 53:3 On the cross he cried out the words of a man forsaken Mark 15:34.

He came to that which was his own, but his own did not receive him. Yet to all who did receive him, to those who believed in his name, he gave the right to become children of God—

John 1:11-12NIV

The rejection was carried, so the welcome could be given. Peter puts both halves in one line: As you come to him, the living Stone—rejected by humans but chosen by God and precious to him— 1 Peter 2:4 In the next breath he says the same of everyone who comes to him.

What is already said

The need has a short list of questions. Each one already has an answer.

  • Am I wanted?

    All those the Father gives me will come to me, and whoever comes to me I will never drive away. John 6:37

  • Am I enough?

    to the praise of his glorious grace, which he has freely given us in the One he loves. Ephesians 1:6 Older translations put it “accepted in the beloved.”

  • Am I still on trial?

    Who will bring any charge against those whom God has chosen? It is God who justifies. Who then is the one who condemns? No one. Christ Jesus who died—more than that, who was raised to life—is at the right hand of God and is also interceding for us. Romans 8:33-34

  • What if the people closest to me leave?

    Though my father and mother forsake me, the Lord will receive me. Psalm 27:10

  • What about what I still need?

    And my God will meet all your needs according to the riches of his glory in Christ Jesus. Philippians 4:19

Go back to the older brother one more time. The father did not argue with him or shame him. He told him what he owned: “everything I have is yours.” The demand ends when you believe the inventory.

And go back to James. “You do not have because you do not ask God.” Asking is the practical shape of all of this. When the need shows up as irritation, take it to the one who has already answered. If you, then, though you are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father in heaven give good gifts to those who ask him! Matthew 7:11

09 · Living from it

“So he got up”

Jesus knew that the Father had put all things under his power, and that he had come from God and was returning to God; so he got up from the meal, took off his outer clothing, and wrapped a towel around his waist. After that, he poured water into a basin and began to wash his disciples’ feet, drying them with the towel that was wrapped around him.

John 13:3-5NIV

John gives the reason before the towel. Jesus knew where he came from, where he was going, and what was in his hands. So he got up and washed feet, including the feet of the man who would sell him that night.

You can’t wash Judas’s feet while you still need something from Judas.

Security came first. Service came out of it. Received people become the ones who receive. We love because he first loved us. 1 John 4:19 The loop runs the other way.

Four moves when it fires

None of these work as techniques on their own. Each one is possible only for someone who isn’t demanding the thing anymore.

Ask

When you are reading a refusal into something unclear

Do what Phinehas did. Go and find out before you act. “If your brother or sister sins, go and point out their fault, just between the two of you. If they listen to you, you have won them over. Matthew 18:15

Overlook

When it was small, and you are secure enough to let it go

Not pretending. Choosing. It is “to one’s glory to overlook an offense” Proverbs 19:11, and “love keeps no record of wrongs” 1 Corinthians 13:5.

Entrust

When it was real, and you can’t fix it

When they hurled their insults at him, he did not retaliate; when he suffered, he made no threats. Instead, he entrusted himself to him who judges justly. 1 Peter 2:23 Hand the case to the Judge.

Receive

When someone is hard to accept

Give them what you were given, before they earn it. Accept one another, then, just as Christ accepted you, in order to bring praise to God. Romans 15:7
