Colour · Sound · Number

What colour
is middle C?

People have answered this confidently for three hundred years. They all disagree — and the reason they disagree turns out to be the most interesting thing about the question.

01 · The rainbow was tuned

Newton put seven colours in the spectrum because a scale has seven notes.

In his Optical Lectures of 1670–72, Newton described the spectrum as five colours. By the time he published Opticks in 1704, there were seven. He had inserted orange and indigo — and he put them at the positions corresponding to the semitones of a diatonic scale, spacing the bands to a Dorian interval pattern.

He was explicit about why. He was working in a Pythagorean tradition that held number and harmony to underlie the cosmos, and he drew the parallel to the seven known planets. It was an aesthetic and philosophical decision, not a measurement.

The rainbow every child learns has seven colours because of a musical analogy from 1704.

Hold onto that, because almost everything that follows — chakra colours, "the frequency of C", the whole architecture of seven-ness in wellness culture — inherits this one decision downstream.

02 · What the ear does

Your ear really is a spectrum analyser.

The basilar membrane inside the cochlea is a mechanical frequency analyser. A gradient of stiffness and mass means each position along it resonates at its own characteristic frequency — high notes at the base, low notes at the apex. This is tonotopy, and it is a genuine physical decomposition of sound into its component frequencies.

Roughly 3,500 inner hair cells sample that map, each feeding around ten of some thirty thousand auditory nerve fibres. Above a few hundred hertz, place coding is joined by phase locking — neurons firing in step with the waveform itself. The result is that humans can tell two pitches apart when they differ by about 0.2%.

This is why two notes played together are heard as two notes. The frequency information survives as frequency information, all the way up.

03 · What the eye does

Your eye is not. It has three numbers, and that's all.

Colour vision starts with three cone types — S, M and L, peaking near 440, 535 and 565 nanometres. They are not narrow channels. Their sensitivity curves are broad and overlap heavily; the L and M peaks sit only twenty or thirty nanometres apart.

Worse, a single cone cannot tell you anything about wavelength at all. Once a photon is absorbed, the cone's response is identical regardless of which photon it was. This is the principle of univariance, and it means the entire incoming spectrum — however complicated — gets collapsed into just three totals. Colour is what the brain computes by comparing them.

The consequence is metamerism

Because the spectrum is squeezed into three numbers, wildly different mixtures of light can produce identical triplets — and therefore look exactly the same. A single pure wavelength at 580 nm and a mixture of 540 and 620 nm can be perceptually indistinguishable.

This is not a curiosity. It is why screens and printing work at all: three primaries are enough to fake almost any colour, because your eye was never measuring the spectrum in the first place.

It also means colour does something sound never does. Play two notes together and you hear two notes. Mix two wavelengths and you see one new colour — one that may be identical to some entirely unrelated single wavelength.

And some colours have no wavelength whatsoever. Magenta is the clearest case: there is no such thing as magenta light. It only exists when short and long wavelengths arrive together with a gap in the middle, and the brain closes the spectrum into a loop that physics leaves open. Brown, pink and grey are similar — they need a context and a comparison to exist at all.
04 · Doing the arithmetic

You can transpose a note up into light. The arithmetic is clean. It just doesn't mean anything.

Here is the honest version of the calculation, which is worth doing because it is the foundation of every "frequency of C" chart on the internet. Double a pitch enough times and you eventually reach the frequency of visible light. From A = 440 Hz, it takes about 40 doublings.

Two free choices, and the answer moves

The scheme has two parameters that physics does not constrain: how many octaves you jump, and what tuning you start from. Switch the reference from A = 440 to A = 432 — a difference of under two percent — and the resulting hue shifts by about 11.5 nanometres. That is a visible change, produced entirely by a preference.

So it should not surprise you that the published schemes contradict each other outright. One popular system built on A = 432 makes C green. Scriabin's own synaesthetic table made C red. Newton's spectrum division gives a third answer again. Three internally consistent, mutually incompatible results — the signature of a convention rather than a discovery.

There is one more thing the table gives away. Run up a scale in the transposed columns and the colours do not run up a rainbow — they wrap. C lands in green, G in deep red, F over the edge into violet. That is not a spectrum being traversed; it is an octave being wrapped around a spectrum that has no octave in it.

And the ranges don't match at all

Human hearing runs from roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz. That is just under ten octaves — ten full doublings of frequency. Visible light runs from about 380 to 750 nanometres, which is 789 down to 400 terahertz. That is 0.98 octaves: less than a single doubling.

Ten octaves of hearing, squeezed into less than one of sight. Any mapping has to throw away almost everything.

There is one more thing colour cannot do. Pitch is periodic — a note and its octave genuinely sound like versions of the same thing, which is what makes octave equivalence meaningful. Colour has no such recurrence. There is no visual octave. Nothing in hue comes back around.

05 · The scale is not universal either

If frequency ratios were physics, the world's music would agree. It doesn't.

Everything so far has assumed the twelve-note scale as a starting point. It shouldn't. Twelve-tone equal temperament was described independently by Zhu Zaiyu in 1584 and Simon Stevin a year later — and then rejected for more than a century as sounding worse than the alternatives. Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier of 1722 is not written in it. It only became the fixed, standardised Western default in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The scale that feels like the natural order of things is roughly as old as recorded sound.

And it is one option among many that do not line up with each other.

Gamelan does not agree with itself

Javanese gamelan uses two tuning systems: slendro, five tones to the octave, and pelog, seven. Neither is equal-tempered. But the deeper point is structural: there is no culture-wide standard tuning. Each gamelan set is tuned individually by its maker, with its own interval pattern — its embat — and instruments from two different sets generally cannot be played together in tune.

Western tuning is a standard across a civilisation. Gamelan tuning is a standard across one orchestra.

The other systems don't fit the grid either

Turkish makam theory divides the octave into fifty-three unequal commas; Arabic theory conventionally describes twenty-four quarter-tones. Neither is really what performers play — both are idealised grids that regional practice diverges from by ear. And the blue note is not a fixed microtone at all. It is a continuously bent pitch, sliding between the major and minor third or seventh, shaped in the moment. Any chart giving you "the frequency of the blue note" has misunderstood what it is.

A caution about the twenty-two shrutis. Indian classical theory's division of the octave into 22 shrutis is often quoted as though it were a measured acoustic fact. It is a canonical theoretical construct from the Natya Shastra. When researchers actually measure performances, musicians do not reliably land on 22 discrete reproducible positions, and intonation for the "same" raga differs between performers. The framework genuinely shapes how the music is taught and discussed — it is not a physical scale sitting in the sound.

The one tradition that really did tie music to colour

There is a genuine historical colour-music tradition, and it is not Newton's. From the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, painters working under Rajput, Mewar, Deccan, Mughal and Pahari patronage produced raga-mala — "garland of ragas" — sets of paintings personifying each raga as a figure, bound to a mood, a season, and a time of day, with a dominant palette chosen to carry the raga's rasa, its emotional essence. The principle even has a name: ragavarnika.

What makes it different from every colour-organ scheme in this piece is that it never claimed to be physics. Nobody argued that Bhairava is a wavelength. The colour was understood as the correct emotional dress for the raga, conventional within a school and a period, and it varies across regions and centuries exactly as a convention should. It is the honest version of the thing Castel and Scriabin were reaching for.

So what does hold everywhere?

Something does — just not scales, and not frequencies. Two large studies are worth knowing. Savage and colleagues (2015) analysed 304 recordings across nine world regions and found no absolute universals at all — but strong statistical ones: discrete pitches, small pitch sets of seven or fewer, unequal divisions of the octave, small melodic intervals, and music tied to group coordination. Strong

Mehr, Singh and colleagues (2019) examined song across more than 300 societies and found music reliably appearing in four recurring contexts — dance, love, healing, and infant care — with acoustic features differing so predictably by context that listeners worldwide could identify which was which, even in music from cultures entirely unfamiliar to them. Strong

And a 2023 study of rhythm across 39 groups — Malian drummers, Bulgarian folk musicians, Bolivian villagers, Botswanan musicians, urban students — found every single group biased toward simple integer ratios in rhythm. But which ratios they preferred tracked their own musical culture. Moderate–strong

A universal scaffold, carrying culturally specific content. Which is precisely the shape of everything else in this piece.

06 · Sound made visible

Cymatics is real, and genuinely beautiful. The mysticism attached to it is not.

Sprinkle sand on a metal plate and vibrate it, and the grains flee the moving regions and settle along the lines that stay still. Those nodal lines form intricate symmetrical figures, different for every frequency. Ernst Chladni demonstrated this in the eighteenth century; Hans Jenny extended it with oscillators and photography in the 1960s and 70s and named the field cymatics.

The claim that these figures reveal sacred geometry, or that chanting a particular syllable produces a particular sacred diagram, does not follow. A Chladni figure is a nodal map fixed by the plate's shape, the material, and the frequency driving it. That it sometimes resembles a mandala tells you something about the limited repertoire of symmetries available in two dimensions, not about ancient acoustic knowledge.

The real phenomenon does not need the embellishment. Standing waves make invisible physics visible, on a kitchen table, for anyone who wants to look.

07 · The connections that hold

Something does connect sound, light and the body. It is stranger than the myths.

Having taken away the frequency mysticism, it would be dishonest to leave the impression that nothing links these things. Several connections are real, well evidenced, and considerably weirder than anything in the wellness literature.

Light reaches you through a channel that has nothing to do with seeing Strong

In 2002, two groups confirmed a class of retinal cells that are photosensitive in their own right — intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells, containing the pigment melanopsin, most sensitive around 480 nm. They respond to light even when rods and cones are silenced, and they contribute nothing to the image you see. Their job is to run the clock: they project to the suprachiasmatic nucleus, suppress melatonin, and set your circadian phase. Light in the early biological night delays that clock; light in the late night advances it. A fifth photoreceptor, discovered in living memory, doing something other than vision.

Music moves dopamine, and we know it causally Moderate

Salimpoor and colleagues showed in 2011 that intensely pleasurable music triggers endogenous dopamine release in the striatum, with a neat split — the caudate active during anticipation, the nucleus accumbens at the emotional peak. That was eight people, and it has never had a large direct replication. But a 2019 study went further and manipulated dopamine directly: levodopa increased musical pleasure, risperidone blunted it. That moves the claim from correlation to cause.

Rhythm can restore a walk Moderate–strong

The strongest clinical finding in this whole area is unglamorous: a steady auditory beat measurably improves gait in Parkinson's disease. Meta-analyses across many trials find better gait speed and stride length and fewer freezing episodes. The mechanism is plausible — entrainment of basal ganglia timing circuits. It is graded cautiously only because you cannot blind someone to whether they can hear a metronome.

Some brains genuinely do see sound Strong

Chromesthesia is real. For a minority of people, sounds reliably and involuntarily evoke colours, plausibly through cross-activation between neighbouring auditory and visual regions. But it is idiosyncratic: two synaesthetes rarely agree on what colour a note is. It is a fact about particular brains, not a law about notes.

And the rest of us have a weaker version. Cross-modal correspondences are one of the most replicated findings in psychology: without any synaesthesia, people reliably match higher pitches to lighter, smaller, higher things. That is shared abstract magnitude coding — not frequency arithmetic.

08 · A genealogy

Where the modern claims actually come from.

The "seven colours, seven notes, seven centres" system is usually presented as ancient wisdom. Its paper trail is short, recent, and traceable.

1704

Newton fixes the spectrum at seven

Inserts orange and indigo to match the diatonic scale, in service of a Pythagorean cosmology. Five colours become seven.

1927

Leadbeater colours the chakras

The Theosophist C. W. Leadbeater maps Newton's rainbow onto the Indian subtle body. In the actual Tantric sources, from the 8th–12th centuries, the number of centres varies — four, five, six, seven, twelve — and there is no fixed colour sequence.

1974

Puleo derives the "Solfeggio" frequencies

Joseph Puleo applies Pythagorean numerological reduction to numbers in the Book of Numbers and produces six frequencies. Leonard Horowitz adds three more and markets the set in Healing Codes for the Biological Apocalypse (1999).

1977

The package closes

Christopher Hills completes the synthesis: seven chakras, seven colours, seven notes, one system. Barely fifty years old, and presented as immemorial.

The Solfeggio names are borrowed, not inherited. Guido d'Arezzo really did invent solfège in the 11th century, drawing syllables from the hymn Ut queant laxis. But it was a sight-singing aid, and it carried no frequencies — standardised pitch did not exist yet. The hertz values were attached in the 1970s and backdated.

The 432 Hz claim, specifically

The most repeated argument is that 432 aligns with the Earth's Schumann resonance of about 7.83 Hz. Round that to 8, and 432 ÷ 8 = 54, a clean number. The trouble is immediate:

440 ÷ 8 = 55. The arithmetic works exactly as well for the number it is meant to discredit.

There is also no mechanism by which a 7.83 Hz electromagnetic cavity resonance between the Earth and the ionosphere would couple to audible pitch. And the popular history is wrong in a specific, checkable way: the story that Goebbels imposed A = 440 to make the world discordant is false. Germany had used 440 since a Stuttgart standard of 1834, a century earlier. The conspiracy version was popularised in the 1980s by Lyndon LaRouche's Schiller Institute and amplified later with Rockefeller embellishments.

Verdi did prefer something near 432 — for the practical comfort of singers, and he lost the argument in the 1880s. That is a real and rather human piece of history, and it does not need the mythology.

As for the claim that 528 Hz repairs DNA: there is no peer-reviewed evidence for it and no plausible biophysical mechanism by which audible sound would alter the structure of a DNA molecule. The one real finding in the vicinity is a small study showing lower salivary cortisol after half an hour of listening — a modest stress effect, which pleasant music of any tuning would be expected to produce. Weak

09 · The other map

Scripture is full of numbers. They are architecture, not measurement.

Biblical scholarship has a precise term for how number works in these texts: schematic. Numbers mark emphasis, closure and pattern. They are structural and rhetorical devices, not empirical counts — which is why the troop figures in Chronicles serve a rhetorical purpose rather than a demographic one.

This is a genuinely different thing from numerology. Numerology holds that numbers carry power independent of context. Schematic reading is the opposite: the meaning comes entirely from the context — from how a number functions inside a particular text's literary and covenantal logic.

Revelation's sevens are the clearest case. The seals, trumpets and bowls share a common shape — heavenly origin, an interlude between the sixth and seventh member, escalating intensity. That is a deliberate literary schema. Read as a countdown, it stops making sense; read as architecture, it works.

Where scholars will and won't go

There is one place where mainstream New Testament scholarship accepts deliberate number-coding by the author: Revelation 13:18. Transliterate "Nero Caesar" into Hebrew consonants and the values sum to 666; use the Latin spelling, dropping a letter, and you get 616 — which is an actual variant reading in the manuscripts. The convergence is elegant.

But it is contested, and honestly so. Irenaeus, writing around 180 CE and closely engaged with the text, does not list Nero among his candidates. The reading also assumes an audience fluent in Hebrew gematria for a Greek document written largely to Gentile churches. G. K. Beale prefers a symbolic reading: six falling short of seven, three times over. Best available solution, not a settled one.

The 153 fish of John 21 have no agreed explanation. Jerome thought Greek zoologists had catalogued 153 species of fish. Augustine read it as a triangular number resolving to law plus Spirit. Richard Bauckham proposed a gematria. Raymond Brown's verdict in the Anchor Bible commentary is the honest one: such readings are possible, but we have no evidence any of them was intended.

And one popular claim should be set aside cleanly. Golden-ratio geometry in Solomon's Temple is not a finding of biblical scholarship. It appears in fringe and self-published sources and nowhere in the peer-reviewed literature; like most golden-ratio claims, it depends on choosing your measurement points to suit. What is well supported is that the dimensions are schematic — cube-like proportions and repeated multiples, encoding graded holiness as you move inward: a cosmos in miniature. Golden ratio: unfounded Schematic reading: settled

Equidistant letter sequences — "Bible codes" — are not an open question either. The 1994 paper was rebutted in the same journal in 1999 by McKay, Bar-Natan, Bar-Hillel and Kalai, who showed the result depended on choices about which names and spellings to search rather than on anything in the text.

10 · What we don't have

The most musical book in the world, and we cannot hear a note of it.

Scripture is saturated with music. The kinnor and nevel, the tof, the shofar and the silver trumpets, cymbals. In 1 Chronicles, David organises the Levitical musicians — the families of Asaph, Heman and Jeduthun — into twenty-four courses, and the text describes their music as a form of prophesying.

And yet the sound is gone entirely.

The Psalm superscriptions carry technical terms — maskil, miktam, shiggaion — whose meanings are genuinely lost. They may be genres, or notations, or performance directions; nobody knows. Selah appears around seventy-one times and no one can tell you what it means. A pause, an interlude, a change in dynamics, a cue to the congregation — most translations simply leave it untranslated, because certainty is not available.

Even the cantillation marks are later than they look: the written system is medieval, from the Tiberian Masoretes of the ninth and tenth centuries. It very likely encodes a much older oral tradition, but what survives is a syntactic and accentual system — not a transcription. The melodies are unrecoverable.

We have the architecture and we have the words. The music itself did not survive.

There is something clarifying in that. A tradition this attentive to music did not think the frequencies were the point — or it would have found a way to write them down.

11 · The convergence

Two maps, and they say the same thing from opposite directions.

Follow the science far enough and you arrive somewhere specific. Colour is not in the light; it is a three-number inference your brain constructs, and it invents hues like magenta that the spectrum does not contain. Pitch is real spectral analysis, but even octave equivalence — the one relationship that feels like bedrock — turns out not to be universal. When researchers asked the Tsimané of Bolivia to sing melodies back, octave relationships were largely irrelevant to them, though their raw pitch discrimination matched everyone else's. The same group rated consonant and dissonant chords equally pleasant. The preference is learned; the hearing is not.

Even inside music theory, the perfect numbers fail. Stack twelve pure fifths and you should land seven octaves up. You overshoot, by 23.46 cents — the Pythagorean comma. Every piano you have ever heard is deliberately, audibly out of tune to hide it. The harmony of pure ratios does not close.

Now follow the scripture. Its numbers are schematic — structure, pattern, theology. Seven is completeness because of what a week is and what rest means, not because of a wavelength. Jubilee's seven sevens are about land and debt and freedom. The meaning is carried by proportion and relationship, and it is meant to be read, not measured.

Both maps arrive at the same place: the correspondences are real, and they live in structure and meaning rather than in physics.

Which is, in the end, a larger claim than the one it replaces — not a smaller one. "Everything vibrates at the same frequency" is a thin idea that happens to be false. That number, proportion and pattern genuinely organise both what we perceive and what we mean is a thicker idea, and it has the advantage of being true.

Newton was not a fraud. He was a Pythagorean who believed number bound the cosmos together, and he arranged his rainbow to show it. The instinct was sound. He was simply reading a structure of meaning, and calling it a measurement — which is exactly the confusion this whole subject keeps making, three hundred years on.

So: what colour is middle C? There isn't one. But the reason there isn't one tells you something true about how you are built — that you hear the world in ten octaves and see it in three numbers, and that meaning was never hiding in the frequencies to begin with.
