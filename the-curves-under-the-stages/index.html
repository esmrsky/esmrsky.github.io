The curves under the stages

The life stages you notice in other people are not containers. They are the visible surface of a handful of continuous gradients, all running at once on the same axis, peaking at different points and crossing each other on the way.

Stages are a reading artifact

Curve shapes directional, heights schematic

Drag through the axis. Nothing switches on or off anywhere. What changes is which gradients are high, which are moving fastest, and which two are furthest apart.

Two things follow from the shape of this. The first is that your stage labels are naming crossings. "Teenager" is where drive is near its ceiling and regulation has not caught up. The late-twenties self-improvement phase feels like a project because openness is at its peak while the maturity traits are climbing at their fastest rate — two curves moving at once, in the same person, in different directions.

The second is harder on the model, and there is a number for it. Across representative samples, chronological age accounts for roughly 2 to 6 per cent of the variance in Big Five traits, averaging about 3.6 per cent. The other 96 per cent is differences between people of the same age. Two randomly chosen 40-year-olds typically differ more in conscientiousness than the average 30-year-old differs from the average 60-year-old.

Age does better where the machinery is raw: it accounts for something in the range of 20 to 35 per cent of the variance in processing speed and novel problem-solving. So it is a fair index of how fast the hardware runs, and a poor one of almost everything you would actually want to predict about a person. These curves describe populations moving. Nothing on them licenses a prediction about the individual in front of you.

The teenage engine is a timing problem

Well replicated

The common reading is that adolescents misjudge risk. They mostly don't. What changes with context is how much a correctly-judged risk gets discounted.

Sensation-seeking is driven by a reward system that matures quickly after puberty. Self-regulation is governed by a control system that matures slowly and does not finish until the mid-twenties. Neither is broken. They are simply on different schedules, and the interval between them is what adolescence is made of. The same staggered maturation shows up in adolescent rodents and birds, which is a decent hint that it is doing something rather than failing at something.

One caveat on the model itself, since the chart above presents it cleanly. The strict dual-systems picture — two modular circuits maturing at different rates and competing for control — has been softened considerably since about 2015. Functional connectivity across the default mode, salience and frontoparietal networks develops continuously rather than as isolated systems racing each other. The cold/hot dissociation is solid. The two-boxes-fighting story underneath it is a simplification that its own authors have moved away from.

This is where the 25-year-old's frustration mislocates itself. He reads the behaviour as a values problem, something a good conversation could fix. It is a timing mismatch between two systems, and his own closed six years ago. He is not arguing with a philosophy. He is arguing with an interval he has already crossed and cannot feel any more.

Why 25 feels like the finish line

Large sample, replicated

At every age, people report large change over the past decade and predict small change for the next one. The prediction is wrong at every age, by roughly the same amount.

Everyone is standing at what feels like the finished version of themselves, looking back at a stretch of obvious change and forward at a stretch of apparent stability. The 25-year-old is not at a vantage point above the 15-year-old. He is at a point the 35-year-old will regard exactly the way he regards the 15-year-old, and he will find this as surprising then as the 15-year-old would find it now.

His earnest self-improvement is not evidence that he has escaped the pattern. It is a stage-typical behaviour with a peak and a decline of its own, sitting right where openness tops out.

And the same asymmetry runs backwards

The forward error has a mirror image. Adults across five preregistered studies reported that today's young people are in decline — and which decline they perceived depended on what the adult was personally good at. Pick an adult below and watch which bar moves.

The mechanism is a memory distortion, and it is unflattering. Adults project their current abilities backward onto their recollection of their own childhood and their childhood peers. Someone who reads seriously at 40 misremembers himself and his friends at 14 as readers. That fabricated baseline is what today's teenagers get measured against, and they lose to a version of the past that never happened.

Put the two together and the asymmetry you notice has a complete two-sided mechanism. Forward, the 25-year-old overestimates how finished he is. Backward, he misremembers what he was. The gap he perceives between himself and a 15-year-old is inflated at both ends, by two independent errors, neither of which he can feel.

The variable is the future, and there are three ways to lose it

Each route independently manipulable

A four-year-old grabbing the marshmallow and a seventy-year-old pruning their address book get filed as opposite problems — one a failure of control, one a sign of wisdom. They are the same computation coming out the same way for three different reasons.

Weighting a future reward against a present one requires three things to hold at once. You have to be able to picture the future vividly enough for it to compete with what is in the room. You have to believe the promise will actually be kept. And you have to expect to be there when it arrives. Knock out any one and the present wins.

Each route has been manipulated directly, which is what makes this more than a metaphor. Helping children build a vivid, specific mental simulation of a future event measurably reduces their preference for the immediate reward. Exposing preschoolers to an adult who breaks a promise, minutes before a marshmallow-style task, sharply shortens how long they wait. And shrinking or expanding a perceived horizon shifts adults of any age toward or away from present-weighting.

The middle one deserves its own sentence. In the reliability experiment, the children who grabbed the treat were not failing at self-control. They had just watched an adult break a promise, and they updated correctly. In an environment where "later" has historically meant "never," taking it now is the right answer.

Which means a substantial share of what gets scored as a trait — impatience, impulsivity, short-termism — is a reasonable inference about the environment, being read as a property of the person.

The three routes are not interchangeable for every outcome, though. They converge on present-weighting, but only the horizon input drives the other well-known shift — the move from expanding your world to deepening it.

Notice what the network does. Close ties barely move. The pruning happens almost entirely in the peripheral band — acquaintances, weak professional ties, people who were interesting rather than important. That is not withdrawal. It is a reallocation that follows directly from a shortened horizon, and young people with shortened horizons do it too.

This is the part with practical bite. Age is not a lever. All three inputs above are, and none of them is visible from outside. What you can see is the behaviour, which looks identical whichever input failed.

Culture sets the clock, not the shape

62 nations, ~884,000 people

Conscientiousness and agreeableness rise and neuroticism falls almost everywhere. What varies is when the rise starts, and it starts when adult responsibilities do.

So the answer to whether this is an American thing is: the direction is not, the timetable is. The long, loose, identity-exploring stretch from 18 to 29 that reads as a life stage in the United States is largely a product of extended schooling and delayed family formation. Where those conditions hold it is real and it is long. Where they don't, it compresses or disappears, and the curve simply starts earlier.

And capacity is not a scalar either

The same applies below the personality level, to the thing everyone treats as a fixed quantity a child either has or lacks. A preregistered study ran a marshmallow-style task with children in Japan and the United States, using two rewards: food, and a wrapped gift.

Neither group has more self-control. Each has a rehearsed waiting routine in one domain and not the other — waiting until everyone is served before eating, in Japan; waiting to unwrap, in the United States. Move the reward across the domain boundary and the apparent trait reverses. Which means the marshmallow test, in any given country, is partly measuring how often that country makes children wait for that particular thing.

The point extends further than one task. Work associated with Barbara Rogoff documents a pattern in a number of Indigenous and Indigenous-heritage communities of the Americas in which children are included in consequential family and community activity from young ages, observing closely and contributing according to developing ability, rather than being sorted into child-specific activities. Studies of Mexican-heritage children have found correspondingly sophisticated collaboration and sustained attentiveness in sibling pairs aged six to ten.

That is the uncomfortable version of the WEIRD caveat, and it is worth stating plainly because every chart on this page inherits it. The profile everyone recognises — children can't sustain attention, want entertainment, need adults to direct them — is measured almost entirely in societies that entertain children, direct their attention, separate work from play, and keep them out of anything consequential. Some of what reads as childhood is the niche, not the child.

Wellbeing does not have one curve

Components solid, the famous U contested

The midlife crisis argument stalled because both sides were averaging over three measures that move in three different directions. Separate them and the picture resolves.

Life satisfaction does not dip in midlife. It bottoms at sixteen, climbs steadily for fifty years, peaks around seventy, and then falls hard — a terminal decline driven by frailty, cognitive impairment and the loss of a social network, not by turning seventy-five.

Positive affect is the one that genuinely declines, and it declines the entire way, from age nine to age ninety-four, without a rebound anywhere. What is draining out is specifically the high-arousal band: excitement, exuberance, the thing that makes a fifteen-year-old sprint between activities. It is replaced by low-arousal contentment, which scores as wellbeing on a life-satisfaction item and feels like nothing much on a Tuesday.

Negative affect peaks around twenty-two, falls through adulthood to a low near sixty, and rises modestly after that. So the twenties are the high-negative-affect decade, not the forties.

Where did the famous U come from, then? From controlling for the wrong things. The trough appears when models hold income, employment, marriage and health constant — but those are consequences of ageing, not confounds to be removed from it. Controlling for health and marital status when you assess seventy-year-olds means evaluating a hypothetical person who reached seventy with a thirty-year-old's body and domestic arrangements. That inflates the late-life end and manufactures the dip.

When panel studies follow the same individuals for decades with fixed effects, the dip flattens or disappears. And in cohorts entering adulthood after 2012 the empirical nadir has moved from midlife to the late teens and early twenties, which is a different finding entirely and a more worrying one.

What children are actually better at

Robust in specific tasks, contested as a general claim

Your closing intuition has a defensible version, and it is narrower and more interesting than "be more playful." Children search their hypothesis space at a higher temperature than is locally optimal — and when the correct rule is improbable, that wins.

Drag the temperature down and you get efficiency: almost every sample lands somewhere plausible, and almost none land on the answer. Drag it up and the waste climbs, but the answer becomes reachable. Adult expertise is a compression. It is genuinely valuable and it genuinely discards live options, and which of those matters depends entirely on whether the answer was inside the compressed region.

Openness also peaks in the early twenties and declines for the remaining sixty years. That is a slow, real, measurable narrowing of the sampled region, and it is the strongest evidence for your instinct that something is being lost rather than merely traded.

There is a correction to make here to the obvious bound. The instinct is to say: borrow the curiosity, leave the recklessness. But those turn out to be dissociable in a more specific way. Romer's lifespan wisdom model separates impulsive risk-taking — acting without thinking — which declines steadily from childhood onward, from exploratory risk-taking, which is deliberate novelty pursuit and peaks in adolescence precisely because it builds real-world competence. Adolescent risk behaviour is not one undifferentiated failure of control. Most of what looks alarming from outside is the second kind.

So the bound is narrower than it first looks. The thing not worth borrowing is the impulsive component, which was already declining before adolescence and has nothing to recommend it. The exploratory component — high-temperature sampling, deliberately chosen, costs accepted — is the part that peaks in adolescence and the part an adult can actually reinstate. Borrow that.

Where this leaves you

The stage model has one durable use and one bad failure. It is a decent shorthand for populations. It is a terrible predictor of the person in front of you, and it is at its worst exactly where you noticed it — when someone uses their stage as a vantage point on someone else's.

Two variables do most of the work the stage model was doing, and unlike age, both are things a person can inspect in themselves:

How much time you feel you have. Not how much you have. The felt estimate is what drives the shift from expansion to depth, it is frequently wrong, and it is the one place where the research gives you a lever instead of a description.

How aggressively you prune before testing. Expertise is a prior, and a prior is a set of hypotheses you have stopped sampling. The cost is invisible from inside, because the discarded options never come up.

That is thinner than a theory of life stages. It is also load-bearing in a way the stage model isn't, and it has the useful property of pointing at yourself rather than at teenagers.

There is also a correction to make to the question that started this. "Depth" was defined throughout as reflective, verbal, disclosing conversation — the thing a 25-year-old wants from a teenager. What the lifespan research actually supports is increasing prioritisation of emotional significance, which is not the same object. Someone can be relationally deep without being verbally introspective: working alongside you, feeding you, telling the same story a sixth time, reliably being there. Doing something together while conversation happens sideways is not a lesser form of the verbal version. For a large share of people and cultures it is the primary form, and the verbal one is the local dialect.

One more thing worth holding onto, because it cuts against the direction of travel here. Complex emotion recognition and theory of mind do not peak until the mid-forties to mid-fifties. It is the one capacity on this whole page that peaks in late middle age. Whatever the 25-year-old thinks he is bringing to the conversation, reading the other person is not yet it.

The honest limit on all of this: nobody has actually studied the thing you noticed. Research on interpersonal communication splits into parent–child work and gerontological work, with essentially nothing on how twenty-somethings and teenagers talk to each other. The claim that one group wants reflective conversation while the other wants immediacy rests on practitioner commentary and qualitative observation, not controlled study.

Everything above is assembled from findings about the two cohorts separately and then pointed at the gap between them. That inference is reasonable. It is not a measurement, and it should not be treated as one.

What is measured and what is drawn

Sample sizes, effect-size ranges and variance figures quoted in the text are as reported in the source literature. Every chart is a schematic rendering. Curve shapes, directions and inflection points follow published findings; specific heights and values are illustrative, normalised for comparison, and do not come from a single dataset. Nothing on this page should be read off as a measurement.
