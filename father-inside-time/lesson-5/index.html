Relational, not transactional

Lessons 3 and 4 said God is in charge but not in control. Lesson 5 asks what kind of person is in charge. Its answer comes in five words: relational, personal, emotional, covenant-making, and pleased with his children even on their worst day.

I

A God who wants to be wanted

The lesson's first point is the simplest: God is relational and social. He talks, he follows people who run from him, and he responds in real time. Read the Bible from its first family to its last page and the lecture sees one storyline: "What does God get at the end of Revelation? He gets us."

"God doesn't need us, but he wants us."A student, and the lecture agreeing

The first question"Where are you?"

Adam and Eve hide in shame. God is the one looking for them. Genesis 3:9

The covenant line"My people"

"So you will be my people, and I will be your God." Language a husband or a father uses. Jeremiah 30:22

The motive"So loved"

The cross is not first a fix for a sin problem. "For God so loved the world that he gave his one and only Son." John 3:16

What he gets"With them"

"God's dwelling place is now among the people ... They will be his people." A bride, a feast, a family. Revelation 21:3

Need, or want?

A student had been taught that the Trinity was complete and God didn't need anyone. The lecture agreed: "Do I need kids? No. But I want them." God "is not served by human hands, as if he needed anything" (Acts 17:25). Love, though, has to have someone to love, which is why the lecture prizes the Trinity: three persons who were already loving each other before anyone else existed. It rejected modalism, one God wearing three masks, as "a massive error."

Where it strains

The other side says all of this too. No classical theologian denies that God called "Where are you?" or that the cross was love. The lecture says classical theology files this language under figures of speech. What it actually treats as figurative is narrower: whether we add anything to God or change him. That narrower question is the real dispute. Case 20

"Each has a soul." The lecture said the Father, the Son and the Spirit each have "a soul that's unique." The creeds say three persons sharing one being. Three souls leans toward three gods, which is further than the lecture means to go. Lesson 5 errors

II

One by one, not by formula

The second point: God is personal and responsive. He doesn't run people through a system. "He doesn't see all humans the same. We're not just a mound of ants." Two brothers bring two offerings, and God answers each one differently.

"Man looks at the outward appearance, God looks at the heart."The lecture, on 1 Samuel 16:7

The younger one, again and again · the class's own examples, with the pattern they noticed
  1. Genesis 4:4over CainAbel
  2. Genesis 21:12over IshmaelIsaac
  3. Genesis 25:23over EsauJacob
  4. Genesis 37:3eleventh sonJoseph
  5. Genesis 48:19over ManassehEphraim
  6. 1 Samuel 16:11the youngest of eightDavid

The firstborn had the rights. God keeps passing them by. The lecture's reading: he chooses people, not patterns.

The text it opened

Two offerings

Cain brought "some of the fruits of the soil." Abel brought "fat portions from some of the firstborn of his flock. The Lord looked with favor on Abel and his offering, but on Cain and his offering he did not look with favor" (Gen 4:4–5). The class offered reasons: the first and best, a lamb that points to Christ, the skins God made in Eden as a picture that sin costs a life. The lecture weighed them and left it open: "I don't think it matters that much. The point is God is dealing with people individually."

Then David. Jesse "had seven of his sons pass before Samuel" (1 Sam 16:10). The eighth was out with the sheep. "Send for him."

Where it strains

The Bible does answer the Cain question. "By faith Abel brought God a better offering than Cain did" (Heb 11:4). Cain "belonged to the evil one ... his own actions were evil and his brother's were righteous" (1 John 3:12). Both back the lecture's "heart, not formula." And before the murder, God reasons with Cain directly: "If you do what is right, will you not be accepted?" (Gen 4:7). Case 35

David's birth. The lecture said a "very common" rabbinic view makes David the son of a non-Israelite woman, a hidden child his father didn't count. The Jewish tradition it is reaching for says something else: his mother was Jesse's own wife, and David was only suspected of being illegitimate. Psalm 69:8, the verse usually cited, calls his brothers "my own mother's children." Case 36

Both sides use these stories. Jacob over Esau is the classical side's favorite election text (Romans 9). "One by one" doesn't separate the two views. What separates them is whether God's choice responds to the person.

III

A God whose feelings move

Third point: God is an emotional being, and his emotions change. Both sides say God has emotions. The lecture's line runs between a God whose feelings never vary and a God whose feelings respond, including to you.

"One out of eight billion people on the earth, and you can influence God's feelings."The lecture

As the lecture describes it

Feelings that never vary

God has emotions, but "change is impossible." So "he basically radiates the emotion of wrath all of the time at the exact same rate," and pleasure the same way. "No human could ever change what he feels."

The lecture's reading

Feelings that respond

"Slow to anger" only makes sense if anger can rise. "Do not grieve the Holy Spirit" only makes sense if he can go from not grieved to grieved. Pleasure, displeasure, sorrow, joy: the Bible says people cause them.

"The Lord, the Lord, the compassionate and gracious God, slow to anger, abounding in love and faithfulness."Exodus 34:6

The point
God's own description of himself, repeated through the Old Testament (Num 14:18; Ps 103:8; Jonah 4:2). Slow is a speed.

"And do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God, with whom you were sealed for the day of redemption."Ephesians 4:30

The point
A command, to believers. It assumes they can do it.

"He will take great delight in you; in his love he will no longer rebuke you, but will rejoice over you with singing."Zephaniah 3:17

The point
Joy with a before and after: rebuke, then singing.

"He looked around at them in anger and, deeply distressed at their stubborn hearts, said to the man, 'Stretch out your hand.'"Mark 3:5

Worth adding
The lecture didn't use it. Jesus feels anger and grief in one glance, at the same people, while he heals. That is the lecture's "both at once" answer, in the Gospels.
The question from the room

Eight billion feelings at once?

If each of us can grieve God or please him, how does he hold it all? The lecture pointed to people. Someone can bury a mother and welcome a newborn in the same week and feel both fully. A father feels one thing about one daughter and another about the other, and can still walk in the door with real joy. "I think God can infinitely more hold all that together." So no one needs to feel sorry for God: "I think he totally has it." But sharing his burden for the lost and the broken "gives him a lot of pleasure."

Where it strains

No classical writer says "radiates." The classical claim is impassibility: God is never overcome or acted upon by a feeling the way we are. Aquinas says love and joy are in God properly, and anger and sorrow by likeness to what they do. That is a real position to argue with. The fixed-rate wrath picture is not it. Case 20

The verses that say he doesn't change. "I the Lord do not change" (Mal 3:6); "who does not change like shifting shadows" (James 1:17). Lesson 2's answer still applies: they are about his character, not his moods. Case 21

IV

You become like the God you picture

Lesson 2 said your picture of God decides your next move. Lesson 5 goes further: it decides who you turn into. How you parent, how you treat the lost, how you feel about people in sins you find disgusting. "People become like the God that they imagine."

"Those who make them will be like them, and so will all who trust in them."Psalm 115:8, said of idols

The picture it drew

Two men watching a child play

One kneels down, laughs, throws the child in the air, and forms a bond. The other stays standing, serious and dignified, above the foolish play. "Which is more like God?" The class answered the first. The lecture's text was Mark 10: people brought children, "the disciples rebuked them," and Jesus "was indignant. He said to them, 'Let the little children come to me'" (vv. 13–14). "He rebuked the disciples, not the children."

The lecture tied this to worship. A church whose God feels nothing will tend to worship without feeling: "Their worship is not very expressive. Why? Because their god is not very expressive." Suppressing emotion is "not what God does."

Where it strains

Psalm 115:8 makes the point better than the lecture did. It is the Bible's own statement that worshipers take on the shape of what they worship. Paul gives the hopeful version: "being transformed into his image" (2 Cor 3:18).

Worship style follows culture as much as doctrine. Reformed churches in Korea and across Africa can be loud and tearful. The lecture itself credits culture: non-Western believers dance because "that culture hasn't influenced them yet." That is a second cause, not the same one.

Mark 10 has no long, hot sermon. The lecture pictured the children breaking up a five-hour meeting in the desert. The text says Jesus "took the children in his arms, placed his hands on them and blessed them" (v. 16). The playing is a fair guess. It isn't in the text.

V

Pleased with you, not with everything you do

This took most of the hour, and it came from the students. If God feels, is he disappointed in me? The lecture's answer splits the person from the behavior: God can be grieved by what you do and pleased with you at the same moment.

"Your actions can please God and displease God, and at the same time he can always be pleased with you."The lecture

With you

Delight that holds

  • "While he was still a long way off, his father saw him and was filled with compassion for him; he ran to his son" (Luke 15:20). Before the speech.
  • "This is my Son, whom I love; with him I am well pleased" (Matt 3:17). Before a single miracle.
  • "As a father has compassion on his children" (Ps 103:13).
  • "While we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Rom 5:8).
  • "Let us then approach God's throne of grace with confidence" (Heb 4:16).
With what you do

Displeasure that's real

  • "God was not pleased with most of them" (1 Cor 10:5). Said of his own people, rescued from Egypt.
  • "So we make it our goal to please him" (2 Cor 5:9). A goal can be missed.
  • "The Lord disciplines the one he loves" (Heb 12:6).
  • "Without faith it is impossible to please God" (Heb 11:6).
  • David's sin with Bathsheba: forgiven (2 Sam 12:13), and still costly.
The story it told

A spider in his daughter's room

A blood-curdling scream from a child's bedroom. He ran in. It was a spider. He doesn't mind spiders, but this one was scaring his girl, so he smashed it with everything he had. "I took all my wrath out on that spider." His daughter loved it, because his wrath was for the thing that frightened her, not for her.

That is how the lecture reads God's anger: "The wrath of God is revealed on the disobedience of men, not on men." Sin starts as a seed in someone made good, and if it is allowed to grow, it spreads through spirit, soul and body like an infection. God's wrath goes after the infection. The Bible speaks of "wickedness" in people God still calls his own, and of "the wicked," who have let it take everything. The flood, in the lecture's reading, was the second kind.

Even a sin that ends in death need not end in judgment. There is a first death, of the body, and "the second death," the lake of fire (Rev 20:14). Paul writes that believers are "disciplined so that we will not be finally condemned with the world" (1 Cor 11:32).

Where it strains

"Please" is usually about conduct. When the New Testament talks about pleasing God, it nearly always means what people do (2 Cor 5:9; 1 Cor 10:5; Col 1:10). The settled word for how God sees his children is love and delight (Zeph 3:17; Isa 62:4). "Always pleased with you" is better said "always delights in you." Case 32

"Not on men" doesn't survive John 3:36. Romans 1:18 does name "the godlessness and wickedness of people" as wrath's object, and 1:24 shows it as God "gave them over." But "God's wrath remains on them" (John 3:36) and "storing up wrath against yourself" (Rom 2:5) are about persons. The spider picture fits a child who wants rescue. It says nothing about one who holds on to the spider. Case 33

"Proud of you." A student pushed back on the word, and the lecture moved to "proud of who they are" as God's image. Scripture's word is "delight." It carries the same warmth without suggesting the person earned it.

VI

A covenant is a relationship, not a contract

Fourth point: God makes and keeps covenants. Both sides agree on that. The lecture's line is about what a covenant is. Marriage is its model: "I'm yours, you're mine," closer and more committed both ways.

"God's covenants are relational. They're not transactional."The lecture

As the lecture describes it

Settled in eternity past

All of God's decisions were made before the world began and "can't ever be altered." So Abraham bargaining over Sodom and Moses pleading at the golden calf "are not real influences," only stories told in human terms.

The lecture's reading

Worked out in partnership

God tells his friend what he is about to do. The friend argues back, and God moves with him. "Moses pleads with God and God changes his mind." The new covenant keeps the shape: "ask whatever you wish, and it will be done for you" (John 15:7).

God starts the conversation, and every request is granted. The city still falls. The answer to Abraham comes as a rescue he didn't ask for in so many words.

What it means for prayer

The lecture's sermon "Presence over Promise" walks the same pattern through Exodus 32 to 34, where "the Lord relented" (32:14) after Moses argued. Lessons 2 and 3 used that text. In both stories the covenant partner is invited into God's plans and gets real say in them.

Where it strains

Reformed theology is called "covenant theology." The Westminster Confession says God relates to people by "some voluntary condescension on God's part, which he hath been pleased to express by way of covenant" (7.1). The other side also calls covenants relational. The real dispute is narrower: whether the human partner changes God's side of it. Case 37

Relationships come with terms. Biblical covenants carry conditions and oaths (Deut 28; John 15:7 begins "If you remain in me"). The lecture itself calls the Mosaic covenant the one transactional covenant. "Relational, not transactional" is a choice the Bible doesn't force. Case 17

VII

Partnership, faith and favor

A student asked the hard one. If God is in charge and not in control, why does he heal here and not there? The lecture's answer is the sentence the whole course leans on. It ended in prayer, and in a phrase it told the class to "chew on for a month."

"God in his sovereignty has chosen to limit a lot of his activity on the earth to partnership with man."The lecture

Ezekiel 22:30

One in the gap

"I looked for someone ... to stand before me in the gap on behalf of the land so I would not have to destroy it, but I found no one."

Mark 6:5–6

Could not

"He could not do any miracles there, except lay his hands on a few sick people ... He was amazed at their lack of faith."

2 Peter 3:9

Not wanting

"Not wanting anyone to perish." People will. So God does not always get what he wants.

The lecture's evidence

Two camps

Churches that pray for the sick see healings. Pastors who leave it "up to God" report almost none. An intercessor woken at 2 a.m. as a storm hits a ship.

The phrase it gave them

"Faith begins where the will of God is known"

The prayer the lecture can't stand is "Lord, if it's your will, please heal him." Faith "isn't like I hope. Faith is I am fully convinced." And you can't be convinced of what you haven't heard: "Faith comes from hearing" (Rom 10:17). So ask first, hear, then pray without the hedge. The example it reached for is the leper: "If you are willing, you can make me clean." Jesus: "I am willing ... Be clean!" (Mark 1:40–41). A frightening diagnosis, it said, often brings fasting and prayer chains out of fear, not faith. Faith starts with asking, "Lord, what is this?"

Two kinds of favor

If God already loves you, what is favor? Jesus "grew ... in favor with God and man" (Luke 2:52). Some favor follows how you live: "God opposes the proud but shows favor to the humble" (1 Pet 5:5). Some follows the job God gave you: Joseph was favored even in prison (Gen 39:21) because a famine was coming and a nation needed him. Why not maximum favor for everyone? "Consequences are a gift of God to stop us from being dumb."

Where it strains

The leper's prayer was an "if" prayer, and Jesus answered it. Jesus also prayed one: "If it is possible ... Yet not as I will, but as you will" (Matt 26:39). Paul asked three times and was told no (2 Cor 12:8–9). "If it's your will" is a hedge when God has already said. When he hasn't, it is surrender, and Jesus prayed it. Case 34

Romans 10:17 is about the gospel. "The message is heard through the word about Christ." Using it for guidance on each request is an extension. It may be a fair one, but it isn't the verse's subject.

Two camps is a testimony, not a test. Churches that expect healing also count and tell more stories of it. Mark 6:5 is the stronger text, because it is Jesus himself who "could not." Case 14 Case 23

VIII

Before next week

The lecture's own assignment: find every emotion the Bible gives God, the extreme ones too. For each, decide whether he really feels it. If he does, ask what that does to your theology.

Sort the list three ways: what God feels about people (Zeph 3:17), what he feels about what they do (Ps 78:40), and what he feels about the damage it causes (Mark 3:5). See which column is longest.

Read Luke 15 as one story, both sons. The father runs to one and goes out to plead with the other (v. 28). Which of them is he pleased with?

Read Genesis 18:16 to 19:29 in one sitting. Note who opens the conversation, what Abraham asks, and what he gets.

Questions worth asking in class: "If God is always pleased with me, what does Paul mean by 'we make it our goal to please him'?" "Was Jesus praying lazily in Gethsemane?" "What does John 3:36 mean by 'God's wrath remains on them'?"

Next time, by the lecture's plan: what the Old Testament reveals about God, what the New Testament reveals, and how we see God in Jesus.
