A Father inside time

The lecture's claim is that most of us read God through lenses we never chose, and that some of them came from Athens rather than Jerusalem. This page lays out the lenses it wants to swap, the pictures behind each one, and the texts both sides lean on.

I

You are already wearing glasses

The lecture opens with tinted lenses. Put on a rose tint long enough and you stop seeing the tint; everything is just "how things look." Its claim is that Western Christians inherited a tint roughly 2,500 years old, and that the class exists to help you notice it and then decide for yourself what is biblical.

"How to think, not what to think."The lecture's stated goal. It also says: "I might be wrong on all this. Study it out."

The method the lecture asks for
1

Notice the assumption. "Where did you get your cosmology? Did you get it from the scriptures or did we inherit it?"

2

Go back to the text in its place in the story. Not a verse, the story the verse sits in.

3

Bring your counter-verses. "If you find scriptures that validate your view, bring them, and let's find out whether we read them with a predetermined assumption."

4

Don't throw it away on contact. "If you get challenged, think about it. Let's go back to scripture and do a good discovery."

Anchor text: Colossians 2:8. "See to it that no one takes you captive through hollow and deceptive philosophy, which depends on human tradition... rather than on Christ." In Christ "lives all the fullness of God in a human body." The lecture's hermeneutic in one line: if you've seen Jesus, you've seen the Father.

II

The swaps

Every lens the lecture wants to replace, side by side with what it puts there instead. The left side is what it says you may have inherited. The right side is its replacement, in its own words where possible. The small violet mark means the replacement is a live debate among serious believers, not a correction of an obvious error.

Filter by what the lens is about. A few of these the lecture only gestured at in Lesson 1 and promised to develop later; they are included because the lecture named them.

Inherited

God is first a judge, a king, a master. Father is one title among many.

Replacement

God is first a Father who "wanted to have kids and that's how he started creation." Judge, king, and master are true, but they are not the room you live in.

Eph 1:4-5 · Luke 15 · John 20:17Who God is
Inherited

A God who demands all worship is a God with an ego.

Replacement

God is a community before creation. The Father hands every ounce of praise to the Son and Spirit, and they hand it back. "God is love" is the only "God is" statement Scripture repeats, and love needs an other.

1 John 4:8, 16 · John 17:1-5, 24Who God is
Inherited

God is unmoved. He cannot be grieved, surprised, or affected by you.

Replacement

"Your thoughts and what you say actually matter and affect his emotions." God grieves, relents, delights. He is passible because he is a Father.

Gen 6:6 · Eph 4:30 · Zeph 3:17Hold looselyWho God is
Inherited

"God can do anything" means God can do the illogical, the arbitrary, the self-contradictory.

Replacement

God can do anything consistent with his nature, and he never violates it. "Can God make a rock too big to move?" is the wrong question. Wisdom and reason come from him; he stays within them freely.

Titus 1:2 · 2 Tim 2:13 · Heb 6:18Who God is
Inherited

Jesus arrived knowing everything, a fully formed deity in a crib.

Replacement

Jesus "came into limited knowledge and grew." He grew in wisdom and in favor with God. He walked out maturity progressively, as a real man, so that men could become children of God.

Luke 2:52 · Heb 5:8 · Phil 2:7Who God is
Inherited

Two worlds: a spiritual world (good, holy) and a natural world (fallen, suspect). God lives in the first.

Replacement

One creation with two overlapping realms, visible and invisible, in the same space. "You are in the spiritual realm and the natural realm at the same time." Matter is not the problem; the incarnation proves it.

Gen 1:31 · Col 1:16 · John 1:14Where God is
Inherited

Heaven always existed. It is God's uncreated home.

Replacement

"In the beginning God created the heavens." Thrones, dominions, the invisible: all created. Where God was before creation, the Bible does not say, and the lecture refuses to invent it.

Gen 1:1 · Col 1:16 · Neh 9:6Where God is
Inherited

God is far above. If you are good enough, maybe your prayer reaches him.

Replacement

God made creation and then moved in. He fills "every square millimeter" of it. "He can't be closer than he is to you." You are already seated in heavenly places.

Jer 23:24 · Eph 1:23, 2:6, 4:6 · Acts 17:27Where God is
Inherited

If God is everywhere equally, then nowhere is special, and "his presence came" means nothing.

Replacement

Omnipresence is not manifest presence. God is equally present everywhere and does not manifest in the same measure everywhere. The cloud, the fire, the man who wrestled Jacob: present differently, not present more.

Replacement

The dead "sleep." Time passes; they don't feel it. The grave has two chambers, rest and torment. The resurrection is "from the grave, not from heaven." RIP meant rest in peace because the alternative was real.

III

Story first, then topic

The lecture's method is "biblical theology presented in a systematic way." The complaint against systematic theology: it pulls every verse on a topic into one pot regardless of when and to whom it was said, and sometimes "that makes a big mess." The alternative: trace how God's revelation unfolds across the covenants, then ask what it means for people living after the cross.

Things the lecture says are in the Bible and are not God's heart: slavery, divorce on demand, animal sacrifice, the tattoo prohibition. Each one is "permitted" in the story and none is the design.

Four layers of context, every time

  1. The sentence. What the words say.
  2. The passage. What story or argument the sentence is inside.
  3. The book. Who wrote it, to whom, under which covenant, and why.
  4. The whole arc. "God as a father with humanity." Does the reading fit the story Scripture is telling from Genesis to Revelation?

The lecture's worked example: Genesis 2:25 ("naked and felt no shame") plus Job 1:21 has been used to found nudist Christian communities. Layer one permits it. Layers two through four do not.

CheckThe biblical-vs-systematic split is real and useful, but the lecture overstates it. The distinction itself was developed by Reformed scholars (Vos, Gaffin), and covenant theology is built on progressive revelation. "Ignores context" describes bad systematic theology, not the discipline.

IV

Where the tint came from

The lecture's history: Greek philosophers about 500 years before Christ proposed a God who is timeless, changeless, and far from matter. The church absorbed those ideas through the Roman world, and nearly every Western theology since, Catholic and Protestant, Calvinist and Arminian, is built on that foundation. Father-Son Theology tries to reach back behind it to a Hebraic picture.

"Classical" because it came out of classical antiquity. "Western" not as opposed to Eastern geography but as the tradition shaped by Greco-Roman thinking, which by now everyone has been touched by.

Swipe the diagram sideways to follow both lines.

Calvinist or Arminian? Neither, says the lecture

"God decided, or you decided. Those are the two options." The lecture says both answers live inside classical theology and share more with each other than either shares with Father-Son Theology, because both assume a timeless God with exhaustive foreknowledge and then argue about what that means for human will. Change the picture of God and time, and the Calvinist-Arminian question is reframed rather than answered.

Where it strains

Where an idea came from is not whether it is true

"Greek philosophers said it" tells you an idea's biography, not its truth value. The Greek schools also disagreed wildly (Stoic immanence, Epicurean indifference). The church fathers argued against Plato's view of matter; the creeds affirm good creation and bodily resurrection, which is the opposite of the dualism the lecture describes. And the immutability texts (Malachi 3:6, Numbers 23:19, James 1:17) are Hebrew and Christian, not Athenian. The Hellenization story has some truth in it and is doing more work than it can bear.

V

Two rooms God could meet you in

The lecture never says "courtroom" or "living room." Those are our labels for the contrast it keeps drawing: God as judge, king, and master, which it calls true, against God as father, which it calls primary. Every other move in the class follows from which room you think you are standing in.

The thesis text is Ephesians 1:4-5: chosen before the foundation of the world, predestined to adoption as sons and daughters, "according to the good pleasure of his will." The lecture reads that as a family plan that predates creation.

Why a family at all

"God is love" is the only "God is" statement Scripture repeats. The lecture's argument: love needs an object, God was love before creation, so God cannot be a solitary being. Father, Son, and Spirit are a community before anything exists. Creation is then an overflow of that community, not a remedy for loneliness. That is also why, in the lecture's view, God can ask for all worship without ego: each Person passes the glory to the others.

Where it strains

The two rooms are not rivals in the tradition

The courtroom lens the lecture argues against is real in some preaching, but the classical tradition never made judge the primary category. Augustine, Anselm, and the Reformers all read God as Father first; the Lord's Prayer settles the address. The sharper disagreement is not which room but whether the Father in the living room already knows how the evening ends. That is the time question, and it is where the real weight sits.

VI

One-sided or if/then

The lecture's test for which room a covenant belongs to is its shape. A one-sided covenant is a promise God makes and the human simply wears the sign. An if/then covenant has terms on both sides, and broken terms obligate the greater party to punish. Tap a covenant to see how the lecture sorts it, and whether the sorting holds.

The lecture's claim: only Moses is two-sided, God "didn't want" that covenant, and Israel wanted it because they already saw God as a judge. Jesus then lifts the curses and takes the punishment of the law onto himself.

VII

Every relationship is a rehearsal

The most original stretch of the lecture. Snails reproduce alone, so God did not need to build humans in families. The lecture reads the family as curriculum: each earthly bond you pass through is practice for a way Scripture says you will relate to God. The order is roughly the order of a life.

Note the shape of the argument. It runs from the heavenly relationship back to the earthly one: because we will be God's children, we are born as children. The earthly thing is the copy.

The payoff

The fifth rung is the hinge. The first four teach you how to receive a kind of love. Only the last one lets you feel the love from the giver's side, which is the side God is on. Once you have loved a child who gave you nothing, "he wants you to see what he feels towards you" stops being abstract.

And then: grow up

A student added the missing rung: a child is meant to become an heir. "God trusts Jesus with everything, with his authority, and God wants us to come into that level of maturity." Infancy is the entry, not the destination. Co-heir with Christ is. The next section follows this all the way through.Rom 8:17 · Gal 4:1-7

Where it strains

The ladder is a reading of Scripture's metaphors, not a teaching Scripture states. It also assumes marriage and children as the complete curriculum, which the lecture half-corrects with "unless you get really broken." The New Testament treats singleness as a full path (1 Cor 7, and Jesus himself). Read the rungs as five ways Scripture names the bond, not five stages everyone must climb.

VIII

Same heart, different approach

A father loves his two-year-old and his twenty-five-year-old the same. He does not treat them the same. One gets carried and told no. The other gets reasons, then his plans, then the keys. The love stayed put; the child moved. Scripture uses this picture more often than the lecture showed, and it is the cleanest way to hold "God does not change" and "God changed his approach" in one hand.

This section goes past Lesson 1. The lecture supplies the pieces: the ladder above, a student's "graduation," and "there are ways in which God doesn't change and ways in which he does." The texts here are the ones that join them.

The key text the lecture did not read

"The heir, as long as he is a child, is no different from a slave, though he is the owner of everything, but he is under guardians and managers until the date set by his father."Galatians 4:1-2 · then 4:7: "So you are no longer a slave, but a son, and if a son, then an heir through God."

Status
"Owner of everything." Fixed from the start. Nothing about the heir's position grows.
Treatment
"No different from a slave." Set by his age: guardians, managers, rules.
Timing
"The date set by his father." The father decides when the treatment changes.

Paul then reads all of history this way. The law was the guardian (3:24). "When the fullness of time had come" (4:4) was the date the Father set. In Paul's telling the Bible is a coming-of-age story with one Father from the first page to the last.

Does not change in any season

"The Lord, the Lord, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness."Exodus 34:6, God describing himself at Sinai

That sentence is quoted again across the Old Testament by people in very different seasons: Moses pleading after a rebellion, psalmists at worship, Levites after the exile, Joel calling for repentance, and Jonah complaining that it was true. The approach changed every time. The sentence did not.

Ex 34:6 · Num 14:18 · Neh 9:17 · Ps 86:15 · Ps 103:8 · Ps 145:8 · Joel 2:13 · Jonah 4:2

Same love, fresh each day: "his mercies never come to an end; they are new every morning" (Lam 3:22-23). And why the constancy matters: "I the Lord do not change; therefore you, O children of Jacob, are not consumed" (Mal 3:6).

Why it changes · 1

Where you are

A father adjusts to the child in front of him. "He knows our frame; he remembers that we are dust" (Ps 103:14). Jesus held back what his disciples could not carry yet: "I still have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them now" (John 16:12). Paul fed the Corinthians milk "for you were not ready" (1 Cor 3:2).

Why it changes · 2

Where the story is

Some changes come to the whole family at once. God spoke "at many times and in many ways" through prophets, and "in these last days" by a Son (Heb 1:1-2). "The times of ignorance God overlooked, but now he commands" (Acts 17:30). Sacrifices ended for everyone at the cross, not one believer at a time.

Why it changes · 3

What you do

He responds. "If that nation... turns from its evil, I will relent" (Jer 18:8). The father in Luke 15 runs the moment the son turns toward home (15:20). "Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you" (Jas 4:8). This is case 07 in the Cross-Examination, the part of it that holds.

The Son walked it first

Jesus went through the seasons himself. He was a child "submissive" to his parents (Luke 2:51). He "increased in wisdom and in stature" (2:52). He "learned obedience through what he suffered" (Heb 5:8). He called his disciples friends (John 15:15), then brothers (John 20:17). Then: "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me" (Matt 28:18).

The lecture put it this way: the Son of God became the son of man "so that the sons of men could become children of God." He is "the firstborn among many brothers" (Rom 8:29), the first one through each door.

When the approach changes, it can feel like he left

A child who stops being carried can feel dropped. Scripture marks these hand-offs. The manna stopped "the day after they ate of the produce of the land" (Josh 5:12). The psalmist describes a quieted soul "like a weaned child with its mother" (Ps 131:2): still with her, no longer nursing.

The approach changed because you grew. The heart did not move. If a season of God feeling distant follows a season of God feeling near, this is one of the first readings to try.

Where it strains
  • Status comes all at once. "To all who did receive him, he gave the right to become children of God" (John 1:12). The seasons describe how he handles you as you grow. A new believer is not a lesser child.
  • The seasons overlap, and they are not a timeline. Abraham was called a friend centuries before Sinai. Paul still called himself a servant (Rom 1:1), by choice, and Jesus took "the form of a servant" (Phil 2:7). Servant as a status ends. Serving as a posture stays.
  • The classical tradition agrees with the center of this. Calvin wrote that God "lisps" with us the way a nurse talks to a small child (Institutes 1.13.1). Both models of time below accept that God's approach adapts. They disagree about whether the change reaches God's inner life.
  • Paul calls the law a guardian (Gal 3:24). That is a gentler and better-sourced reading than the lecture's "God didn't want that covenant." A guardian is a good thing with an end date.

IX

One world, two realms, and a God who moved in

The lecture spends real time on cosmology because, it says, a wrong map of the universe produces a wrong map of prayer. If God lives in a separate good world far above a bad one, you are shouting across a gap. If there is one creation and he fills it, you are already in the room.

Why the lecture cares about the word "realm" over "world": "The idea of a separate, altogether world that's far away is dualism." Semantics, until it shapes how close you think God is.

Classical cosmology, as the lecture describes it

Two worlds, a gap between

  • Spirit is good, matter is suspect. The incarnation becomes a problem.
  • Heaven is uncreated and outside time; earth is created and inside it.
  • Holiness means distance from the physical.
  • The lecture traces this to Plato and names Gnosticism as one of its children.
Biblical cosmology, as the lecture reads it

One cube, two layers, fully occupied

  • "In the beginning God created the heavens." Heavens plural, thrones included. All of it is made (Gen 1:1, Col 1:16).
  • Two realms occupy one 3D space. "You are in the spiritual realm and the natural realm at the same time."
  • God made it and then "decided to live inside creation" and fill it (Jer 23:24, Eph 4:6).
  • So: "You're seated with Christ in heavenly places right now." That sentence only works in this picture.

Omnipresence: equally everywhere, always. Manifest presence: "you might start shaking."

Everywhere equally, not everywhere the same

"God is equally present in every location, but he doesn't manifest his presence the same way everywhere." The lecture's examples: the cloud of glory, the fire, the man who wrestled Jacob. A theophany is God appearing in some visible form. A christophany is specifically the pre-incarnate Son appearing: the lecture reads "the angel of the Lord" this way, because that figure accepts worship and works miracles, where ordinary angels refuse worship on the spot.

Guardrail the lecture adds: filling is not being. "Just because he fills the pen doesn't mean the pen is God." That is pantheism, and the Bible does not teach it.

CheckRejecting matter-bad dualism, affirming created heavens, and distinguishing omnipresence from manifest presence are all mainstream positions. The classical tradition agrees with nearly all of this section; its only distinct claim is that God himself, not heaven, is timeless. The "angel of the Lord as Christ" reading is old and common but not universal.

X

Does God stand outside the line, or walk it with you?

The lecture's hardest claim, and the one it admits fumbling every year. Two models of God and time. Switch between them; the diagram and the consequences change together.

Key terms as the lecture defines them. Timeless: exists outside time, not governed by it. Temporal: within time and governed by it. Not "temporary." A temporal God can still "transcend time, speed up time, restore fifty wasted years."

Every point is equally present to God; the line is complete.

God is timeless

  • God relates to every moment at once. Nothing is future to him.
  • He is immutable: no change of mind, will, or plan is possible, because change needs a before and an after.
  • Foreknowledge is exhaustive. Prophecy is description, not prediction.
  • "God regretted," "God relented" are anthropomorphisms: human-shaped language for a reality that does not change.
  • Prayer is answered from eternity; it does not alter God's state.

The lecture's charge: this came from Plato and Aristotle, roughly 500 years before Christ, and was carried into Christianity through Rome.

He knows everything that can be known. Some of the future is not yet a thing to know.

God is temporal

  • Time is "just a measurement of progression," not a thing to stand outside of.
  • God moves with creation. He can transcend time but is not exempt from it.
  • He knows everything that can be known: all possibilities, all hearts, all probabilities. Free choices not yet made are not yet knowable.
  • Prophecy works because "whatever God plans, he will do." He can announce the cross 5,000 years early because he intends it.
  • He can be grieved, surprised, change his will, and respond to prayer in a way that alters what happens.

The lecture resists the label, but a student named it correctly: this is open theism, the position of Boyd, Pinnock, and Sanders, with a serious literature behind it.

The lecture's core argument, in four moves, with the classical reply
1

Scripture says God regretted, relented, changed his mind. Hundreds of verses.

2

Change requires a before and an after. "Today the steak is good. In a week it is not. That requires time."

3

So a God who changes is a God in time. A timeless God "would say everything at once" and nothing would progress.

4

Therefore "God is outside time" is not biblical. It is philosophy, and it forces you to explain away every change text as anthropomorphism.

Reply to 1

Scripture also says God does not change (Mal 3:6, Num 23:19, Jas 1:17). Both sides have to treat one set of texts as the control.

Reply to 2

Agreed for creatures. The classical claim is that God's effects are ordered in time while his act is not, like a single decree that unfolds as history.

Reply to 3

"Say everything at once" confuses how God is with how God speaks. A timeless cause can produce a sequenced message.

Reply to 4

"Time is only a measurement" is itself metaphysics. Both positions are philosophy about the Bible; neither is the Bible alone.

Why the lecture says it matters

A student asked whether any of this is worth deciding. The answer: because the alternative is "lazy theology," holding "God can change his mind" and "God is unchanging" at once without noticing they conflict, and letting a hodgepodge quietly shape how you pray and how you grieve. "You will see the ripple effect of some of these views" across the rest of the semester.

What a temporal God can still do

The lecture is careful here. Temporal does not mean small. God can "fast forward, restore, redeem." He can "take fifty years of you wasting your life and say, it's not wasted anymore." He stops time on the earth when he chooses. He honors how he made things, the way he honors his own nature, and inside that he does whatever he wants.

Terms worth keeping straight
Immutable

Classical: nothing about God changes, including his mind and plans. Lecture: his character does not change; his plans and will can.

Anthropomorphism

Describing God in human terms (regret, grief, surprise). The classical tool for the change texts. The lecture calls it a way to "pretend none of that is true."

Omniscience

Classical: God knows all true propositions, past and future. Lecture: God knows everything knowable. Open free choices are not yet facts.

Dualism

Two worlds, spiritual (good) and physical (bad). The lecture's foil. Its own view: one creation, two overlapping realms, God filling every part.

Open theism

The future is partly settled (what God has planned) and partly open (free choices). God's knowledge is perfect about both, as settled and as open.

Progressive revelation

God's self-disclosure unfolds across covenants. Shared by nearly everyone; the dispute is how much of God's own nature it is allowed to re-describe.

XI

The texts both sides claim

Each one with the classical reading, the lecture's reading, and the pressure the text puts on one side or the other. Open the ones you want to sit with.

The one the lecture mentions but does not work is 1 Samuel 15. Start there. Both sides' key sentences are in the same chapter, eighteen verses apart.

"The Lord regretted that he had made Saul king." And eighteen verses later: "He is not a man, that he should have regret."1 Samuel 15:11, 29, 35
Classical

Verse 29 is the doctrinal statement; the regret language in 11 and 35 is phenomenological, how it looks from Samuel's side.

Lecture / FST

The regret is literal. Verse 29 means God will not reverse this decision about Saul, not that he never changes course.

Pressure

Both readings have to make one verse govern the other. This is the chapter where the question is actually decided, and the lecture skipped it. Ask about it.

"The Lord was sorry that he had made man on the earth, and he was grieved in his heart."Genesis 6:5-8
Classical

Anthropopathism: grief ascribed to God so that readers grasp the moral weight. God's judgment was always in the decree.

Lecture / FST

The lecture's main proof: "He did not expect it to get this bad. So I'm going to change the plan." God pivots to Noah because he needs a man to work through.

Pressure

Cuts against the lecture elsewhere. It said pre-Moses God "easily forgave" and barely confronted sin; its own proof text has a pre-Mosaic God drowning the world. Add Cain, Babel, Sodom.

"The Lord relented from the disaster he had spoken of bringing on his people."Exodus 32:9-14 · also Jonah 3:10, 2 Kings 20:1-6
Classical

God ordained both the threat and Moses' intercession; the "change" is in the unfolding, not in God. The threat was conditional from the start.

Lecture / FST

Promised for later weeks as the pattern: a man stands in the gap, and God does something different because he did. "What you did made God do something differently now."

Pressure

The strongest texts for the lecture's view of prayer. The classical reading works but asks you to treat a lot of plain narration as "how it looked." Jeremiah 18:7-10 states the responsiveness as a general policy.

"The Lamb slain from the foundation of the world."Revelation 13:8 (the lecture cites 10:9)
Classical

The plan of redemption was fixed before creation. Many read "from the foundation of the world" as modifying "written in the book of life" (compare 17:8).

Lecture / FST

"I'm already going to make a plan of redemption available before I even create them." A plan, not a pre-temporal event.

Pressure

Little. Both sides read this as plan, not event. The lecture's reading is the standard one and does not favor either model of time.

"There was silence in heaven for about half an hour."Revelation 8:1
Classical

Heaven is a created place; of course it has duration. The claim was never that heaven is timeless, only that God's own being is.

Lecture / FST

"That sounds like time." Used against the popular idea that heaven is outside time and the dead experience no passage of it.

Pressure

Lands on folk theology, not on classical theism. A good correction to "there's no time in heaven," but it does not reach God's own relation to time.

"With the Lord a day is like a thousand years, and a thousand years like a day."2 Peter 3:8 · Psalm 90:4
Classical

Often cited for timelessness. Careful classical readers agree it is about patience, not metaphysics.

Lecture / FST

Promised for later: in context it answers scoffers about the delay of the return. It says God is not slow, not that God is outside time.

Pressure

Little either way. The lecture is right that this verse is misused, and most scholars on both sides would agree.

"I am God, and there is none like me, declaring the end from the beginning."Isaiah 46:9-10 · Cyrus named in 44:28 · Peter's denials, Luke 22:34
Classical

Exhaustive foreknowledge, including of free human acts: a pagan king named generations early, a disciple's three denials before a specific rooster.

Lecture / FST

"Because he plans it." God knows the future he intends to bring about, and humans are "pretty predictable" to him.

Pressure

The hard case for the lecture. Planning covers what God does. Predicting specific free sins (Judas, Peter) needs more than probability. Open theists have answers; they are answers to a real problem.

"I the Lord do not change." "God is not a man, that he should lie, nor a son of man, that he should change his mind."Malachi 3:6 · Numbers 23:19 · James 1:17
Classical

Direct statements of immutability. The change texts must bend to these, not the reverse.

Lecture / FST

"What is he applying that to?" Malachi 3:6 is about covenant faithfulness to Jacob; Numbers 23 is about a blessing Balaam cannot reverse. Character, not plans.

Pressure

These are biblical texts, not Greek ones. The "it all came from Plato" story has to account for why Hebrew authors said this first. Context does help the lecture here, but the texts exist.

"Jesus grew in wisdom and stature, and in favor with God and man."Luke 2:52 · Mark 13:32 · Philippians 2:6-7
Classical

Growth belongs to Christ's human nature; the divine nature remained omniscient. Two natures, one person.

Lecture / FST

"He came into limited knowledge and grew." A real human development, by age twelve clear about his Father's business, before that not knowing everyone's thoughts.

Pressure

Little. Both sides affirm real human growth; they differ on how to describe the divine side during it. The lecture's emphasis is pastoral: he did it as a man so we could follow.

XII

What hangs on it

A student asked why any of this matters. The lecture's answer: because it ripples. Here are the three ripples it names, as each model would state them.

"If you get unveiled and your theology gets a little better, you're now not getting hurt at God. You're fighting the real enemy, who is not God."The lecture's motive, stated near the end

Prayer

OutsidePrayer is a means God ordained from eternity. It matters, but it does not change God. Along"What you did made God do something differently now." Your words affect his emotions and his next move.

Suffering

OutsideGod permitted it knowingly, for purposes you may never see. Trust rests on his character. AlongGod did not script it and may not have foreseen the specific act. "No matter what your spouse does to you, I've got you."

Guidance

OutsideGod directs with full knowledge of outcomes. A hard road he leads you down was still chosen. AlongGod guides from perfect knowledge of hearts and odds, not of outcomes. "A guide instead of a dictator." So yes, still ask him. "That is definitely a mishearing of what I'm saying" if you stop.

God looks for a person

"Why does God need a man? Didn't God already do it?" The lecture's answer is a partnership picture: the earth was handed to people, so God works on the earth through people who say yes. Noah, then Moses, then whoever is next. This is why, in the lecture's view, your yes is not decorative. It is how things happen here.

Ps 115:16 · Ezek 22:30 · 2 Chr 16:9 · Gen 6:8

Where it strains

The marriage argument does not escape its own problem

The lecture says a God with full foreknowledge who green-lights a destructive marriage is cruel. But it also says God knows hearts, wiring, and intentions, and that people are "pretty predictable" to him. A God who saw a high probability of harm and said go faces the same moral question. The comfort the lecture is reaching for actually rests on two other things it also teaches: that humans are free, and that God is present in the harm. Neither requires limiting what God knows. A student caught this in the room: "maybe he was warning and you just weren't listening."
