The lecturer asked for exactly this: "If you find scriptures that validate your view, bring them, and let's find out."

Pattern worth noticing before the detail: his cosmology, Trinity, atonement, and Christology claims hold. His history claims are overstated. His three time claims are open questions he presented as settled. His two claims about the Mosaic covenant and about suffering fail on his own evidence. The pastoral conclusion he wants survives all of this; the route he takes to it does not.

01

Biblical theology beats systematic theology

"Systematic theology does not consider progressive revelation. It just clumps all the scriptures together in a big pot. And sometimes that makes a big mess."His framing of the whole course

Case for

  • Heb 1:1-2God spoke "at many times and in many ways" through the prophets, and "in these last days" by a Son. Revelation is staged; the stages are not interchangeable.
  • Matt 19:8"Because of your hardness of heart Moses allowed you... but from the beginning it was not so." Jesus himself reads a Mosaic permission as a concession, not a revelation of God's heart.
  • Matt 5:21-48"You have heard it said... but I say to you." Six times. Earlier text, later fuller sense.
  • Heb 10:1, 8:13The law is "a shadow of the good things to come"; the first covenant is "obsolete." The author of Hebrews refuses to flatten the covenants.
  • Gal 3:19-25The law was "added because of transgressions, until the offspring should come," a guardian "until Christ." Time-bounded by design.
  • 1 Pet 1:10-12The prophets "searched and inquired" about what they wrote and did not fully grasp it. Later readers know more than earlier writers.
  • Lev 19:28 + 19:19, 27Tattoos, mixed fabrics, beard trims in one chapter. Nobody treats all three as binding, which proves everyone already reads by covenant and context.

Case against

  • Matt 22:37-40Jesus pulls Deut 6:5 and Lev 19:18 out of their contexts, sets them side by side, and says "on these two hang all the Law and the Prophets." That is a topical synthesis. Jesus does systematic theology.
  • Rom 3:10-18Paul strings Psalms 14, 5, 140, 10, 36 and Isaiah 59 into one chain to prove one doctrine. Topic first, texts gathered from everywhere.
  • Heb 1:5-13Seven Old Testament texts from four books assembled to establish the Son's superiority. A catena is systematic method.
  • Acts 15:13-21The Jerusalem council settles a doctrine (Gentile inclusion) by reasoning from Amos 9 plus the apostles' experience. Doctrine is decided by synthesis.
  • Lev 19:18 vs 19:28His own example cuts back. To keep "love your neighbor" (reaffirmed by Jesus) and drop the tattoo rule, you need a category like moral versus ceremonial law. That category is a systematic tool. Biblical theology cannot run without one.
  • 2 Tim 3:16"All Scripture is... profitable for teaching." Not "the later Scripture." Earlier covenants still teach, including about God.

Cross-examination

  1. The distinction is real and he is right to insist on it. Pulling Deuteronomy 28 curses onto a Christian is a category error, and a common one.
  2. But he describes a strawman. The term "biblical theology" was coined by Johann Gabler (1787) and developed by Geerhardus Vos at Princeton (1894), a Reformed systematician. Covenant theology, the system he opposes, is built on progressive revelation. "Ignores context" describes sloppy proof-texting, which every tradition does and every tradition condemns.
  3. His "story" is itself a system. "God as a father with humanity" is a thesis he brings to the text and reads every passage through. That is fine; everyone has one. But it means the choice is not story versus system. It is which system, and how openly you hold it.
  4. Progressive revelation has a limit he does not state. It tells you God's dealings and demands changed. It does not by itself tell you God's nature changed. Hebrews, his best source for progression, is also the book that says "Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever" (13:8). The author saw no tension.
  5. The Holocaust claim ("scripture verses by Christians pushed all that") is a partial truth stated as a whole one. Christian anti-Judaism was a real precondition (Chrysostom; Luther's 1543 tract, which the Nazis reprinted). Nazi ideology itself was racial and largely anti-Christian, and the Confessing Church resisted it with the same Bible. Scripture misused contributed. Scripture did not drive.
Overstated

The method is sound and he applies it well to divorce, slavery, and sacrifice. The contrast he draws is with a caricature, and his own examples require the systematic categories he says he is avoiding.Confidence: high. The historical facts are not disputed; the Jesus-and-Paul examples are plain.

Ask in class"When you keep Leviticus 19:18 and drop 19:28, what rule are you using? Is that rule biblical theology or systematic theology?"
02

Classical theology is Greek philosophy in disguise

"God is outside of time. Not anything you find in the Bible, but something that came out about 500 years before Jesus. Spiritual good, physical bad. That's dualism. We got that from Greek Roman philosophy."The Hellenization thesis, repeated through the lecture

Case for

  • Col 2:8"See to it that no one takes you captive by philosophy and empty deceit, according to human tradition." Paul names philosophy as a live threat to the Colossian church.
  • Acts 17:18-32Stoics and Epicureans mock the resurrection. The Greek mind found a bodily future absurd. That is exactly the matter-is-lesser instinct he is describing.
  • 1 Cor 1:22-23"Greeks seek wisdom... we preach Christ crucified." Paul sets the gospel against Greek expectations about what God could be.
  • HistoryPhilo of Alexandria read Moses through Plato. Justin, Clement, and Origen used Platonic categories openly. Augustine's account of time in Confessions XI is Neoplatonic in structure. Boethius defined eternity as "the whole, simultaneous, and perfect possession of unending life." Aquinas built on Aristotle. Influence is not in question.
  • Ex 3:14 (LXX)Hebrew ehyeh asher ehyeh ("I am who I am" or "I will be who I will be," a verb of becoming) was rendered in the Greek Septuagint as egō eimi ho ōn, "I am the Being One," a static ontological title. A Hellenizing shift inside the Bible's own translation history.

Case against

  • Mal 3:6; Num 23:19; 1 Sam 15:29; Jas 1:17"I the Lord do not change." "God is not a man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his mind." "No variation or shadow due to change." These are Hebrew and Jewish-Christian texts, centuries before or independent of Athens.
  • Ps 90:2, 4; 102:25-27"From everlasting to everlasting you are God." "A thousand years are as yesterday." "They will perish, but you remain... you are the same." Quoted of Christ in Heb 1:10-12.
  • Isa 57:15; 40:28; 46:9-10God "inhabits eternity," "does not faint or grow weary," declares "the end from the beginning." Isaiah is the Bible's own source for divine transcendence over time.
  • 2 Tim 1:9; Titus 1:2; Jude 25; 1 Cor 2:7Grace given "before the ages began" (pro chronōn aiōniōn); glory "before all time" (pro pantos tou aiōnos). The New Testament speaks of God's acts prior to time itself.
  • Gen 1:1; 1:14"In the beginning God created." The lights are made "for signs and for seasons, and for days and years." Scripture presents measured time as created along with the heavens. Augustine's "God made time" is an exegesis of this verse, not a Platonic import.
  • The Fathers vs. PlatoIrenaeus, Tertullian, and Athanasius fought the matter-is-evil view by name. Creation from nothing (not from eternal matter) and the resurrection of the flesh are the two most anti-Platonic doctrines in the creeds, and the church defined itself by both.

Cross-examination

  1. Genetic fallacy. Where an idea came from is not whether it is true. If Plato said God is changeless and Malachi said God is changeless, the question is what the texts mean, not who said it first.
  2. The dualism he attacks is Gnosticism, which classical theology condemned. He says so himself ("lots of heresies, including Gnosticism") without noticing that the condemners were the classical theologians. The creeds affirm good creation, incarnation, and bodily resurrection. The "matter bad" view he describes is the view the classical tradition exists to refute.
  3. "The Greeks" did not hold one view. Aristotle's god was outside time but time itself was eternal. Stoics placed god inside the cosmos as its fiery breath. Epicureans had gods who did nothing. "Greek philosophy says God is timeless" is one school, and it is also what Isaiah says.
  4. He does philosophy while disclaiming it. "Time is not a thing, it's a measurement." "God doesn't do things that are illogical." "Change requires time to pass." Every one of these is a metaphysical claim. They may be right. They are not exegesis.
  5. The LXX point is his best evidence and it cuts both ways. Jews translated Exodus 3:14 ontologically in the third century BC. Either Hellenization started inside Judaism before Christ, which complicates "go back to the Hebrew view," or the translators heard something in ehyeh that supports being-language. Revelation 1:4 then does something unexpected: it calls God "the one who is, and who was, and who is to come," temporal tenses, in a book written in Greek. Scripture uses both registers.
  6. Modern scholarship has pushed back hard. Paul Gavrilyuk (The Suffering of the Impassible God, 2004) shows the Fathers used impassibility as a guardrail against pagan gods with mood swings, while insisting God loves, is angered, and suffered in Christ. The "they just swallowed Plato" story does not survive reading them.
Overstated

Greek influence on Christian theology is real and worth naming. But the immutability and eternity texts are biblical, the church fought the dualism he describes, and his alternative is also a philosophy. The honest version: two philosophical readings of the same Bible, one older.Confidence: high on the history; the texts on both sides are not contested, only their interpretation.

Ask in class"Malachi 3:6 and James 1:17 were not written by Greeks. What do they mean, and how do we know that reading is not also a lens?"
03

One world, created heavens, God fills all

"God created one world with two realms, the visible and invisible, instead of two different worlds. The heavens were created. God fills all of creation. He can't be closer than he is to you."His biblical cosmology

Case for

  • Gen 1:1; Neh 9:6; Ps 33:6"God created the heavens and the earth." "You have made heaven, the heaven of heavens, with all their host." Heaven, plural, is made.
  • Col 1:16"By him all things were created, in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible." The invisible realm is creature, not co-eternal.
  • Gen 1:31; 1 Tim 4:4"God saw everything that he had made, and behold, it was very good." "Everything created by God is good." Matter is not the problem.
  • John 1:14; Col 2:9; Luke 24:39"The Word became flesh." "In him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily." A risen body with flesh and bones. The incarnation is the end of matter-is-evil.
  • Jer 23:23-24; 1 Kgs 8:27; Ps 139:7-10"Do I not fill heaven and earth?" "Heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you." "Where shall I flee from your presence?"
  • Acts 17:27-28; Eph 4:6; Col 1:17"He is not far from each one of us, for in him we live and move." "Over all and through all and in all." "In him all things hold together."
  • Eph 1:3, 2:6"Seated us with him in the heavenly places." Present tense, addressed to people standing in Ephesus. The realms overlap.
  • 2 Kgs 6:17; Dan 10:12-13; Luke 2:13Elisha's servant has his eyes opened and sees what was already there. Angels delayed over Persia. The invisible realm is here, not elsewhere.

Case against

  • Col 1:16 misreadHe says "God's throne didn't pre-exist. Thrones were created." The thronoi in Col 1:16 are a rank of angelic powers (parallel: "rule, authority, power, dominion" in Eph 1:21). Scripture says the opposite of God's own throne: "Your throne is established from of old" (Ps 93:2); "your throne, O God, is forever and ever" (Ps 45:6, Heb 1:8); "your throne endures to all generations" (Lam 5:19).
  • Isa 66:1; 1 Kgs 8:27; Acts 17:24"Heaven is my throne and the earth my footstool; what is the house you would build for me?" The tradition's point is not that God is far away but that God is not spatial at all. Asking "where did God live before creation" is a creaturely question; so is "when."
  • Zech 1:12; 3:1-6; Luke 1:11, 19His rule that "the angel of the Lord" is always Christ fails. In Zechariah 1 the angel of the Lord prays to the Lord of hosts. In Zechariah 3 the angel of the Lord and the Lord speak as two. In Luke 1 the angel of the Lord is Gabriel. The title is not a fixed christophany marker; some appearances function that way (Ex 3; Judg 13), others plainly do not.
  • Deut 4:24; 9:3; Heb 12:29Minor: he called "God is a consuming fire" a one-off. It appears three times.

Cross-examination

  1. Nearly all of this is mainstream and he teaches it well. Created heavens, good matter, one creation with an unseen dimension, omnipresence distinguished from manifest presence: Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin, and Wesley would sign every line. He presents a shared Christian cosmology as if it were a recovery against the tradition.
  2. The one classical claim he actually disputes is about God, not heaven. Classical theism never said heaven is timeless. It said God is. Revelation 8:1 ("silence for half an hour") is a good answer to folk piety and no answer to Boethius.
  3. His own logic undercuts his time argument. He grants that God is not confined to space, that "where was God before creation" has no answer, and that location is a created category. The classical theologian asks: why is time different? If God needs no "where," why must he have a "when"? He does not address this, and it is the strongest objection to his whole project.
  4. The throne error matters because he used it as evidence that "nothing pre-existed but God." The texts say God's throne is eternal in the sense that his rule is. A small misreading in service of a large claim.
Holds with repair

The cosmology is biblical and shared. Repair the Colossians 1:16 throne misreading, loosen the angel-of-the-Lord rule, and stop presenting it as a correction of classical theology, which agrees.Confidence: high. The texts are plain, and the Zechariah counterexamples are decisive.

Ask in class"If God doesn't need a 'where,' why does he need a 'when'?"
04

"God is love" requires the Trinity

"To be love, you can't be an individual being. If there's not an object of affection before the world exists, you can't be love. God didn't become love after creation."His argument from 1 John 4

Case for

  • 1 John 4:8, 16"God is love." Stated twice, as a definition, not a description of behavior.
  • John 17:24"You loved me before the foundation of the world." The Father's love for the Son is explicitly pre-creational. Here is the eternal object of affection.
  • John 17:5; 1:1-2"The glory that I had with you before the world existed." "The Word was with God." Relationship inside God before anything else is.
  • John 3:35; 5:20; 14:31; Matt 3:17Father loves Son, Son loves Father, the Spirit rests on the Son at the baptism. The Gospels narrate the inner life of God as mutual love.
  • Phil 2:6-11; John 17:1The Son empties himself and the Father exalts him; the Son glorifies the Father and the Father glorifies the Son. Glory passed around, not hoarded. This is his answer to "why isn't demanding worship egotistical," and it is the standard one.
  • TraditionRichard of St. Victor (12th c.) argued that perfect love requires a third who is co-loved; Augustine's De Trinitate works the lover-beloved-love structure. He is in good company.

Case against

  • John 4:24; 1 John 1:5; Deut 4:24Minor factual repair: he said there are "three primary" God-is statements. Scripture also says God is spirit, God is light, God is a consuming fire, God is faithful (1 Cor 1:9), God is one (Gal 3:20), God is holy (Lev 19:2), God is a righteous judge (Ps 7:11), God is greater than our heart (1 John 3:20). "God is love" is central but not alone.
  • Deut 6:4; Isa 44:6The argument proves God is not solitary. It does not by itself prove three rather than two or many. The Trinity rests on revelation (Matt 28:19; 2 Cor 13:14), with the love argument as support, not foundation.
  • LogicA classical theist could say God's love is first his own infinite self-knowledge and delight, and that this is one thing a simple God is. The "love needs another" premise assumes love is essentially other-directed, which is a theory of love, not a verse.

Cross-examination

  1. This is the best-constructed argument in the lecture. It is old, it is widely held, and John 17:24 gives it a direct textual anchor he did not cite but could have.
  2. It also quietly supports classical theology. If God's love is complete in the Trinity before creation, then creation adds nothing God lacked. That is the classical doctrine of aseity (God needs nothing, Acts 17:25), and it sits awkwardly with the later claim that God "needs a man." He cannot have both without qualification.
  3. The biology illustration (snails, male and female as image-bearing) is suggestive, not probative. Genesis 1:27 grounds the image in humanity as such, male and female, which supports his point that relationship is built in.
Holds

Sound argument, standard in the tradition, with a direct text in John 17:24. Fix the "three God-is statements" claim and notice that the argument implies a God who needs nothing from us.Confidence: high.

Ask in class"If God's love was complete before creation, what does 'God needs a man' mean?"
05

Only Moses was two-sided; wrath appears only there

"Before the Mosaic covenant, God's easily forgiving his kids, not even confronting their mistakes. Enter Mosaic covenant. Now you see wrath, punishment, curses. Only in that covenant. God didn't want that covenant."His account of the covenants

Case for

  • Gen 15:9-18God alone passes between the pieces while Abraham sleeps. The covenant is cut unilaterally. His clearest example, and it is a good one.
  • Gal 3:10-13, 19-25"All who rely on works of the law are under a curse." The law "was added because of transgressions," a guardian until Christ. Paul treats Sinai as provisional and curse-bearing.
  • 2 Cor 3:7-11"The ministry of death, carved in letters on stone... the ministry of condemnation." Paul's own name for the Mosaic administration.
  • Heb 8:7-13; 7:18-19; Jer 31:31-32"If that first covenant had been faultless..." "Weak and useless." The new covenant will be "not like the covenant that I made with their fathers."
  • Ezek 20:25"I gave them statutes that were not good." A startling verse that fits his reading of Sinai as something God did reluctantly.
  • Ex 20:18-21; Deut 5:23-29The people ask Moses to mediate because they fear God. Israel chose distance. "They are right in all they have spoken."
  • Rom 5:20; 7:7-13"The law came in to increase the trespass." The law exposed and provoked sin. Paul's experience of it was condemnation.
  • Deut 28; Lev 26The if/then shape is undeniable. Blessings for obedience, curses for disobedience, in long explicit lists. He is right that no other covenant reads like this.

Case against

  • Gen 3:14-19; 4:10-12Curse and expulsion in Eden. "You are cursed from the ground" to Cain. Divine curse is pre-Mosaic by two chapters.
  • Gen 6:5-7, 13; 7:21-23God drowns every living thing but eight people. His own proof text for God changing his mind is a pre-Mosaic act of wrath on a planetary scale. "Easily forgiving, not even confronting" does not describe Genesis 6.
  • Gen 11:7-9; 12:17; 19:24-25; 38:7, 10Babel scattered. Pharaoh plagued for taking Sarai. Sodom burned. Er and Onan killed. Judgment is a steady feature of Genesis.
  • Acts 5:5, 10; 12:23; 1 Cor 11:30; Rom 1:18; John 3:36; Heb 10:31; Rev 6:16Ananias and Sapphira fall dead. Herod is struck. Some Corinthians "have died" for abusing the table. "The wrath of God is revealed." "The wrath of God remains on him." Wrath is also post-Mosaic. It is not a covenant feature; it is a feature of holiness meeting evil.
  • Gen 17:9-14; 18:19; 22:16-18; 26:5Abraham's covenant acquires terms: "walk before me and be blameless"; the uncircumcised "shall be cut off"; "because you have done this... I will bless"; "because Abraham obeyed." Not purely one-sided.
  • 2 Sam 7:14; Ps 132:12; 1 Kgs 9:4-7David's son "will be disciplined." "If your sons keep my covenant... their sons shall sit on your throne." Conditions inside the Davidic promise.
  • John 15:6, 10; Col 1:23; Heb 3:14; 10:26-29; Rom 11:22"If anyone does not abide in me he is thrown away." "If indeed you continue in the faith." "If we hold our original confidence firm to the end." The new covenant has if/then language throughout.
  • Ex 19:4-6; Deut 7:7-8; Deut 4:7-8Sinai opens with God's initiative and tenderness: "I bore you on eagles' wings and brought you to myself... you shall be to me a kingdom of priests." "Because the Lord loved you." "What great nation has statutes so righteous?" Nothing here reads as reluctance.
  • Ps 19:7-11; 119; Rom 7:12; Rom 3:31; Matt 5:17-19"The law of the Lord is perfect." "The law is holy, and the commandment is holy and righteous and good." "Do we overthrow the law? By no means." "Not an iota will pass." Scripture never calls Sinai a mistake.
  • Ex 34:6-7; Lev 17:11; Deut 30:1-10The heart of Sinai is "merciful and gracious, slow to anger." The sacrificial system is itself a provision of mercy. Restoration after exile is written into the covenant. Grace is inside the if/then, not opposed to it.

Cross-examination

  1. The structural observation survives. Sinai is bilateral in form in a way the others are not. Hebrews and Galatians treat it as provisional. That much is sound and widely taught.
  2. Three of his four sentences fail on his own evidence. "Wrath only under Moses" is contradicted by Genesis 6, which he reads aloud in the same lecture. "Not even confronting their mistakes" is contradicted by Eden, Cain, the flood, Babel, Sodom. "Only Moses two-sided" is contradicted by Genesis 17 and by the new covenant's own warnings.
  3. "God didn't want that covenant" has no text. Ezekiel 20:25 is the nearest, and it refers to later statutes given in judgment after Israel's rebellion in the wilderness (vv. 21-26), not to Sinai. Exodus 19:4-6 presents Sinai as a wedding proposal. Paul calls the giving of the law a privilege (Rom 9:4). Jesus calls the law good.
  4. He confuses two things: the law as a covenant administration (provisional, Hebrews 8) and the law as revelation of God's character (permanent, Matthew 5). The first ended. The second did not, which is why Jesus could say "Moses said... but I say" while also saying "not an iota will pass."
  5. Why this matters for his project: he needs the pre-Mosaic God to be the gentle Father so that the Mosaic wrath can be an aberration. Genesis will not cooperate. The Father in the living room also drowned the world. Any honest Father-theology has to hold both.
Fails as stated

A true kernel (Sinai is uniquely bilateral and provisional) wrapped in three claims that Genesis, the New Testament, and his own proof text contradict. The replacement should be: God's holiness confronts evil in every covenant; Sinai codified the terms; Christ bore them.Confidence: high. This is the clearest failure in the lecture, and the counter-texts are not obscure.

Ask in class"Genesis 6 is pre-Mosaic and God wipes out the world. How does that fit 'before Moses, God barely confronted sin'?"
06

God is in time, not outside it

"There is no such thing as outside of time, because time is not a thing. It's a measurement. If God says a sentence outside of time, you hear everything at the exact same time. God is temporal, not temporary."The claim he admits fumbling every year

Case for

  • Ex 3:14ehyeh asher ehyeh. The verb is imperfect and can be rendered "I will be who I will be" (as in 3:12, "I will be with you"). A name of ongoing presence, not static being.
  • Rev 1:4, 8; 4:8"The one who is and who was and who is to come." Three tenses applied to God by John. If God's own book speaks of him with a past and a future, timelessness is not the plain sense.
  • John 1:14; Gal 4:4"The Word became flesh." "When the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son." God enters history and acts at a date. An incarnate God has a before and after.
  • Gen 6:6; Ex 32:14; Jer 18:7-10The relenting texts (case 07). If even one is literal, God has a before and after.
  • Rev 8:1; Rev 6:10-11"Silence in heaven for about half an hour." Martyrs cry "how long?" and are told to "rest a little longer." Heaven, the created place, has duration.
  • Heb 1:1-2; Acts 17:30-31God "spoke... and in these last days has spoken." He "overlooked" past ignorance and "now commands." Sequence in God's own dealings.
  • PhilosophyWolterstorff ("God Everlasting," 1975), Swinburne, Craig, and the open theists all hold God is temporal at least since creation. A timeless being, they argue, cannot know what time it is now, cannot respond, cannot be the agent Scripture describes. This is a serious minority position among Christian philosophers, not a fringe one.

Case against

  • Gen 1:1, 14"In the beginning." Lights "for seasons, days, and years." Measured time begins with the heavens. If time is created, its Creator is not inside it by nature.
  • 2 Tim 1:9; Titus 1:2; Jude 25; 1 Cor 2:7; John 17:5, 24"Before the ages." "Before all time." "Before the world existed." Scripture speaks of God's purposes, glory, and love prior to time.
  • Ps 90:2, 4; 2 Pet 3:8"From everlasting to everlasting you are God." "A thousand years as one day." The lecturer is right that 2 Peter 3:8 is about patience, but it works as an argument for patience only if God's relation to duration is unlike ours.
  • Ps 102:25-27; Heb 1:10-12; 13:8Creation wears out "like a garment"; "you are the same, and your years have no end." Jesus Christ "the same yesterday and today and forever."
  • Isa 57:15; 40:28; 46:10; 41:4God "inhabits eternity," declares "the end from the beginning," is "the first and the last." Isaiah's whole polemic against idols rests on a God who stands over the sequence of history, not in it.
  • Ex 3:14 (again)The same verb is rendered "I AM" and Jesus takes "I am" as his own title in John 8:58 ("before Abraham was, I am"), a present tense set against a past, which is the grammar of timelessness.
  • Rev 1:8; 22:13"Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the end." A student raised this and the lecturer said it was "100% concrete" and consistent with his view. It is consistent with his view only if "beginning and end" means "first in sequence," which is not the natural sense of a title for God.

Cross-examination

  1. "Time is just a measurement" is doing all the work and it is a philosophical claim. Whether time is a dimension, a relation, a measurement, or an illusion is exactly what philosophers of time argue about. The Bible does not settle it. If he is going to disallow classical theology for importing metaphysics, he cannot import his own.
  2. "You'd hear everything at once" confuses God's being with God's speaking. A cause that is not in time can produce effects that are. The classical picture is a single eternal act that appears in history as sequence, like one decree that unfolds as a story. He may find this unsatisfying; he has not shown it incoherent.
  3. Revelation 8:1 proves heaven has time. Nobody denied that. The classical claim is about God, not about heaven. He hit a target the other side was not defending.
  4. His strongest card is the incarnation, and he barely plays it. If God the Son has a body "permanently" (he says this), and a body is in time, then at least one Person of the Trinity is temporal since Bethlehem. The classical answer (two natures; the divine nature remains timeless) is coherent but costly. This is where the debate actually lives.
  5. The middle position he never mentions: William Lane Craig's view that God is timeless without creation and temporal since creation. It takes "before the ages" seriously and takes Genesis 6 seriously. It is almost what the lecturer says when he admits "where God was before creation, the Bible doesn't say," but he does not follow it through.

Open question

Both readings are philosophical frameworks laid over texts that speak in both registers. He presented a live debate as a recovered truth. His position is defensible and has serious defenders; so does the one he dismissed.Confidence: high that it is open; low that either side can close it from Scripture alone.

God changes his mind, plans, and will

"The Bible reveals to us God changed his plans. God changed his will. God changed his mind. God repented. How do we deal with hundreds of verses like that? Systematic theology has to pretend none of that's true."The immutability dispute

Case for

  • Word studyHebrew nacham (niphal), "to relent, regret, be moved to pity," is used of God roughly three dozen times: Gen 6:6-7; Ex 32:12, 14; Judg 2:18; 1 Sam 15:11, 35; 2 Sam 24:16; Ps 106:45; Jer 18:8, 10; 26:3, 13, 19; 42:10; Joel 2:13-14; Amos 7:3, 6; Jonah 3:9-10; 4:2. Not "hundreds," but far too many to be a slip.
  • Jer 18:7-10The policy statement. "If that nation turns from its evil, I will relent of the disaster I intended. And if it does evil, I will relent of the good I had intended." God announces responsiveness as his standing rule.
  • Ezek 33:13-16; Jer 26:3, 13Same policy restated. Threats and promises are explicitly conditional on response.
  • Ex 32:9-14"Now leave me alone, that my wrath may burn." Moses intercedes. "The Lord relented from the disaster." The narrative gives Moses causal weight.
  • Jonah 3:10; 4:2"God saw what they did... God relented." Jonah complains that this is exactly who God is: "I knew you were a God who relents." Relenting is treated as a divine attribute.
  • Hos 11:8-9; Judg 10:16; Isa 63:9-10"My heart recoils within me." "He could bear Israel's misery no longer." "They grieved his Holy Spirit." Emotional movement in response to people.
  • Heb 6:17-18; Ps 110:4God "guaranteed it with an oath" to show "the unchangeable character of his purpose." Why swear an oath if every word is already unchangeable? The oath marks the irrevocable things, implying ordinary announcements can be conditional. This text helps him more than any he cited.

Case against

  • Num 23:19; 1 Sam 15:29"God is not a man that he should nacham." The same verb, negated, in a general statement about God's nature. In 1 Samuel 15 the negation sits eighteen verses after two affirmations.
  • Mal 3:6; Jas 1:17"I the Lord do not change." "With whom there is no variation or shadow due to change." Direct and unqualified.
  • Ps 33:11; Prov 19:21; Isa 14:24, 27; 46:10-11"The counsel of the Lord stands forever." "The Lord of hosts has purposed, and who will annul it?" "My counsel shall stand, and I will accomplish all my purpose." God's plans are presented as unthwartable.
  • Rom 11:29; 2 Tim 2:13"The gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable." "If we are faithless, he remains faithful." Constancy as the ground of hope.
  • Jer 4:28; Ezek 24:14; Zech 8:14"I have not relented, nor will I turn back." "I will not go back; I will not spare; I will not relent." God also announces non-relenting as policy. Both are rhetorical stances inside a relationship.
  • Jonah 3:4 vs 3:10"Forty days and Nineveh shall be overthrown." No condition stated, yet the condition was real. Which means announced outcomes are not always what God has settled, which is the classical reading of all the relenting texts: the threat was conditional from the start, so nothing in God changed.

Cross-examination

  1. Both sides must make one set of texts govern the other. He makes Numbers 23:19 mean "will not break this oath." The classical reader makes Genesis 6:6 mean "appears to regret." Neither is reading "what's there." Both are interpreting, which he said everyone does.
  2. His own resolution is the classical one. "There are ways God doesn't change and ways he does": character constant, plans responsive. That is Aquinas's distinction between God's nature and God's relations to creatures. Calvin said the relenting texts describe God "accommodating himself to our capacity" while his counsel stands. The disagreement is narrower than "they pretend none of it is true."
  3. Where the real disagreement sits: was the condition real to God? Jeremiah 18 says God relents when people turn. Classical: God eternally knew they would turn and eternally willed to relent; the sequence is in history, not in God. Open: then the relenting is theater. Classical: no, the condition genuinely operates in time; God's knowledge of it is simply not in time. This is case 06 again. Change follows time.
  4. Hebrews 6:17 is his best unused weapon. An oath marks the "unchangeable" things. Scripture itself distinguishes sworn purposes (the Abrahamic promise, the Davidic throne, the priesthood of Ps 110:4) from announced intentions (Nineveh, Hezekiah's death, Israel at Sinai). Numbers 23:19 is a Balaam oracle about a sworn blessing. 1 Samuel 15:29 is about a sworn removal of a king. Read that way, the negations are about oaths, and the affirmations are about intentions, and there is no contradiction. This is a strong reading and he gestured at it without working it.
  5. What "hundreds of verses" overstates: the count is dozens, concentrated in narrative and prophecy, almost always about judgment averted. There is no text where God changes his mind about his character, his covenant with Abraham, or the cross.
Holds with repair

God's announced intentions change in response to people. That is written as policy in Jeremiah 18 and shown dozens of times. The repair: his resolution (nature fixed, plans responsive) is the classical one, and Hebrews 6:17 gives him a textual way to reconcile the negations he did not use.Confidence: high on the pattern; moderate on how far "will" changes, since Scripture also insists his counsel stands.

God knows everything knowable; the future is partly open

"God knows everything that can be known. But there are things that don't exist yet. He knows all the possibilities. Man is pretty predictable to God. But sometimes, as wise as he is, he's like, I couldn't predict that. I'm shocked and I'm changing my mind."Open theism, unnamed

Case for

  • Gen 22:12"Now I know that you fear God." After the test, not before. The plain sense is that God learned something.
  • Deut 8:2; 13:3; 2 Chr 32:31"To test you, to know what was in your heart." God "left Hezekiah to himself, in order to test him and to know all that was in his heart." Tests described as God's means of knowing.
  • Gen 6:6; Isa 5:4; Jer 3:7, 19-20"I thought, 'After she has done all this she will return to me,' but she did not return." "What more was there to do for my vineyard?" Divine expectation disappointed, in God's own voice.
  • Jer 32:35; 7:31; 19:5"Nor did it enter my mind that they should do this abomination." If taken at face value, God did not foresee the specific sin.
  • Ezek 12:3; Jer 26:3; 36:3, 7"Perhaps they will understand." "It may be they will listen." God speaks in "perhaps" about human response.
  • Matt 23:37; Luke 7:30; Acts 7:51; Luke 13:34"How often would I have gathered your children, and you were not willing." "Rejected the purpose of God for themselves." "You always resist the Holy Spirit." God's will for people is thwarted by people.
  • 1 Tim 2:4; 2 Pet 3:9; Ezek 18:23, 32God "desires all people to be saved," "not wishing that any should perish," has "no pleasure in the death of the wicked." Yet some perish. If outcomes were fixed by God's knowledge and will, these desires would be odd.
  • LiteraturePinnock et al., The Openness of God (1994); Sanders, The God Who Risks (1998); Boyd, God of the Possible (2000). A developed position with answers to the hard texts below.

Case against

  • Isa 41:21-26; 44:6-8; 46:9-11God's challenge to the idols: "Tell us what is to come." "Who told this long ago?" "Declaring the end from the beginning." Isaiah makes knowledge of the future the test of deity. A God who knows only probabilities fails Isaiah's own test.
  • Isa 44:28-45:1; 1 Kgs 13:2Cyrus named about 150 years early. Josiah named about 300 years early. Specific free human agents, by name, generations before birth.
  • Matt 26:34; John 13:19, 38"Before the rooster crows, you will deny me three times." A specific sin, a specific count, a specific deadline, by a free agent who swore he would not. "I am telling you this now, before it takes place, so that when it does take place you may believe."
  • John 6:64, 70-71; 13:11; Acts 1:16"Jesus knew from the beginning... who it was who would betray him." Judas's free act, known in advance and called the fulfillment of Scripture (Ps 41:9).
  • Acts 2:23; 4:27-28The cross happened "according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God." Herod, Pilate, Gentiles, and Israel did "whatever your hand and your plan had predestined." The most evil free act in history is described as foreknown and planned in detail.
  • Ex 3:19; Deut 31:16-21"I know that the king of Egypt will not let you go." "I know what they are inclined to do even today... they will turn to other gods." God predicts specific future rebellion flatly.
  • Matt 11:21-23; 1 Sam 23:11-12"If the mighty works done in you had been done in Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented." "Will Saul come down? He will." God knows what free agents would do in situations that never occur. That is more than knowledge of possibilities.
  • Ps 139:4; Ps 147:5; 1 John 3:20; Heb 4:13"Before a word is on my tongue, you know it altogether." "His understanding is beyond measure." "He knows everything." "Nothing is hidden."
  • Dan 2; 7; 11Successive empires, battles, and rulers described in advance in detail. Whatever one's dating of Daniel, the text presents God as knowing the free political future.

Cross-examination

  1. His omniscience is redefined, not denied, and he should say so. "Knows everything knowable" is the open theist formula. It is a coherent position. It is not what the word has meant for two thousand years, and students deserve to know they are being taught a minority view with a name.
  2. "Because he plans it" covers Cyrus. It does not cover Peter. God can ensure Cyrus conquers Babylon. Can God ensure Peter freely denies him three times before a rooster, with no coercion? If yes, then God can guarantee free acts, and the "free acts are unknowable" premise collapses. If no, then Jesus made a lucky guess with a specific count. Open theists answer that Peter's character made it near-certain and God arranged the circumstances. "Near-certain" is still not "I am telling you this so that you may believe."
  3. Acts 4:28 is the hardest text for him. It calls the free choices of Pilate and the mob "what your plan had predestined." If God's knowledge of that night was probabilistic, Luke chose strange words. Open theists read "plan" as God's purpose for the cross, with the human actors supplying their own means. That works only if you already hold the view.
  4. The classical side has to answer Genesis 22:12 and Jeremiah 3:7. "Now I know" is read as demonstrative (James 2:21-23: Abraham's faith was "completed" by the act), and "I thought she would return" as rhetorical pathos. These readings are possible. They are also the "it says that but doesn't mean that" move he complained about. He is right that it is a move.
  5. Jeremiah 32:35 is weaker than it looks for him. "Did not enter my mind" (lo altah al-libbi) is a fixed idiom in Jeremiah (7:31; 19:5) meaning "I never commanded this." It is about God's will, not his foresight.
  6. Middle knowledge (Matt 11:21) is the quiet killer. If God knows what Tyre would have done under conditions that never happened, then God's knowledge of free acts is not limited to what exists. The open view has to read counterfactual knowledge as inference from character, and the text does not sound like inference.
  7. What survives for him: Scripture plainly depicts God's revealed will being resisted (Matt 23:37, Acts 7:51) and God desiring what does not happen (1 Tim 2:4). The classical tradition handles this with two senses of "will" (what God decrees, what God commands). He is right that this is a strain. He is wrong that it is a knockdown.
Open question

Scripture contains both a God who tests to know and a God who names kings centuries early. Open theism and classical theism each have a reading for every text; the deciding factor is which set you let govern. His version is a recognized position. It is not "what the Bible teaches" versus "philosophy."Confidence: high that it is open; the Peter and Acts 4 texts make me think the weight tilts classical, but that is interpretation, and I hold it loosely.

Ask in class"How did Jesus know Peter would deny him exactly three times before a rooster if free choices aren't knowable in advance?"

Prayer changes what God does

"Your prayers and relationship with him actually matter and affect his emotions. What you did made God do something differently now. We can change God's mind on something."His account of why prayer works

Case for

  • Ex 32:9-14; Num 14:11-20; Deut 9:18-20Moses intercedes; God "relented." Moses himself says God would have destroyed Israel "but the Lord listened to me." Intercession is given causal weight in the text.
  • 2 Kgs 20:1-6; Isa 38:1-5"You shall die." Hezekiah weeps and prays. "I have heard your prayer... I will add fifteen years to your life." An announced outcome reversed by prayer within one chapter.
  • Gen 18:22-33Abraham bargains from fifty to ten and God agrees at each step. The dialogue is portrayed as real negotiation.
  • Jas 5:16-18; 4:2"The prayer of a righteous person has great power as it is working." Elijah "prayed fervently that it might not rain, and for three years and six months it did not." "You do not have, because you do not ask."
  • Luke 11:5-13; 18:1-8The friend at midnight and the persistent widow. Jesus teaches prayer as something that moves a response by persistence.
  • Ezek 22:30-31; Isa 59:16"I sought for a man among them who should build up the wall and stand in the breach... but I found none. Therefore I have poured out my indignation." Judgment fell because no one interceded.
  • Matt 7:7-11"Ask, and it will be given." The whole grammar of petition assumes asking makes a difference.

Case against

  • Matt 6:8"Your Father knows what you need before you ask him." Prayer is not information transfer. Jesus says so immediately before teaching the Lord's Prayer.
  • Isa 65:24"Before they call I will answer; while they are yet speaking I will hear." The answer precedes the asking.
  • Matt 26:39, 42; 1 John 5:14"Not as I will, but as you will." "If we ask anything according to his will." Prayer is shaped to God's will, not God's will to prayer, in the model prayers.
  • Rom 8:26-27"We do not know what to pray for as we ought, but the Spirit himself intercedes... according to the will of God." Even our prayers are God's work in us.
  • Phil 2:13; Ezek 36:37"It is God who works in you, both to will and to work." "I will let the house of Israel ask me to do this for them." God moves people to ask for what he intends to give.
  • 2 Sam 12:16-23; 2 Cor 12:8-9David fasts and pleads for his child; the child dies. Paul pleads three times; the thorn stays. Prayer does not reliably "change God's mind," and Scripture records the refusals.

Cross-examination

  1. Both sides agree prayer matters. They disagree about the mechanism. He says prayer alters what God does. The classical tradition says prayer is the appointed means through which God does what he eternally willed to do in response to it. Practically identical. The texts (Exodus 32, 2 Kings 20) are written from the human side and sound like his version.
  2. His view has a cost he does not mention: if prayer changes God, then unanswered prayer means God weighed your request and said no, or that free agents overrode it. He prefers the second (God is "not responsible"). But David's child and Paul's thorn were not overridden by anyone. God declined. His system has to absorb divine refusal as readily as the classical one does.
  3. Ezekiel 22:30 cuts both ways. It says God sought an intercessor and found none, which supports him. It also says God had already decided what judgment deserved, and intercession was the appointed off-ramp, which is the classical model exactly.
  4. What he gets right pastorally: many believers pray as if it changes nothing, and Scripture does not talk that way. Moses is not told "it was already decided." He is told, in effect, "you made a difference." The narrative grants that and theology should not take it away.
Holds with repair

Scripture presents intercession as causally real and God as responsive. The repair: classical theology affirms the same thing with a different account of the mechanism, and his view has to handle refused prayers just as the other does.Confidence: high. This is less a dispute than two descriptions of one practice.

  • Jas 1:13-17"God cannot be tempted with evil, and he himself tempts no one." "Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above." Evil is not from God.

  • Luke 13:1-5; John 9:2-3Jesus refuses to read disasters as targeted divine judgments. Suffering is not a code to be decoded as God's intent.

  • Gen 50:20; 45:5-8"You meant evil against me, but God meant it for good." Joseph's brothers' free wickedness and God's intent, both fully affirmed, in one sentence.
  • Acts 2:23; 4:27-28The cross: free human evil, "according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God." If foreknowledge of a free evil act makes God complicit, the cross makes God complicit in the worst act in history. Scripture does not flinch from that; it calls it salvation.

  1. The argument does not escape its own problem. He also says God knows hearts, intentions, wiring, and that people are "pretty predictable." So in his model God saw a very high probability of abuse and still said go. The moral question is identical at 95% and at 100%. Lowering God's knowledge does not get God off the hook; it only moves the hook.

  3. The cross is the counterexample that ends the argument. Acts 2:23 says God foreknew and planned an event carried out by free, guilty men. Scripture holds the men fully responsible and God fully sovereign and does not call God cruel. Whatever logic reconciles that reconciles the marriage.
  4. His view has its own theodicy problem he does not raise.   5. Jeremiah 29:11 is misused in the way he criticized others for misusing verses. It is a promise to a nation after a judgment God sent. It does not say God's plan for an individual excludes suffering.
  6. What is right here: the instinct that God is not the author of evil (James 1:13) and that abusers, not God, are responsible (Ezekiel 18). Both classical and open theists affirm this.   • Gen 1:27; 9:6; Jas 3:9Humans are made in God's image, and after the fall the image is still the reason murder is wrong and cursing people is wrong. The image survives.

Cross-examination

  1. He is attacking a caricature of total depravity. The doctrine means sin is extensive (every faculty touched), not intensive (as evil as possible). Reformed people do not have to defend it.
  2. His own view is older than he realizes. He is not inventing a Hebraic alternative to tradition; he is choosing one tradition over another.
  3. Romans 5 is the text he must answer and did not. "Death reigned over those whose sinning was not like Adam's" (5:14) is usually read as infants, who die without personal transgression. If death is the wage of sin (6:23) and infants die, Paul is saying something about their condition. "Born innocent" has to account for why the innocent die.
  4. Psalm 51:5 is poetry and that is a fair point against the classical side. David is confessing, not writing dogma. But Genesis 8:21 and Ephesians 2:3 are not poetry, and "by nature" is a strong word.
  5. Where he is right: Scripture does not teach inherited guilt in the sense of being punished for Adam's act (Ezekiel 18 is plain), and it does speak of an age before moral knowledge. Where the classical side is right: Scripture does teach inherited condition, a bent nature that will sin, which is why no one has to be taught to.
  6. The practical gap is smaller than the rhetoric. Both sides say every person sins, every person needs Christ, and no infant is beyond God's mercy. The dispute is about the condition of the newborn, which no one can inspect.

Innocence-with-inherited-mortality has real texts and an Orthodox pedigree. Inherited corruption has Romans 5 and Ephesians 2. The church has never settled this to everyone's satisfaction, and the version of total depravity he refuted is not the one its defenders hold.Confidence: high that it is open; moderate lean toward "inherited condition, not inherited guilt," which is between the two positions he presented.

  • Matt 20:28; 26:28; Rev 5:9"A ransom for many." "Poured out for many." "Ransomed people for God from every tribe." "Many" and "from every," not "all."
  • John 3:36; Col 2:13; Acts 10:43; Eph 1:7"Whoever does not obey the Son... the wrath of God remains on him." Forgiveness is predicated of believers: "having forgiven us all our trespasses"; "everyone who believes in him receives forgiveness." Not "everyone has already been forgiven."
  • Rom 5:18 in context"All men" in 5:18 is paralleled by "the many" in 5:19 and qualified by "those who receive" in 5:17. Paul's "all" has a scope set by the argument.

Cross-examination

  1. On the scope of the death, he is on strong ground. 1 John 2:2 and 1 Timothy 2:6 are about as plain as texts get, and the "sheep" texts are compatible with a death sufficient for all and effective for believers. Most of the church (Orthodox, Catholic, Lutheran, Wesleyan, and a good many Reformed) holds some form of this. Limited atonement is the minority view he presents it as.
  2. On "already forgave all," he is on thin ice. 2 Corinthians 5:19 says God was "not counting their trespasses," and then verse 20 says "be reconciled to God," an imperative that makes no sense if it is already done. John 3:36 says wrath remains on the unbeliever. The New Testament predicates forgiveness of believers. The careful version is: Christ's death is sufficient for all and offered to all; forgiveness is received by faith. He said something stronger.
  3. Why the stronger version matters: "already forgiven, just accept it" is a Lutheran-leaning objective-justification view with its own defenders, but it has to explain why Scripture never tells the unbeliever "you are forgiven," only "repent and be forgiven" (Acts 2:38; 3:19).
  4. His caricature problem again: "He wouldn't waste his blood on anyone else" is a hostile paraphrase. Reformed theologians say the death is of infinite worth and freely offered to all; they dispute God's intent in it. Argue with the real position.
Holds with repair

Christ died for all: well-supported and widely held. "Already forgave all": overstated; Scripture conditions forgiveness on faith and describes wrath remaining on unbelief. Keep the first, soften the second.Confidence: high on both halves.

Ask in class"If everyone's sins are already forgiven, why does John 3:36 say the wrath of God remains on the one who doesn't believe?"

The dead sleep in a two-chambered grave

"They're not dead, they're asleep. Time is passing, they don't feel it. There's two chambers in the grave: rest and torment. We're all going to be resurrected from the grave, not from heaven."His account of the intermediate state

Case for

  • Luke 16:19-31Hades with two regions: Abraham's side and a place of torment, with a chasm between. His "two chambers" comes straight from here.
  • 1 Thess 4:13-16; 1 Cor 15:51-52; John 11:11-14; Dan 12:2"Those who are asleep." "We shall not all sleep." "Lazarus has fallen asleep." "Those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake." Sleep is the standard biblical word for death.
  • Acts 2:27, 31, 34; John 3:13"You will not abandon my soul to Hades." "David did not ascend into the heavens." "No one has ascended into heaven but he who descended." Jesus went to Hades, not heaven, at death; David is not in heaven.
  • Rev 20:13; Hos 13:14; 1 Cor 15:55"Death and Hades gave up the dead." "O Sheol, where is your sting?" The resurrection empties the grave.
  • Luke 23:43; Acts 2:31"Today you will be with me in Paradise," spoken by one about to enter Hades (Acts 2). So Paradise, on that day, was the blessed side of the grave, not the throne room.

Case against

  • Luke 16:24-25; Rev 6:9-11The rich man is conscious, in pain, asking for water, and remembering his brothers. Lazarus is "comforted." The martyrs cry "how long?" and are told to wait. Neither chamber is "asleep, unaware of time." His own proof text describes awareness and duration.
  • Phil 1:21-23; 2 Cor 5:6-8"To depart and be with Christ, which is far better." "Away from the body and at home with the Lord." Paul expects death to bring him to Christ, not to a waiting room.
  • 2 Cor 12:2-4; Luke 23:43Paul equates "Paradise" with "the third heaven." If Paradise is heaven in Paul, the thief went to heaven.
  • Heb 12:22-23; Rev 6:9; 7:9-17"The heavenly Jerusalem... the spirits of the righteous made perfect." Souls "under the altar," before the throne. The righteous dead are located in heaven in these texts.
  • 1 Thess 4:14"God will bring with him those who have fallen asleep." Jesus comes with the dead in Christ, then raises their bodies (v. 16). The dead are with him before the resurrection.
  • Matt 17:3; 22:32; Luke 20:38; Acts 7:59Moses and Elijah appear, conscious. "He is not God of the dead, but of the living." "Receive my spirit." The dead are alive to God now.

Cross-examination

  1. He has fused two things that Scripture keeps apart: the body, which sleeps in the grave and is raised from it, and the spirit, which is with Christ. "Resurrected from the grave, not from heaven" is true of bodies and false of persons. 1 Thessalonians 4:14-16 has both: Jesus brings the dead with him, then their bodies rise.
  2. "They don't feel time passing" contradicts his own text. Luke 16 is a conscious rich man in torment and a conscious Lazarus comforted. If he wants Luke 16 for two chambers he has to take its awareness too.
  3. The Acts 2 / John 3:13 texts are his best and they do complicate the "straight to heaven" picture, at least before the ascension. Many hold that Christ's descent changed the location of the righteous dead (Eph 4:8-9 is sometimes read this way). That is speculative in both directions.
  4. "RIP versus RIT" is an invented illustration. Requiescat in pace is an ancient Christian epitaph; "rest in torment" was never carved on anything. Harmless as rhetoric, but students will repeat it as history.
  5. Why he needs this: if the dead were timelessly in heaven, it would support a timeless realm, which threatens case 06. So he places them in a grave where time passes. But Revelation 6:10 gives him time-aware saints in heaven, which serves his time argument better than the grave does.
Overstated

Scripture uses "sleep" for the body and "with Christ" for the person, and his account keeps only the first half. Luke 16 supports two regions but not unawareness. Paul's "depart and be with Christ" is the plainest text and he did not address it.Confidence: high on the fusion error; the geography of the intermediate state is itself uncertain and I would not press anyone hard on it.

Ask in class"Paul says to die is to be with Christ, which is far better. Is Christ in the grave?"

God needs a man; you can limit God on earth

"Why does God need a man? Because the heavens are the heavens of God, but the earth is given to the sons of men. He looks for a man to do his works on the earth through a man. So you can limit God on the earth."The partnership claim

Case for

  • Ps 115:16; Gen 1:26-28; Ps 8:4-8"The heavens are the Lord's heavens, but the earth he has given to the children of man." "Have dominion." "You have given him dominion over the works of your hands." Delegated rule is a creation ordinance.
  • Ezek 22:30; Isa 59:16; 63:5"I sought for a man... but I found none." "He saw that there was no man, and wondered that there was no one to intercede." God is portrayed as looking for human partners.
  • 2 Chr 16:9; Gen 6:8; Ex 3:10"The eyes of the Lord run to and fro throughout the whole earth, to give strong support to those whose heart is blameless." Noah found. Moses sent. The pattern is one person, then a people.
  • Rom 10:14-15; Matt 9:37-38; 2 Cor 5:20; 1 Cor 3:9"How are they to hear without someone preaching?" "Pray earnestly to the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers." "Ambassadors for Christ." "God's fellow workers." The New Testament treats human agency as the ordinary means of God's work.
  • Mark 6:5-6; Matt 13:58"He could do no mighty work there... because of their unbelief." A real text where human response limits what Jesus does.

Case against

  • Ps 24:1; 115:3; Dan 4:35"The earth is the Lord's and the fullness thereof." "He does all that he pleases." "None can stay his hand." Psalm 115 itself (v. 3) says God does whatever he pleases, three verses before v. 16.
  • Acts 17:25; Job 41:11; Rom 11:35"Nor is he served by human hands, as though he needed anything." "Who has first given to me?" God needs nothing. The lecturer's own Trinity argument (case 04) establishes this.
  • Isa 59:16; 63:5His own texts continue: "then his own arm brought him salvation." When no man is found, God acts alone. The search for a man is not a dependency.
  • Isa 46:10-11; Prov 21:1; Ps 33:10-11"I have purposed, and I will do it." "The king's heart is a stream of water in the hand of the Lord." "The Lord brings the counsel of the nations to nothing." God's purposes are not finally limitable by people.
  • Rom 9:16-18; Ex 9:16God raised up Pharaoh, a hostile man, to show his power. God works through people who are not partnering with him at all.

Cross-examination

  1. "God chooses to work through people" holds. "God needs people" does not. He said both. The first is the whole shape of biblical history. The second contradicts Acts 17:25, Isaiah 59:16, and his own argument that God's love was complete before creation.
  2. Psalm 115:16 is read against its own psalm. Verse 3: "Our God is in the heavens; he does all that he pleases." Verse 16 describes delegation, not abdication. A landlord who gives tenants the run of a house has not lost the deed.
  3. Mark 6:5 is the real text for "limiting God" and he did not use it. It is also narrower than his claim: Jesus "could not" do mighty works in Nazareth because faith is the ordinary channel for them, not because Nazareth had veto power over God.
  4. The pastoral payload is right: your yes matters, and Scripture never treats human participation as decorative. The theological frame around it is the problem. "You can limit God" will be heard as "God is stuck without you," and Isaiah says otherwise in the very verse he cites.
Overstated

Partnership is the pattern of Scripture and he teaches it well. "Needs" and "limit" are the wrong words; his own texts show God acting alone when no partner is found, and his own Trinity argument shows God needing nothing.Confidence: high.

Ask in class"Isaiah 59:16 says God looked for a man, found none, and 'his own arm brought salvation.' Does God need a man or prefer one?"

Jesus grew in knowledge

"Jesus came into limited knowledge and he grew. He grew in wisdom, stature, and favor with God. He wasn't two years old knowing everyone's thoughts."His Christology, in a student exchange

Case for

  • Luke 2:40, 52"The child grew and became strong, filled with wisdom." "Jesus increased in wisdom and in stature and in favor with God and man." Growth in wisdom is stated flatly.
  • Mark 13:32; Matt 24:36"Concerning that day or that hour, no one knows, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father." Jesus names something he does not know.
  • Mark 5:30-32; 11:13; Luke 8:45"Who touched my garments?" He goes to the fig tree to see if it has fruit. Narratives that read naturally as not-knowing.
  • Heb 5:8; 2:17-18; 4:15"He learned obedience through what he suffered." Made like us "in every respect." Tempted "in every respect." Learning is part of a genuine human life.
  • Phil 2:6-8"Emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant." Whatever kenosis means, it means a real descent into limitation.

Case against

  • John 2:24-25; 1:47-48; 4:17-18; 6:64"He knew all people... he himself knew what was in man." Nathanael under the fig tree. The Samaritan woman's five husbands. "Jesus knew from the beginning who would betray him."
  • John 16:30; 21:17; 13:1, 3"Now we know that you know all things." "Lord, you know everything." "Knowing that his hour had come... knowing that the Father had given all things into his hands."
  • Col 2:3, 9"In whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge." "In him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily."
  • Matt 9:4; 12:25; Luke 6:8"Knowing their thoughts." Repeatedly. The Gospels show Jesus reading minds as routine.

Cross-examination

  1. He is right, and so is the other column. The Gospels show both growth and supernatural knowledge. The church's answer (Chalcedon, 451) is one person in two natures: as man he grew and did not know the hour; as God nothing is hidden from him. He did not mention this, but his statement is compatible with it.
  2. His emphasis is sound pastoral Christology. Hebrews 2 and 5 make Jesus's learning the ground of his sympathy. A Jesus who never learned anything cannot "sympathize with our weaknesses" in the way Hebrews says he does.
  3. "He wasn't two years old knowing everyone's thoughts" is speculation in either direction. Luke gives us one scene at twelve and silence before it. He said "I don't know" first, which is the right answer.
  4. Minor overreach: "grew in favor with God" he takes as evidence of limited development. Luke's phrase echoes 1 Samuel 2:26 about Samuel and is a growth formula; it does not mean the Father approved of him more at thirty than at three.
Holds

Real human growth in Jesus is stated by Luke, Mark, and Hebrews. The classical two-natures doctrine affirms it alongside the "knew all things" texts. No dispute here, only an unmentioned framework that would strengthen what he said.Confidence: high.

Ask in class"How do you hold Luke 2:52 together with John 2:24, 'he knew all people'?"

Every human bond rehearses a bond with God

"You have to learn how to be a child because the invitation is to become a child of God. He gives us siblings because we're brothers and sisters of Christ. Friends because we've become friends of God. A spouse because we're the bride. Kids so you feel what he feels toward you."The ladder of relationships

Case for

  • Eph 1:5; Rom 8:15-16; Gal 4:6Adoption as sons; "Abba! Father!" The child relation is the New Testament's primary name for salvation.
  • John 20:17; Heb 2:11-12; Rom 8:29"Go to my brothers." "He is not ashamed to call them brothers." "Firstborn among many brothers."
  • John 15:13-15; Jas 2:23; Ex 33:11"No longer servants... I have called you friends." Abraham "was called a friend of God." Moses spoke with God "as a man speaks to his friend."
  • Eph 5:31-32; Hos 2:16-20; Isa 54:5; Rev 19:7; 21:2"This mystery is profound, and I am saying that it refers to Christ and the church." Marriage is explicitly declared a type of Christ and the church, by Paul, and the Old Testament already spoke of God as husband.
  • Matt 22:30; Luke 20:34-36"In the resurrection they neither marry nor are given in marriage." Marriage is temporary; what it pictured is not. His use of this text is exactly right.
  • Luke 15:20; Isa 49:15; Ps 103:13; Matt 7:9-11The father runs. "Can a woman forget her nursing child?" "As a father shows compassion." "If you then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children..." Scripture repeatedly argues from parental love to God's love. The fifth rung has a direct biblical method behind it.
  • Gen 1:27; 2:18"Male and female he created them... in the image of God." "It is not good that the man should be alone." Relationship is in the design, not added to it.

Case against

  • 1 Cor 7:7-8, 32-35; Matt 19:12Paul wishes all were single as he is; the unmarried are "anxious about the things of the Lord" with undivided devotion. Jesus honors those who forgo marriage for the kingdom. A curriculum that requires spouse and children leaves out Jesus and Paul.
  • Isa 56:3-5; Ps 68:5-6Eunuchs are promised "a name better than sons and daughters." God is "father of the fatherless." Scripture goes out of its way to include those outside the family ladder.
  • MethodThe ladder is an inference from Scripture's metaphors. Scripture uses each image; it does not say God arranged human life as a graded rehearsal of them. The direction of the argument (heavenly reality explains earthly design) is plausible for marriage because Ephesians 5:32 says so, and asserted for the others.
  • John 1:12-13; 3:3Being a child of God is by new birth, not by being good at being a human child. The rehearsal metaphor could suggest the earthly relationship earns or trains the heavenly one; Scripture grounds it in grace.

Cross-examination

  1. This is the most original and most useful stretch of the lecture, and it has more textual support than he gave it. Ephesians 5:32 makes marriage a designed type. Matthew 7:11 and Isaiah 49:15 argue from parenthood to God. Hebrews 2 makes brotherhood literal in Christ. He could have cited all of these.
  2. The fifth rung is the strongest and the one Scripture itself uses most. The Bible's standard method for teaching God's love is to point at a parent. He has rediscovered a biblical argument form.
  3. The repair is about who is on the ladder. Jesus climbed two rungs (child, friend; arguably sibling). Paul climbed three. Neither married nor fathered. A frame that implies a full human curriculum requires marriage and children needs the exceptions written in, and Scripture writes them in emphatically (Isaiah 56).
  4. Hold it as illustration, not mechanism. "Each relationship pictures a way we relate to God" is biblical. "God built the family so you would learn these in order" is a sermon, and a good one, with the status of a sermon.
Holds with repair

Each image is biblical and the method (argue from human bonds to God's) is Scripture's own. The repair: write in singleness and childlessness as full paths, and present the ladder as a reading of the metaphors rather than a revealed structure.Confidence: high.

Ask in class"Where are Jesus and Paul on the ladder?"

Smaller errors, for the record

None of these change the big picture. They are the kind of thing students repeat.

Revelation 10:9 → 13:8

"The Lamb slain from the foundation of the world" is 13:8. The Greek word order is ambiguous: "from the foundation" may modify "written in the book of life" (so ESV, following 17:8) rather than "slain" (so NIV, KJV). 1 Peter 1:20 ("foreknown before the foundation of the world") is the firmer text for a pre-creation plan. Either way, both sides read it as plan, not event.

Colossians 1:16 "thrones"

Refers to a rank of created powers, not God's throne. Psalm 93:2, Psalm 45:6, Lamentations 5:19 call God's throne eternal.

"Three God-is statements"

Scripture has many: love, light, spirit, consuming fire, faithful, one, holy, a righteous judge, greater than our heart. "Consuming fire" is not a one-off; it is in Deuteronomy twice and Hebrews once.

"The angel of the Lord is always Jesus"

Fails in Zechariah 1:12 (prays to the Lord), Zechariah 3:1-6 (distinct from the Lord), and Luke 1:11, 19 (Gabriel). Some appearances function as theophanies; the title is not a fixed marker.

Ephesians 1:4-5 "us" = humanity

Paul writes "us" to the saints at Ephesus (1:1). Reading "us" as all humanity is possible as a corporate-election view but it is a reading, and the verse is also the Reformed proof text for the opposite. Not a neutral anchor.

Ezekiel 20:25 and Sinai

"Statutes that were not good" refers to later judgments on the wilderness generation (vv. 21-26), not to the Sinai covenant itself.

"RIP vs RIT"

"Rest in torment" was never an epitaph. Rhetorical invention presented as church history.

The 6,000-year earth

Dropped as an unexamined assumption ("if creation happened around 6,000 years ago"). Not argued, not necessary to anything else he said, and a lens of its own.

"Scripture verses by Christians pushed the Holocaust"

Christian anti-Judaism was a real precondition. Nazi ideology was racial and largely hostile to the church. A contributing cause stated as the cause.

TULIP at its harshest

"God will break you until you yield," "he wouldn't waste his blood," "a child is born wicked to the core." Each point stated in a form its defenders reject. He half-admits this ("I'm saying it in a harsh way").

Psalm 139:16

He asked for this one to be done without AI first. It stays closed unless you open it.

Open the spoiler only after you've read the psalm yourself

The Hebrew is genuinely ambiguous, and that is almost certainly his hint. Verse 16 reads, word for word: "Your eyes saw my golem [unformed mass, embryo]; and in your book all of them [kullam] were written; days were formed, and not one among them." The question is what "all of them" refers to.

Reading A (NIV, ESV, NASB): "all of them" = the days. "All the days ordained for me were written in your book before one of them came to be." This is the reading that supports exhaustive foreknowledge of a life.

Reading B (KJV, JPS Tanakh): "all of them" = the body parts from verses 13-15. KJV: "in thy book all my members were written, which in continuance were fashioned, when as yet there was none of them." JPS: "Your eyes saw my unformed limbs; they were all recorded in Your book; in due time they were formed, to the very last one of them." On this reading the verse is about God's blueprint for David's body, not his biography.

Context favors B, or at least blunts A. Verses 13-16 are one unit about formation in the womb: kidneys, knitting, frame, unformed mass. "Days were formed" (yamim yutzaru) is odd Hebrew; yatzar is the potter's verb used of shaping bodies (Gen 2:7). Even on reading A, "ordained days" in a poem of intimacy reads most naturally as God's intention and care for a life, which both open and classical readers affirm, rather than a decree of every free act.

But the psalm does not help his larger case as much as he may think. Verse 4: "Even before a word is on my tongue, behold, O Lord, you know it altogether." That is foreknowledge of a free human act (speech), stated without qualification. Verses 7-12 are the strongest omnipresence text in Scripture and support his cosmology. Verse 16 is ambiguous. Verse 4 is not. If the point of the homework is "verse 16 doesn't prove exhaustive foreknowledge," that is fair. If the point is "Psalm 139 is on our side about God's knowledge," verse 4 is in the way.

Confidence: high on the ambiguity (the translations split), moderate on which reading is better (B fits the stanza; A has the majority of modern versions). Low confidence that the psalm settles anything about open theism either way; it is a poem of being known, not a treatise on knowledge.
