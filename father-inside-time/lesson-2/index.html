Unchanging, not unmoved

Lesson 1 put God inside time. Lesson 2 follows that through. If heaven has a clock, what happens to the verses that seem to say God has none, and to the verses that say he never changes? The lecture answers by drawing one line through God himself: his nature on one side, his dealings with people on the other.

I

Four words that carry the lesson

The lecture starts with vocabulary, because the argument turns on four words that sound alike and are not. Get these straight and the rest of the hour follows.

"All creation, including the heavens, is governed by time. This is controversial. People don't like this. I think it's true."The lecture's thesis for the night

Temporary

Lasts for a while, then ends.

Some created things are temporary. God is not. In the lecture's map, Hades is temporary; the lake of fire is not.

Temporal

Governed by time. Things happen in order.

"Temporal is different from temporary." Heaven, the lecture says, is "temporal and forever." So is God.

Timeless

No passing of time at all.

The idea the lecture wants to retire, for heaven and for God: "an error that we got from Greek philosophers, not Scripture."

Transcending

Acting on time from inside it.

The sun stood still (Josh 10:13). Wasted years can be restored. "Can God transcend time? Sure, he can do whatever he wants."

"Eternal" means two things

Both sides say God is eternal and mean "always existed, always will." The classical side also folds "outside time" into the word. The lecture's advice is practical: when you use "eternal" with someone, define it first, or you will argue past each other for an hour.

Its own picture of time is a ruler. "Do inches exist? The name 'inches' was created, but the length just is. We measure progression using time." And a student added a line the lecture agreed with: time is what makes memory possible. Before creation, the Father, Son and Spirit were already "in relationship, in communication, in memory."

Where it strains
  • "Transcend" is doing two jobs. The lecture says God can transcend time, and also "I don't think God goes into the future." Those fit together only if transcending means acting on time from inside it, which is how this page uses the word. Ask which one is meant.
  • "Time is a measure, like inches" is philosophy, not exegesis. It may be right. It is the same kind of claim the lecture faults the other side for making. Lesson 1's case 06 tries it.
  • The lecture holds this openly. "I teach that view. I don't say that it's the only truth. I want you to go on a journey and explore."
II

Heaven runs in order

The argument is short. Wherever there is a before and an after, there is time. The lecture finds before-and-after all through the unseen realm, including around the throne.

"So in heaven, clearly God experiences the passing of time. And that's just right there in the Bible."

  1. "He gave them permission, and the impure spirits came out and went into the pigs."Mark 5:13Spirits move from one place to another. Moving takes a before and an after.
  2. "Our struggle is ... against the spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly realms."Eph 6:12A struggle starts and is decided. "You can't struggle unless there's a progression."
  3. "The Lord has established his throne in heaven."Ps 103:19Established, so there was a time before it was.
  4. "They were calling to one another: 'Holy, holy, holy.'"Isa 6:3Calling and answering is a sequence, even at the throne.
  5. The seals open, one after another.Rev 6:1–8:1Worship, then a seal, then another. The lecture placed the seals in Revelation 5, where the Lamb takes the scroll; they open in chapters 6 to 8.
  6. "There was silence in heaven for about half an hour."Rev 8:1 (the lecture said 7:15)Measured, in heaven.

The conclusion: temporal and forever

Heaven was made, so it had a start. It is ordered in time, so it is temporal. It will not end, so it is not temporary. The lecture applies the same three words to God's throne and to people: made, in time, and able to live forever. That last phrase opens a door it walks through later (section VI).

Where it strains

The classical side agrees that heaven has time. Its claim is about God, not about the place he made, so this list lands on a folk belief, "there's no time in heaven," and not on the position the lecture opposes. The folk belief is worth losing. It just isn't the debate. Lesson 1's page says the same about Revelation 8:1.

From the room

"I thought hell was the absence of God"

The lecture's answer is Psalm 139: "If I make my bed in the depths, you are there" (Ps 139:8). Everywhere present is not the same as everywhere showing up. It added "if I go to the highest mountain"; the psalm's four places are the heavens, the depths, the wings of the dawn and the far side of the sea.

From the room

Jacob's ladder, and the hills full of fire

Bethel (Gen 28:12) and Elisha's servant (2 Kgs 6:17) are eyes opened, not trips taken. "Open his eyes, Lord, so that he may see." The unseen realm was already there, in the same place. Whether the ladder was a private vision or visible to anyone standing there, the lecture called "not super important."

From the room

Then why did Jesus look up?

At Lazarus's tomb, "Jesus looked up and said, 'Father, I thank you that you have heard me'" (John 11:41). The lecture reads looking up as acknowledging a realm we don't see, not as pointing at a location. "If the earth is round, looking up is really any direction." The next verse supports it: "I knew that you always hear me."

III

The four verses used for a timeless God

The lecture takes the four texts it says people quote for a God outside time and puts each one back in its paragraph. Its verdict on all four: "if you look at them in context, they seem to fail to hold that weight." Pick one.

Quotations are from the NIV. The KJV wording is given where the argument depends on it.

"With the Lord a day is like a thousand years, and a thousand years are like a day."

2 Peter 3:8
How it gets used

Time means nothing to God. A day and a millennium are the same to him, so he stands outside both. Sometimes it is also stretched into a formula for the days of creation.

In its paragraph

The next sentence answers scoffers about the delay of Christ's return: "The Lord is not slow in keeping his promise ... Instead he is patient with you, not wanting anyone to perish" (v. 9). "We're not dealing with a scientific formula." The lecture calls it one of its "top 10 most out-of-context" verses.

CheckHolds

Careful readers on both sides agree it is about patience. One caution from Lesson 1's cross-examination: the comparison only works because God's relation to duration is unlike ours. It does not prove timelessness, and it does not prove the opposite either.

"There will be no more delay!"

Revelation 10:6KJV: "that there should be time no longer"
How it gets used

There is time now, but one day time itself will stop, and eternity will be timeless.

In its paragraph

Modern translations read "no more delay": the angel swears that judgment will not be put off any longer. The lecture reads it from the NASB: "there will no longer be a delay."

CheckHolds

The Greek word is chronos, and "delay" is the reading of nearly every translation since the KJV. The very next verse settles it: "But in the days when the seventh angel is about to sound his trumpet" (v. 7). A verse that ends time cannot be followed by days.

"... be glory, majesty, power and authority, through Jesus Christ our Lord, before all ages, now and forevermore!"

Jude 25ESV and NASB: "before all time"
How it gets used

God existed "before all time," so he exists outside it.

In its paragraph

The Greek is aiōn, "age." The NIV says "before all ages." The point is God's eternity, that he was God before anything and will be after, not a statement about time as such.

CheckDoesn't escape

"Before all ages" is not weaker than "before all time." It still places God before every age there has been. Both sides affirm that much, so neither wording decides the question. The phrase is also missing from the manuscripts behind the KJV, which ends "both now and ever."

"I am the Alpha and the Omega, the First and the Last, the Beginning and the End."

Revelation 22:13 (the lecture cited 20:13)
How it gets used

He exists at the beginning and the end at the same moment, so all of history is present to him at once.

In its paragraph

He was there at the beginning and he is there at the end. "Before there was anything, God is." The simultaneous reading, the lecture says, "is based more on philosophy than it is on just Scripture."

CheckHolds

The title comes from Isaiah, where it is a claim of sovereignty over history set against idols: "I am the first and I am the last; apart from me there is no God" (Isa 44:6). It says God rules from start to finish. It does not say how he relates to the middle.

What the four can and cannot do

Clearing four misused verses removes four bad arguments. It does not settle the question, because the classical position never rested on them. It rests on the "before" texts at right and on philosophical arguments (Augustine's Confessions XI, Boethius). The lecture did not take those on this week. Case 19

IV

What changes and what doesn't

"If God is timeless, outside of time, then nothing about him can change." That is the hinge. A God in time can change, so the lecture has to say what does and what doesn't. Its answer is one line drawn through God.

"There are ways in which he does not change, and there are ways in which he does."The lecture's summary of the textbook's view

Never changes · his nature

Who he is

  • love
  • holiness
  • wisdom
  • power
  • goodness
  • faithfulness

"Anything regarding his nature." When God says "I do not change," the lecture hears: "I'm loving and forgiving no matter what. Sorry, you can't do anything about that."

Can change · his dealings with free people

What he does next

  • mind
  • plans
  • will
  • emotions
  • responses
  • methods

"God's a free-will being dealing with another free-will being. When those intersect, there can be some change." A God who cannot change, it says, is "an emotionless god, because emotions require a change."

Where it strains

The lecture lists Wayne Grudem with the classical side. Grudem's own definition draws nearly the same line: God is "unchanging in his being, perfections, purposes, and promises, yet God does act and feel emotions, and he acts and feels differently in response to different situations" (Systematic Theology, ch. 11). So the real disagreement is narrower than "emotionless God versus relational God." It comes down to one word: are his purposes fixed, or only his character? Case 20

The lecture

Its favorite. Saul is begging, and Samuel is saying "stop asking me, I'm not changing my mind on this." God has torn the kingdom away after "strike one, strike two, strike three, strike four." In the same chapter, God regrets making Saul king, twice.

Check · strong, and older than the lecture

All three verses use the same Hebrew verb, nacham, which is why the tension is real. The strongest form of the lecture's reading is that verse 29 is about a sworn verdict. Hebrews 6:17 says God confirms with an oath what will not change. Lesson 1's case 07 works this through.

The lecture's case for real change

Five times Moses said no

At the bush, Moses objects five times: "Who am I," "what shall I tell them," "what if they do not believe me," "I have never been eloquent," and "please send someone else" (Ex 3:11–4:13). Only then does Aaron come in. "It wasn't plan A for Moses to have Aaron as his mouthpiece." The same pattern shows at the golden calf: "the Lord relented" (Ex 32:14).

Where it strains

Aaron was already on the road

Read the next line slowly: "What about your brother, Aaron the Levite? I know he can speak well. He is already on his way to meet you" (Ex 4:14). Aaron set out before Moses' last refusal. That fits a God who saw it coming better than one caught off guard. It doesn't hand the point to the other side either. "The Lord's anger burned against Moses" in the same verse is a real response to a real refusal.

A quotation to check before repeating

The lecture quotes William Lane Craig as the voice of the classical view: God "never thinks successive thoughts. He never performs successive activities. He never undergoes even the most trivial alteration." We could not find those sentences in Craig's published work, and they do not describe his position. Craig, a research professor of philosophy at Talbot School of Theology, holds that God is timeless without creation and temporal since creation. He says God does change in creating the world (Time and Eternity, 2001). If the sentences are his, they describe a God without creation, which is not the God the lecture is talking about. On the question that matters here, Craig is closer to the lecture than to its opponents. Case 20

V

Figure of speech, or the real thing?

The classical answer to every "God regretted" and "God relented" is that it is a figure of speech: human language for something that does not happen in God. The lecture grants that some figures are real. Its question is whether all the change texts are figures, or only seem so if you have already decided God is timeless.

Its honest limit, stated twice: "There's real anthropomorphism ... I don't know where that line is." Its test: does the plain reading violate God's nature? If not, "maybe we should just believe it."

Can you mix the two?

A student asked whether a person can take some answers from one side and some from the other. Sometimes, the lecture said, but not these two: "Is God timeless plus changes his mind? That's a bad concept. They don't work together." Most believers hold both without noticing, it said, "a little bit of a mishmash." Lesson 1 called that "lazy theology."

Where it strains

The lecture's test, "does it violate his nature?", is a good one, but both sides pass their own reading through it. The classical reader says real regret would violate a perfect knowledge. The open reader says it wouldn't. The test moves the argument to what God's nature includes, which is where it was in Lesson 1. A student's simpler question deserves an answer in class: "Could God be both in time and timeless?" The lecture's reply: "Illogically, no."

VI

Who lives forever?

The lecture raised this only because it touches time, and flagged it as "pretty controversial." Heaven is temporal and forever. Are people forever too, by nature? Or is living forever a gift, given through the tree of life?

"I keep both available in my view."The lecture's stance, after a five- to seven-year study it says is still going

The common view

Every soul lives on

Once made, a person exists forever, with God or apart from him. Final punishment is conscious and does not end.

  • "They will go away to eternal punishment, but the righteous to eternal life."Matthew 25:46 · one Greek word, aiōnios, for both
  • "The smoke of their torment will rise for ever and ever. There will be no rest day or night for those who worship the beast."Revelation 14:11
  • "Some to everlasting life, others to shame and everlasting contempt."Daniel 12:2
  • "The worms that eat them do not die, and the fire is not quenched."Mark 9:48

Held by most of the church since Augustine (City of God XXI).

The lecture's lean: conditional immortality

Life is a gift; the lost come to an end

Immortality comes only through the tree of life. After a just and limited punishment, those who refuse it cease to exist. The lecture's map: Hades now, which is temporary; the lake of fire after the judgment, where "no one's there now" and from which there is "no reversal."

  • "He must not be allowed to ... take also from the tree of life and eat, and live forever."Genesis 3:22
  • "... who alone is immortal."1 Timothy 6:16 · and Romans 2:7, "seek glory, honor and immortality"
  • "Be afraid of the One who can destroy both soul and body in hell."Matthew 10:28 · 2 Thessalonians 1:9, "everlasting destruction"
  • "They will be ashes under the soles of your feet."Malachi 4:3 · Psalm 37:20

An old minority. Justin Martyr wrote that the souls of the wicked are punished "so long as God wills them to exist" (Dialogue with Trypho 5). Edward Fudge's The Fire That Consumes (1982) is the standard case, and John Stott held it tentatively.

How the lecture got there

It says it has "120-something verses" on one side "and the three that seem to point a different direction," and that the common view "we got more from philosophy than we did from Scripture." "I'm going to be like the Bereans. I care about truth more than about being right."

Where it strains

"No good evidence that humans are included" in endless torment is too quick. Revelation 20:10 is about the devil, the beast and the false prophet, but 14:11 is about "those who worship the beast," which means people. Conditionalists answer that rising smoke is a lasting memorial of a finished destruction, as with Edom in Isaiah 34:10. That is the real argument, and it should be made.

Where it strains

"From Greek philosophers" is half right. Plato's Phaedo does argue that the soul cannot die. But the strongest text for the common view is Jesus' own Matthew 25:46, which sets the two fates side by side with one word. Counting verses doesn't settle it. Weighing that one does. Case 22

VII

We're staying. Evil's leaving.

Where you picture God shapes how you pray. "If 'our Father in heaven' is a picture of someone far away in a different world, you have the wrong picture." The lecture's alternative is a story in three frames, and the last frame is on this earth.

"Our destination is not heaven. Our destination is a more perfect union, without all the barriers."The lecture

unseen realmseen realmGod's presence, as people meet itthe barrier
Eden: one place, both realms"Why is it not ever physically found? Because it's ... the union of the spiritual and the physical in a more married way."Gen 2–3
Now: a barrierSin put a barrier between the realms. God still fills both; we see one.Gen 3:22–24 · Rom 8:20–21
Then: heaven comes down"God's dwelling place is now among the people, and he will dwell with them."Rev 21:1–3 (the lecture said Revelation 20)

The seed, not the rescue boat

"Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven" (Matt 6:10). The kingdom grows like a seed and a stone (Matt 13:31–32; Dan 2:35), "in your midst" already (Luke 17:21). The Father doesn't pull his people off a doomed planet.

Weeds out first

"First collect the weeds and tie them in bundles to be burned; then gather the wheat and bring it into my barn" (Matt 13:30). "We're staying. Evil's leaving." A student pushed back: the wheat is gathered too. The lecture agreed it is "technically still him taking the wheat," gathered to a Lord who has come to the earth.

"I didn't leave you"

The lecture's picture of a near Father is the Footprints poem: one set of prints in the sand, and the answer, "those are my footprints. I carried you." Seated with Christ in heavenly places (Eph 2:6), "we're not trying to get to heaven."

Where it strains
  • "The flood is not a destruction, it's a cleansing." Peter calls it both: "the world of that time was deluged and destroyed" (2 Pet 3:6). The coming fire is described the same way, "the elements will be destroyed by fire" (v. 10), and it still ends in "a new heaven and a new earth" (v. 13). Renewal through judgment fits the text better than a gentle rinse.
  • "A restoration of all things, which Hebrews talks about." The phrase is Acts 3:21: "until the time comes for God to restore everything." Hebrews 12:27 speaks of "the removing of what can be shaken."
  • "Eden isn't physical." Genesis 2:10–14 names its rivers, two of them the Tigris and the Euphrates, and says there is gold in the land. Eden as a place where both realms met can stand without denying it was a place.
  • The direction holds. Revelation 21:2–3 has the city "coming down out of heaven," and Romans 8:21 has creation itself set free. Heaven coming here is a well-supported reading. Case 25
VIII

Your picture of God decides your next move

The lecture says it does not argue about time for its own sake. "It only matters because I see my friends and I see the consequences in their life."

"God allows on the earth what humans allow on the earth."The lecture's rule

The story it told

Two bad things a day, for three weeks

A Christian friend's business was hit every day for three weeks: money lost, staff leaving, the bills nearly unpaid. "But God's in control, so I guess I have to take it." The lecture's question: where does "Resist the devil, and he will flee from you" (Jas 4:7) fit in that picture? And the verse it says comes to mind: "my people are destroyed from lack of knowledge" (Hos 4:6).

Its sharper point is about the word "allow." "We say, well, God didn't cause it, but he allowed it. Did he? Did he allow it, or did we allow that?" On its view God "allows freedom, which has chaos, and then he's going to give redemption and he's going to hold you through it."

Where it strains

Permission language is in the Gospels. At the pigs, Jesus "gave them permission" (Mark 5:13), the same verse the lecture used for heaven's clock.

Job is the hard case, and the lecture's answer reverses the text. In passing it said, "I don't think God gave permission to Satan. I think Job allowed that." But God says, "Very well, then, everything he has is in your power" (Job 1:12; 2:6). God says to Satan, "you incited me against him" (2:3), and the narrator calls it "all the trouble the Lord had brought on him" (42:11). Job's "what I feared has come upon me" (3:25) is a lament, not the cause. Case 23

The lecture's test for any theology, this page included
1

With him more

Does it make you want to be with him?

2

Like him more

Does it make you want to be like him?

3

Partner with him

Does it send you out to do what he wants done on the earth?

"Bad theology, you could have one of those or two of them. You usually don't have three." Its charge against a settled future is that it fails the third: a church that is comforted and inactive. "Why are we evangelizing? Why are we praying for the sick?"

Limited by choice, not by weakness

"I believe he's unlimited, but in his sovereignty, in his wisdom, he's chosen to limit his activity on the earth ... to partnership with man." This is a careful restatement of Lesson 1's "God needs a man." It says God chose this, not that God is stuck. The lecture's picture is its own house: "I'm in charge, but they are totally free. And within that, I can guide them, and they can change my mind."

Where it strains

The passivity charge fits fatalism. It does not fit the history of the settled view. The modern missions movement was launched by a Calvinist, William Carey, in 1792. The Westminster Confession (3.1) says God's decree takes away neither human freedom nor "the liberty or contingency of second causes." Paul held both: "I endure everything for the sake of the elect" (2 Tim 2:10), and "how can they hear without someone preaching to them?" (Rom 10:14). The test of three is a good test. The settled view can pass it. Case 24

IX

Then how do I find his will?

A student asked the question everyone in the room was holding: if the future isn't written, how do I walk in God's will, say, about who to marry? The lecture's answer was one word, then a picture.

"Relationally. We talk to him about it. We hear him in real time."The lecture

The one path

Find the exact line

One right spouse, one right job, already known. Miss it and you are off the map.

The pasture

Pick, inside the fence

"This is green pastures. Have a pick." Head for the cliff, and "I'll talk to you about that."

The texts

"He makes me lie down in green pastures ... He guides me along the right paths" (Ps 23:2–3). "I will counsel you with my loving eye on you" (Ps 32:8). "In their hearts humans plan their course, but the Lord establishes their steps" (Prov 16:9). The last one is read by both sides, and it holds both halves: you plan, he steers.

Does he read my journal?

"God knows everything that can be known." But the lecture wonders whether he also "will honor your privacy a little bit," so that what you tell him means something. "Imagination is communion with God." Psalm 139 holds both: "Before a word is on my tongue you, Lord, know it completely" (v. 4), and still, "Search me, God, and know my heart" (v. 23).

Not a math book

"I don't know if God made the Bible to be a textbook for theology." It is "a relational, historical invitation into communion." The lecture's own summary of the night: "Sometimes we study God instead of talk to him." It added that the answer is to do both.

Where it strains

The pasture picture is good pastoral counsel on either view. The classical side does not teach "the one" either. Its standard answer is wisdom inside God's revealed will, which is the fence. The privacy idea is weaker. Psalm 139:4 and Jeremiah 17:10 ("I the Lord search the heart") are plain. The version that survives is the lecture's softer one: he knows, and he still wants to be told. Psalm 139 ends on exactly that invitation.

Read Exodus 3:1 to 4:17 in one sitting. Count Moses' refusals. Then read 4:14 slowly, and decide what "already on his way" does to "it wasn't plan A."

The lecture ran out of time before finishing the immutability texts and promised "a few more" next week. Bring two it hasn't touched: Numbers 23:19, which has the same shape as 1 Samuel 15:29, and Hebrews 6:17–18, where God swears an oath to show "the unchanging nature of his purpose." Ask whether the oath is what marks the things that cannot change.

If the Job claim caught your attention, read Job 1 and 2 before watching anything about it. Mark every line where God speaks.

Three questions worth asking in class: "Grudem says God feels and responds. Where exactly do you differ from him?" "Is 'before all ages' really weaker than 'before all time'?" "Revelation 14:11 is about people. How do you read its smoke?"
