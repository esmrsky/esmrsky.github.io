In charge, not in control

Lessons 1 and 2 put God inside time and drew a line through him: his nature never changes, his dealings with people do. Lessons 3 and 4 ask what that does to his rule. If God changes course, what does "sovereign" mean? Did he know? And what about the verses where he seems to steer a human heart?

I

The times God changed course

Lesson 3 opens with a stack of texts where God announces one thing and then does another. Its question for each: was the first word real? "Was he doing that to kind of manipulate them? ... I wasn't really going to anyways. I just wanted to threaten you. Is that what God's doing?"

"The accurate translation really is repent or change mind."The lecture, on Jonah 3:10

"Then the Lord relented and did not bring on his people the disaster he had threatened."Exodus 32:14

What moved him
Moses argued with him, and appealed to his reputation and his promise to Abraham (vv. 11–13). Lesson 2 used this verse for the same point.

"When God saw what they did and how they turned from their evil ways, he relented and did not bring on them the destruction he had threatened."Jonah 3:10

What moved him
Nineveh repented. The warning had no condition attached: "Forty more days and Nineveh will be overthrown" (3:4). The king still bet on one: "Who knows? God may yet relent" (3:9).

"So the Lord relented. 'This will not happen,' the Lord said."Amos 7:3, again at 7:6

What moved him
Amos prayed, twice: "Sovereign Lord, forgive! How can Jacob survive?" The third vision, the plumb line, gets no reprieve (7:8).

"Put your house in order, because you are going to die; you will not recover." Then: "I have heard your prayer and seen your tears; I will add fifteen years to your life."Isaiah 38:1, 5

What moved him
Hezekiah turned his face to the wall and wept. Both sentences open with "This is what the Lord says."

"I promised that members of your family would minister before me forever.' But now the Lord declares: 'Far be it from me! Those who honor me I will honor, but those who despise me will be disdained."1 Samuel 2:30

What moved him
Eli's sons treated the offerings with contempt and Eli let them. The lecture: "I did say this, I'm going to admit, I told you." A promise with "forever" in it was withdrawn.

"If that nation I warned repents of its evil, then I will relent ... and if it does evil in my sight ... then I will reconsider the good I had intended to do for it."Jeremiah 18:8, 10

What moved him
Nothing yet. This is the rule stated in advance, both ways: warnings can be withdrawn and blessings can be too. Section V comes back to it.

"The Lord regretted that he had made human beings on the earth, and his heart was deeply troubled."Genesis 6:6

The lecture's question
"Why experience regret if you 100% already saw that and knew that was coming?"

"How often they rebelled against him in the wilderness and grieved him in the wasteland! Again and again they put God to the test; they vexed the Holy One of Israel."Psalm 78:40–41

Alongside it
"Do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God" (Eph 4:30). The New Testament still treats God as someone people can hurt.

"How can I give you up, Ephraim? ... My heart is changed within me; all my compassion is aroused. I will not carry out my fierce anger ... For I am God, and not a man."Hosea 11:8–9

Worth noticing
The lecture did not use this one, and it may be the strongest. Here being God and not a man is the reason he relents. In 1 Samuel 15:29 the same contrast is the reason he does not: "he is not a human being, that he should change his mind."

"For a brief moment I abandoned you, but with deep compassion I will bring you back."Isaiah 54:7

What moved him
Compassion, by his own account. Anger is "for a moment"; kindness is "everlasting" (54:8).
The Hebrew wordnacham

Used of God in nearly every text above. Its range runs from regret to relief.

"Repented"KJV, ASV

The older wording. The lecture counted "10 or more" versions that use it.

"Relented"NIV, ESV, NASB

The lecture counted "28 plus," and said the softer word wins "because again, it's theologically jarring."

"Comforted"Gen 24:67

The same verb: "Isaac was comforted after his mother's death." Jacob "refused to be comforted" (37:35).

What the lecture draws from it

These are not threats God never meant. They are real announcements that real people changed. That fits Lesson 2's line: God's character does not move, his dealings do. It also fits a God who lives through the moment, which is Lesson 1's claim.

Where it strains

"Relented" is not a dodge. Nacham is not the usual word for human repentance from sin; that is shuv, "turn." "Repent" in modern English means turning from wrongdoing, which God never does, so "relent" is arguably the more accurate word. Even the Greek translation of Jonah 3:10 uses metanoeō, so the strong sense is ancient. The verb is strong either way. Case 31

1 Samuel 15 holds both in one chapter. "I regret that I have made Saul king" (v. 11), "he is not a human being, that he should change his mind" (v. 29), and "the Lord regretted that he had made Saul king" (v. 35). Any reading has to keep all three.

II

Sovereign: in charge, or in control?

"Sovereign means that since God created all things, he rules over all things." Both sides say that much. The lecture's whole move is one word swapped for another: "I am very intentionally using the word in charge rather than in control."

"I am in charge in my house but I don't control my children."The lecture, Lesson 4

In control

Every event is his decision

  • God plans each detail, including human choices.
  • Freedom means doing what you want, and what you want is part of the plan.
  • "God allowed it" covers every loss.
  • The lecture: "the majority of Christian theology is comfortable with saying God is fully in control and including controlling human will."
In charge

He rules; people are free inside it

  • He sets the house rules, the boundaries, and the end.
  • He does not decide when his children breathe or what they choose.
  • "Within that, I can guide them, and they can change my mind" (Lesson 2's picture).
  • Some things he has fixed and "no one's going to get in the way." Others he leaves open.

The texts both sides start from

"God, the blessed and only Ruler, the King of kings and Lord of lords" (1 Tim 6:15). "His kingdom rules over all" (Ps 103:19). "Our God is in heaven; he does whatever pleases him" (Ps 115:3; also 135:6). "No purpose of yours can be thwarted" (Job 42:2). "He does as he pleases with the powers of heaven and the peoples of the earth" (Dan 4:35). That last one is said by Nebuchadnezzar after God has humbled him; it is a king's confession, not a definition.

The lecture reads these as rule, not management. The open question is what "whatever pleases him" includes. If it pleases him to make free people, freedom is one of the things he does.

Where it strains

The other side has texts that sound like control, not charge, and the lecture did not take them on.

"Him who works out everything in conformity with the purpose of his will" (Eph 1:11). "In the Lord's hand the king's heart is a stream of water that he channels" (Prov 21:1). "The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the Lord" (Prov 16:33). "The Lord moved the heart of Cyrus" (Ezra 1:1). And the cross: "They did what your power and will had decided beforehand should happen" (Acts 4:28).

"In charge" survives these only if it allows God to steer some hearts at some times. That is a real concession, and it is where the next sections go. Case 26

III

Four models, one morning

"Tomorrow, this random girl Anna is going to have an opportunity to help her neighbor." All four views, the lecture says, "affirm that God rules and that people are responsible and that prayer matters." They differ on what God knows and when. Pick one.

The descriptions follow the lecture, tightened where it misspoke. Each view is given the way its own holders would put it.

God planned that Anna would help, and Anna helps because she wants to.

Held by Calvin's heirs: Westminster, most Reformed and Presbyterian churches.
What God knows

Everything, because he decreed everything. He knows the future the way an author knows the book.

How Anna is free

She does what she desires, with no one forcing her. Her desire is part of the plan. "Compatible" means both are true at once.

Why prayer matters

God ordains the prayer along with the answer. Prayer is one of the means by which the plan happens.

The hard question

If God decrees every choice, he decrees the evil ones. Holders answer that God ordains through secondary causes and is not the author of sin. Critics say that is a name, not an answer.

God knows Anna will help, but his knowing doesn't make her do it.

Held by Arminius, Wesley, and most Methodist, Pentecostal and Holiness churches. The lecture guessed it was "maybe the majority of people in the room."
What God knows

The whole future, exhaustively. Psalm 139:16 is the favorite text: "all the days ordained for me were written in your book before one of them came to be."

How Anna is free

She could do otherwise. God sees what she will choose, the way you might watch a replay without causing the play.

Why prayer matters

God knew the prayer would be prayed and took it into account. Answers are real responses to it.

The hard question

If the future is already known as certain, can it go any other way? And if God sees it and does not act, knowing becomes a kind of permitting.

God knows what Anna would freely do in every possible situation, and chose to make the world where she helps.

From Luis de Molina (1588). Today William Lane Craig is its best-known voice.
What God knows

Three things: what could happen, what would happen in any given setting, and what will. The middle one gives the view its name.

How Anna is free

She could do otherwise. God does not force her; he picks the circumstances in which she freely chooses. If she has time she helps one way, if she is rushed another.

Why prayer matters

God knew which prayers would be offered in which world, and chose a world that answers them.

The hard question

What makes "Anna would help" true before there is any Anna? Critics call this the grounding objection. Note: Lesson 3 described it as God working things out "as it's happening," with no exact foreknowledge. Molinism holds exhaustive foreknowledge, chosen before creation. Lesson 4 put that right.

Some of tomorrow is settled by God and some is open. If Anna's choice is truly free, it is not there to be known yet.

Named in Richard Rice's The Openness of God (1980), and in a 1994 book of the same name by Clark Pinnock and others.
What God knows

Everything that can be known: all of the past and present, every possibility, his own plans, and every heart. Free choices not yet made are possibilities, not facts.

How Anna is free

Fully. The future is "partly open and you can kind of create it with God."

Why prayer matters

It changes what happens. This is where the lecture's three tests from Lesson 2 point: partner with him.

The hard question

The detailed predictions. If free acts can't be known, how did Jesus know Peter would deny him? Section IV takes this up. Critics also ask whether a God who can be surprised can promise anything.

Where the lecture lands

It leans toward open theism and says so with care: "I'm not fully sure." The view's draw is that the relational God of the text and the God of theology are the same God: "let's actually have our experience of relational God match our theology."

Its most important line came when a student pressed the point. Whether God knew is "the part I am least adamant about trying to prove." What it holds firmly is that God really responds. Knowing and responding can come apart. That leaves room for the other three views, which all say he responds too.

Where it strains

The lecture said open theism alone allows "God genuinely being surprised." That is true. But Lesson 3's heaviest texts are about grief and relenting, not surprise. A student made exactly this point: you can see a spouse's death coming and still grieve when it comes. The lecture agreed it was "a very logical concept where you can know and then still be present emotionally."

So grief is the easier case for the other views and surprise is the harder one. Most of section I's texts are grief texts. Genesis 6:6 does not need open theism; Hosea 11 and Jeremiah 18 press harder.

IV

Then how did he call the rooster?

"The big hurdle with open theism is how does this explain biblical passages about the definite knowledge and detailed predictions." The lecture's answer: God knows his own plans, he knows the enemy's plans, and he knows every heart. "That's a lot of knowledge to be like, 'Oh, I could tell you what's going to happen without having to be in the future.'"

Test the answer against each text. The filter sorts them by what the lecture's answer would have to draw on.

"This very night, before the rooster crows, you will disown me three times."Matthew 26:34

Lecture
The enemy's plan: "Satan has asked to sift all of you as wheat. But I have prayed for you, Simon" (Luke 22:31–32).
Check
Satan's request explains the sifting, not the denial. The denial is Peter's own free act, three times, by morning. The better open-theist answer, which the lecture did not give, is that Jesus knew Peter's heart and the pressure he was walking into. That works if a known character can make a free act certain.

"I make known the end from the beginning ... I say, 'My purpose will stand, and I will do all that I please.'"Isaiah 46:10

Lecture
His own plan. What he decides to do, he can announce.
Check
Fits well. The text itself says it is about his purpose. So does "the plans of the Lord stand firm forever" (Ps 33:11).

"I am telling you now before it happens, so that when it does happen you will believe that I am who I am."John 13:19

Lecture
The cross was his plan: "the details of the cross that he was going to do? Well, that was his plans."
Check
Fits the cross. But the thing Jesus is predicting in the paragraph is a betrayal (13:18), which is a human choice.

"For Jesus had known from the beginning which of them did not believe and who would betray him."John 6:64

Lecture
Not discussed. The natural answer is a known heart.
Check
"From the beginning" is the hard phrase. If it means from the start of his ministry, a known heart fits. If it means before Judas chose, it is foreknowledge of a free act.

"... who says of Cyrus, 'He is my shepherd and will accomplish all that I please.'"Isaiah 44:28

Lecture
Not discussed.
Check
A named king about a century and a half ahead on the traditional dating, which means his parents' choices too. Open theists answer that God can bring about what he plans, including a person. Then the text is about steering, which is the very thing section II says God doesn't do. Ezra 1:1 adds: "the Lord moved the heart of Cyrus."

"A son named Josiah will be born to the house of David. On you he will sacrifice the priests of the high places."1 Kings 13:2

Lecture
Not discussed.
Check
About three centuries ahead on the traditional dating, with a name and a deed, and fulfilled in 2 Kings 23:15–16. Same shape as Cyrus, and the same answer costs the same thing. Case 30
V

The potter, twice

Romans 9 is the chapter every view has to answer for. The lecture reads it as one note in a chord: "you could talk about one aspect of God but it's not like all of God. It's like, hey, let me hit this note right now." Then it plays the other note, from Jeremiah's potter.

"Paul's hitting this note of like God can choose and do what he wants."The lecture, Lesson 4

Romans 9

The potter's right

  • "Before the twins were born or had done anything good or bad ... 'The older will serve the younger.'"Romans 9:11–12
  • "It does not, therefore, depend on human desire or effort, but on God's mercy."9:16
  • "Therefore God has mercy on whom he wants to have mercy, and he hardens whom he wants to harden."9:18
  • "Does not the potter have the right to make out of the same lump of clay some pottery for special purposes and some for common use?"9:21

The lecture's reading: true, and one side of God. "The short answer is both are possible."

Jeremiah 18

The potter's patience

  • "But the pot he was shaping from the clay was marred in his hands; so the potter formed it into another pot, shaping it as seemed best to him."Jeremiah 18:4
  • "Like clay in the hand of the potter, so are you in my hand, Israel."18:6
  • "If that nation I warned repents of its evil, then I will relent."18:8

The lecture's reading: the potter "would give it multiple goes," and only clay that "did not yield at all" was thrown out. The clay's response changes the potter's plan.

The lecture's case for the chord

Paul himself makes the hardening reversible two chapters later: "if they do not persist in unbelief, they will be grafted in, for God is able to graft them in again" (Rom 11:23). And Paul's potter is quoting older potters. Isaiah 29:16, 45:9 and 64:8 all use the image, and Jeremiah's is the one that explains how the potter works. So "Romans 9 alone" is not the whole of Paul's own argument.

Where it strains

"Multiple goes" is not in the text. Jeremiah 18:4 has one marred pot and one reshaping. The patience is a fair reading of 18:7–10, but the pottery detail is an illustration, not the verse.

Jeremiah's potter also breaks things. One chapter later: "just as this potter's jar is smashed and cannot be repaired" (19:11). The potter image cuts both ways in Jeremiah too.

Romans 9:11 and 16 shut out the clay's response on purpose. "Before the twins were born or had done anything good or bad." A reading that makes response the key has to explain why Paul says it isn't, at least here. Both readings have serious scholars behind them. Case 28

"Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated" (Romans 9:13, quoting Malachi 1:2–3)
Nations, not just twins

Genesis 25:23

"Two nations are in your womb ... the older will serve the younger." Malachi is speaking about Edom, the nation, centuries after the brothers died. That supports the lecture's reading that this is about roles in history, not each man's salvation.

"Hate" can mean "love less"

An idiom of comparison

Jacob loved Rachel more than Leah, and the next verse calls Leah "not loved" (Gen 29:30–31; the Hebrew is "hated"). Jesus says to "hate father and mother" (Luke 14:26); Matthew has "loves ... more than me" (10:37). Esau himself prospered: "I already have plenty, my brother" (Gen 33:9), and God gave him land (Deut 2:5).

Where it strains

Malachi goes on: "I have turned his hill country into a wasteland ... a people always under the wrath of the Lord" (1:3–4). For the nation of Edom, "hated" includes real judgment, not only a lesser blessing. The idiom softens the word; the context gives some of its weight back. Israel was still told, "Do not despise an Edomite" (Deut 23:7). Case 29

Three hands on Pharaoh's heart

Pharaoh is the standard example of God overriding a will, and Paul uses him (Rom 9:17–18). The lecture: "In the Pharaoh story, usually people want one to be true ... I think all of them are true. The question is which one happened first."

The lecture's reading

Pharaoh started it. He thought of himself as divine and had magicians who could match Moses. "Pharaoh's hardening his own heart." God, "fully knowing Pharaoh," knew how hard that heart would get, and in the end confirmed what Pharaoh had chosen: "God doesn't actually have to manipulate Pharaoh against his will." The order in the ledger supports this. The Philistines read it the same way centuries later: "Why do you harden your hearts as the Egyptians and Pharaoh did?" (1 Sam 6:6).

The same pattern, it says, explains Paul and Jonah. Saul was not forced; he was a zealot already "seeking God," and the blinding "was God like in his kindness showing him the real truth he was always seeking." Jonah ran because he already knew God's heart: "I knew that you are a gracious and compassionate God ... who relents from sending calamity" (Jonah 4:2).

Where it strains

Moses never hardens Pharaoh's heart. The lecture listed three agents: God, Pharaoh, and Moses. No verse says Moses did it. The third party in the text is the passive "became hard."

God announces the hardening before the first plague (4:21, 7:3). A sovereignty reader will say the order of the narration is not the order of the cause. The lecture's answer, that God foresaw a heart already set, fits the text but is not stated by it.

The magicians matched two plagues, not most. Blood (7:22) and frogs (8:7). At the gnats "they could not" and said, "This is the finger of God" (8:18–19).

Paul and Jonah were pressed hard. "It is hard for you to kick against the goads" (Acts 26:14); "God, who set me apart from my mother's womb" (Gal 1:15). A storm and a fish are strong persuasion. "Not forced" holds; "not overridden in any sense" is harder. Case 27

VII

Hear the heart, not just the words

The lecture's rule for hard language in the Bible came out of a marriage. "Theology sometimes picks language over the heart of the matter."

"I'm going to hear your heart. That's a win."The lecture, Lesson 4

The story it told

A question about a dress

Before a date, his wife asked whether she looked bad in what she was wearing. The words were about the dress. The heart under them, as he heard it, was: I feel discouraged, will you reject me, will you tell me you love me? He answered the heart. "You have conflict in close relationships because you hear the person's words, but you're not hearing their heart."

Applied to Scripture: "There are times where God sometimes uses extreme language in the Bible, but his heart is" what you have to listen for. That is how the lecture reads "Esau I hated" and the hardening texts. "How do you interpret someone's heart? Well, you have to actually lean in to know them."

Where it strains

The tool works for both sides. "Hear the heart, not the word" is exactly how the classical view reads "the Lord regretted": strong language for a God who did not really change. The lecture uses it on the hard texts; the other side uses it on the tender ones. It needs a rule for when the words are the heart.

"Logically no, but theoretically yes." The lecture said this of a God both outside time and leaving the future open, and compared it to Jesus being fully God and fully man. In Lesson 2 it called the timeless-yet-responsive view "illogical." The two don't sit together, and the comparison is weak: the church has never said the incarnation is a logical contradiction, only a mystery.

VIII

Why it matters on a Tuesday

Lesson 4 ends where Lesson 2 did: with a person. "There's a lot of promises of God in Scripture that I see a lot of humans not even coming near." If God controls everything, every unmet promise is his choice. If he is in charge, some of them are ours to reach for.

"This is not God's will for you, and God didn't even allow it."The lecture, to the man at the clinic

The story it told

Ten years in a waiting room

In a clinic waiting room, a man was on the floor in pain from a nerve condition he had lived with for ten years. "Why am I still here? I want to believe that God loves me." Every Christian had told him the same thing: "Well, God at least allowed it." The lecture told him the opposite, and the man's question back was, "What theology do you believe?"

The texts it reaches for: God's "very great and precious promises," which come "through our knowledge of him" (2 Pet 1:3–4). "My people are destroyed from lack of knowledge" (Hos 4:6). And God's will is not always done: the Pharisees "rejected God's purpose for themselves" (Luke 7:30); God "wants all people to be saved" (1 Tim 2:4), and not all are.

Where it strains

The other side has comfort too. "You intended to harm me, but God intended it for good" (Gen 50:20). "In all things God works for the good of those who love him" (Rom 8:28). Many suffering people have found more rest in "he is still in this" than in "this was never his will." Both sentences can be said to the same man, and the father-heart reading can hold them together: Jesus "went around doing good and healing all who were under the power of the devil" (Acts 10:38), and God still works good out of what he did not send.

Some promises wait. "They did not receive the things promised; they only saw them and welcomed them from a distance" (Heb 11:13). Unreceived is not always unreached for. Lesson 2's Job claim is the hard edge of "didn't even allow it." Case 23

The lecture's test from Lesson 2, applied to "in charge"
1

With him more

A God who can be grieved and moved is someone you talk to, not a decree you accept.

2

Like him more

A father who is in charge without controlling is the kind of parent the lecture is trying to be at home.

3

Partner with him

If outcomes are open, prayer and action change them. The sovereignty side would say they change them on its view too (Lesson 2, case 24).

IX

Before next week

Read Exodus 7 to 11 in one sitting with three colored pens. Mark every hardening by who does it. Then decide for yourself which came first.

Then read Romans 9, 10 and 11 as one letter, not three chapters. Note where 9:18 ("he hardens whom he wants to harden") stands by the end of 11:23 and 11:32 ("so that he may have mercy on them all").

Read Hosea 11 aloud. Few texts are more direct about God changing his mind, and this one says he does it because he is God.

Three questions worth asking in class: "If God won't override free will, what does 'the Lord moved the heart of Cyrus' mean?" "How does open theism explain Josiah, named three hundred years ahead?" "If grief doesn't need open theism, which text needs it most?"

Side reading for the strongest form of each view: Gregory Boyd, God of the Possible (2000), for open theism. Bruce Ware, God's Lesser Glory (2000), for the reply. Kenneth Keathley, Salvation and Sovereignty (2010), for Molinism. James Beilby and Paul Eddy, eds., Divine Foreknowledge: Four Views (2001), for all four side by side.
