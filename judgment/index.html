A word study — Greek · Hebrew · Slavonic · Russian

κρίνωJudgment

Μὴ κρίνετε, ἵνα μὴ κριθῆτε. “Do not judge, so that you are not judged.” Matthew 7:1 The only line anyone quotes
μὴ κρίνετε κατ’ ὄψιν, ἀλλὰ τὴν δικαίαν κρίσιν κρίνατε. “Do not judge by appearance — judge the righteous judgment.” John 7:24 Same verb. Now an order.

Two commands, one verb, thirty pages apart. The contradiction is not in the Bible. It was manufactured in English, by one word doing the work of nine — and it has been quietly deforming how people read, speak and think ever since.

01

Start with your hands, not a versePick the apple you would actually take home.

No stakes. No theology. Six apples on a crate. Take one.

You looked at six things, held them against a standard you never said out loud, separated the ones that met it from the ones that didn’t, and acted on the result. Ancient Greek has a verb for exactly that sequence. It is the first word on this page:

κρίνω krinō — to separate, to sift, to distinguish, to decide

It is the same verb, in the same form, in Matthew 7:1. Whatever “do not judge” means, it cannot mean “do not do the thing you just did at the fruit crate” — because six verses later the same chapter will tell you to inspect fruit and draw a conclusion from it.

Everyone does this a few thousand times a day — which route, which job, which friend, which doctor, which church, which apple. Then they say “I don’t judge people,” and mean it sincerely. Both things cannot be true. Something in the word broke. This page is about exactly where, exactly when, and exactly what it cost.

02

Where the word comes fromThree languages built this word from three different pictures.

Greek, Latin and Slavic each had to name the act of judging, and each reached for a different everyday scene to name it with. Two of them reached for the kitchen and the market. One reached for a courtroom. English inherited the courtroom — and only the courtroom.

Greek
κρίνω
krinō
“To put it through a sieve.”

From Proto-Indo-European *krey-, “to sift, to separate.” The picture is the threshing floor: grain thrown up, wind taking the chaff, the good falling back. Deciding is what is left over once you have separated. The courtroom sense is a later, narrower use of a farming word.

What grew out of it in English

Latin → English
iūdex
iūs + dīcere
“The one who speaks the law.”

Iūs is the law; dīcere is to pronounce. A iūdex is a person with standing to declare a legal outcome over someone else. That is where English gets judge — and the word arrives already wearing a robe, already holding a sentence, already implying that the judge is above the judged.

Slavic → Russian
судъ
sǫdŭ — sud
“A putting-together.”

From *som-dʰh₁-os: the prefix *som- (“together,” as in Latin com-) on the root *dʰeh₁-, “to place, to put.” A judgment is things set side by side so they can be compared. Same shape as сосуд — a vessel, a thing put together.

Built on the same stem
рассудокрассуждениеосуждениепредрассудоксосуд
Greek says sift. Slavic says compare. Latin says sentence. The New Testament was written in the sifting language and is mostly read in the sentencing one — so every neutral Greek sentence about separating wheat from chaff arrives in English already sounding like a man in a wig condemning someone. That single mismatch generates almost every confusion on this page.

There is a hard proof of the Greek picture, and it is not from a lexicon. Latin cernō — “to sift, to separate, to discern” — descends from the very same Indo-European root as κρίνω. From cernō Latin built crībrum, a literal sieve; and also certus (certain), discernō (discern), sēcernō (secret — what is sifted apart), dēcernō (decree) and crīmen (crime). To be certain is to have finished sifting. A secret is something separated out. A crisis is the moment the separating happens. Every one of those words is a judgment word wearing a disguise.

03

The familyNineteen words went into English as one.

This is the whole household. Greek did not need a debate about what kind of judging was meant, because the prefix said so before the sentence started. δια- is “apart.” ἀνα- is “all the way up.” κατα- is “down against.” Change the prefix and you change the morality of the act — and English, which has no live prefix system for this root, flattened all of it into the single syllable judge.

Un-sorting: making no distinction between persons. Listed among the marks of wisdom from above, right beside ἀνυπόκριτος — un-play-acted. Two κρίνω words, back to back, both negated.

Read the colours down the page. Every red word carries κατα-, προ- or ὑπο- — down-against, in-advance, from-under-a-mask. Every gold word carries δια- or ἀνα- — apart, thoroughly. The moral weight is not in the root. It never was. It is in the direction of travel, and English threw the direction away.

04

The chapter nobody finishesMatthew 7, read to the end.

“Judge not” is verse one. Almost nobody quoting it has read verse twenty. Here is the whole chapter in order, with one question asked of each movement: can you obey this without separating one thing from another?

7:1–2

Do not judge — and here is the reason.

The sentence does not stop at “judge not.” It runs on into why: “for with the verdict you pass, you will be judged, and with the measure you measure, it will be measured to you.” That is not a prohibition. It is a warning about which standard you are about to be held to.

You are not being told to drop the scales. You are being told whose neck they will hang around.
A rule for judging
7:3–5

The plank, the speck — and what happens after.

Everyone stops at “take the plank out of your own eye.” The verse does not stop there. It finishes: καὶ τότε διαβλέψεις ἐκβαλεῖν τὸ κάρφος — “and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother’s eye.”

The speck still comes out. Jesus is not cancelling the operation; he is telling the surgeon to clean his own eye first.
Sorting required
7:6

Do not give what is holy to dogs.

Five verses after “judge not,” an instruction that cannot be carried out at all without deciding, about specific people, that they are in a category you will not hand pearls to.

Obeying verse 6 requires the exact activity verse 1 supposedly banned.
Sorting required
7:13–14

Two gates, two roads, two destinations.

Wide and narrow. One leads to destruction and many take it. You are asked to look at two options and choose — which is what κρίνω means before it means anything else.

Sorting required
7:15

Watch out for false prophets.

Προσέχετε ἀπὸ τῶν ψευδοπροφητῶν — they arrive dressed as sheep. To obey this you must assess a teacher, disbelieve the costume, and reach a conclusion about a person who has not confessed anything.

Sorting required
7:16–20

By their fruits you will know them.

ἀπὸ τῶν καρπῶν αὐτῶν ἐπιγνώσεσθε αὐτούς. Grapes are not gathered from thorns. A good tree, a bad tree; good fruit, rotten fruit. Then the whole line is repeated in verse 20 in case it was missed.

Fifteen verses after “judge not,” the same speaker gives an inspection procedure — and it is the one you already used on the apples.
Sorting required
7:21–23

Not everyone who says “Lord, Lord.”

Words are explicitly declared insufficient evidence. Something else — doing the will of the Father — is the test. That is a criterion, stated as a criterion.

Sorting required
7:24–27

Two builders, two foundations.

The chapter closes as it has run: rock or sand, standing or fallen. The last word of the Sermon on the Mount is a comparison you are expected to make.

05

The oldest commentary is inside the BibleLuke already told us which word Jesus meant.

Matthew gives the saying bare: Μὴ κρίνετε. Luke gives the same saying with a second line attached — and the second line uses a different verb. Translators call this a doublet; what it actually is, is a definition.

Luke 6:37 — three commands, three verbs

Greek
καὶ μὴ κρίνετε, καὶ οὐ μὴ κριθῆτε· καὶ μὴ καταδικάζετε, καὶ οὐ μὴ καταδικασθῆτε. ἀπολύετε, καὶ ἀπολυθήσεσθε·
English
“Do not judge, and you will not be judged. Do not condemn, and you will not be condemned. Release, and you will be released.”
Синод.
Не судите, и не будете судимы; не осуждайте, и не будете осуждены; прощайте, и прощены будете;
Слав.
И҆ не сꙋди́те… (и҆) не ѡ҆сꙋжда́йте… ѿпꙋща́йте, и҆ ѿпꙋ́стѧтъ ва́мъ:
Luke sets κρίνω in parallel with καταδικάζω — “to pass sentence against” — and then with ἀπολύω, “to let go, to release.” The company a word keeps is what a word means. Judging here sits between condemning and releasing: it is the courtroom sense, the sense that ends in a penalty. Not the sense you used on the apples.

Romans 14:13 — and here it is settled outright

Greek
Μηκέτι οὖν ἀλλήλους κρίνωμεν, ἀλλὰ τοῦτο κρίνατε μᾶλλον, τὸ μὴ τιθέναι πρόσκομμα τῷ ἀδελφῷ…
English
“Let us no longer judge one another — but rather judge this: not to put a stumbling-block in a brother’s way.”
Слав.
Не ктомꙋ̀ ᲂу҆̀бо дрꙋ́гъ дрꙋ́га ѡ҆сꙋжда́емъ, но сїѐ па́че сꙋди́те…
Paul uses κρίνω twice in one sentence — forbidden, then commanded. In English that reads as a riddle. In Slavonic it does not, because the translator had a prefix and used it: the first becomes осуждаем (condemn), the second stays судите (judge). A ninth-century translator solved this in one word, and English readers have been arguing about it ever since.

And the verse that is never quoted

Greek
Τί δὲ καὶ ἀφ’ ἑαυτῶν οὐ κρίνετε τὸ δίκαιον;
English
“And why do you not judge for yourselves what is right?” Luke 12:57
НРП
Почему вы сами не рассудите, в чем истина?
Jesus rebukes a crowd for failing to judge. Same speaker, same gospel, five chapters after “judge not.” The modern Russian translator reached instinctively for рассудить — “to judge apart, to reason it out” — because Russian still has the prefix that English lost.

There are more. Paul tells the Corinthians κρίνατε ὑμεῖς ὅ φημι — “judge for yourselves what I am saying” 1 Cor 10:15 — inviting judgment on an apostle. James closes the Jerusalem Council with διὸ ἐγὼ κρίνω, “therefore I judge” Acts 15:19, and the whole Gentile mission turns on it. Paul asks whether the church really has nobody wise enough διακρῖναι between two brothers 1 Cor 6:5 — and he is not impressed.

06

Not a ban — a boundarySix things you genuinely may not judge.

The New Testament is not vague about this. It fences off particular objects of judgment and leaves the rest wide open. Once you see the fences, the apparent contradiction dissolves entirely — because none of these six is “deciding whether a thing is good.”

Fence 01

Motives you cannot see.

Not the act — the reason behind it. Paul refuses to do this even to himself. The machinery for reading hearts exists, and it is not issued to us.

μὴ πρὸ καιροῦ τι κρίνετε… ὃς καὶ φωτίσει τὰ κρυπτὰ τοῦ σκότους “Judge nothing before the time, until the Lord comes, who will bring to light what is hidden in darkness.” 1 Cor 4:5
Fence 02

Where someone finally stands.

Their ultimate standing before God is a case already assigned to another court. You may say “this is wrong.” You may not say “and therefore you are lost.”

σὺ τίς εἶ ὁ κρίνων ἀλλότριον οἰκέτην; “Who are you to judge someone else’s servant? To his own master he stands or falls.” Rom 14:4
Fence 03

By appearance.

The oldest and most consistent prohibition in Scripture — and the one modern life breaks most often. Faces, clothes, accents, follower counts. The Messiah himself is described as refusing this method.

μὴ κρίνετε κατ’ ὄψιν · וְלֹא־לְמַרְאֵה עֵינָיו יִשְׁפּוֹט “He shall not judge by what his eyes see.” John 7:24 · Isaiah 11:3 · 1 Samuel 16:7
Fence 04

Matters of preference.

Food, days, drink, custom. Romans 14 is an entire chapter forbidding judgment in the zone where Scripture has not spoken — and it is precisely the zone where religious people judge most confidently.

μὴ εἰς διακρίσεις διαλογισμῶν “Welcome him — but not to quarrel over opinions.” Rom 14:1
Fence 05

Outsiders.

Paul draws the line and it runs the opposite way to modern practice: the church’s judgment is for the inside of the house. The world is not your docket.

τί γάρ μοι τοὺς ἔξω κρίνειν; οὐχὶ τοὺς ἔσω ὑμεῖς κρίνετε; “What business is it of mine to judge outsiders? Is it not those inside whom you judge?” 1 Cor 5:12
Fence 06

Before you have heard it.

A verdict reached in advance is not a judgment at all — Greek has a separate word for it, and so do English and Russian. Proverbs calls answering first and listening second a public humiliation waiting to happen.

מֵשִׁיב דָּבָר בְּטֶרֶם יִשְׁמָע אִוֶּלֶת הִיא־לוֹ וּכְלִמָּה “To answer before listening — that is folly and shame.” Prov 18:13 · cf. 18:17 · 1 Tim 5:21

And four you are ordered to make.

Required 01

Teaching, and the people who bring it.

Weigh what is said. Weigh who is saying it. The Bereans did it to an apostle and were commended for it; the prophets in Corinth were to be sifted by the room.

οἱ ἄλλοι διακρινέτωσαν “Let the others weigh what is said.” 1 Cor 14:29 · Acts 17:11 · 1 John 4:1
Required 02

Fruit — visible, public, and over time.

Not a glance. Not a rumour. The pattern a life actually produces, which is the only evidence Jesus ever puts in your hands.

ἐκ γὰρ τοῦ καρποῦ τὸ δένδρον γινώσκεται “A tree is known by its fruit.” Matt 12:33 · Matt 7:16–20
Required 03

Yourself, first and hardest.

Self-examination is the one judgment that prevents judgment. It is also the step that converts a hypocrite into someone who can see well enough to help.

εἰ δὲ ἑαυτοὺς διεκρίνομεν, οὐκ ἂν ἐκρινόμεθα “If we judged ourselves, we would not be judged.” 1 Cor 11:31 · 2 Cor 13:5
Required 04

Everything, actually.

The broadest command on the subject is also the plainest, and it has no exemptions written into it. Test it all; keep what survives.

πάντα δὲ δοκιμάζετε, τὸ καλὸν κατέχετε “Test everything; hold fast what is good.” 1 Thess 5:21 · Rom 12:2
Notice the direction of travel. Judgment runs inward first — the plank, then the speck; yourself, then the table. Then it runs into the house — “those inside, you judge.” It is never issued as a licence to sort strangers. Modern religious practice has reversed both vectors exactly: fierce about outsiders, tender about insiders, and silent about the mirror.
07

Underneath the GreekIn Hebrew, to judge is to put something right.

The apostles thought in Hebrew and wrote in Greek. When they used κρίνω they were reaching for a Hebrew idea, because that is the word the Greek Old Testament had already used to translate it — and the Hebrew idea is not a courtroom at all. It is a repair.

שָׁפַט
shāphaṭ · H8199
To set right. To govern. To vindicate.

Used of a ruler who intervenes for someone who cannot get justice on their own. The emphasis falls on the outcome — the situation straightened — not on the pronouncement.

Septuagint renders it κρίνω.
מִשְׁפָּט
mishpāṭ · H4941
The right ordering of things.

Not “a judgment” in the sense of a ruling, but justice as a state of affairs: what each person is owed, actually delivered. This is what Micah says God requires of you.

Septuagint renders it κρίσις and κρίμα.
דִּין
dîn · H1777
To plead someone’s case.

To take up a cause on behalf of a person who has none. The verb is transitive toward the victim, not the offender: you judge for the poor, not against the rich.

Septuagint renders it κρίνω.

The Judges of Israel were not magistrates.

The book called Judges is שֹׁפְטִים — shophetim, the participle of shaphat. Gideon, Deborah, Samson. Not one of them presides over a trial. Read what the text says they did:

וַיָּקֶם יְהוָה שֹׁפְטִים וַיּוֹשִׁיעוּם מִיַּד שֹׁסֵיהֶם
“The LORD raised up judges, and they saved them from the hand of those who plundered them.” Judges 2:16

The verb translated saved is יָשַׁע, yāshaʿ — the root behind the name Yeshua. In the sentence that defines what a judge is, the job description is rescue.

And that is why creation sings about it.

If judgment were fundamentally a sentence, Psalm 96 would be unreadable. Instead the approach of the Judge is the climax of a song, and the natural world joins in:

“Let the heavens be glad, let the earth rejoice, let the sea roar… let the trees of the forest sing for joy before the LORD, for he comes — he comes to judge the earth.” Psalm 96:11–13

Nobody throws a party for a sentencing. They throw one when the person arriving is finally going to fix it. The Hebrew Bible’s emotional register around judgment is relief — and English readers, holding a courtroom word, cannot hear it.

דָּן דִּין־עָנִי וְאֶבְיוֹן אָז טוֹב הֲלוֹא־הִיא הַדַּעַת אֹתִי

“He judged the cause of the poor and needy — then it was well. Is this not to know me? declares the LORD.” Jeremiah 22:16

This is said of King Josiah, in contrast to his son, who built himself a cedar palace. Judging — dîn, pleading the case of someone with no leverage — is here identified outright with the knowledge of God. Not a consequence of it. It. Nothing in the modern sense of “being judgmental” survives contact with this sentence.

Two more, in the same key. בְּצֶדֶק תִּשְׁפֹּט עֲמִיתֶךָ — “in righteousness you shall judge your neighbour” Lev 19:15 — sits three verses above “love your neighbour as yourself.” Same paragraph, same neighbour. And מַצְדִּיק רָשָׁע וּמַרְשִׁיעַ צַדִּיק תּוֹעֲבַת יְהוָה גַּם־שְׁנֵיהֶם — “acquitting the guilty and condemning the innocent: the LORD detests them both” Prov 17:15. Failing to judge is not the safe half of that verse. It is the first half.

08

One of the nine gifts of the Spirit is a judging faculty. It is listed between prophecy and tongues without apology. A church that has decided judging is unspiritual has quietly deleted an item from its own list.

Рассуждение — the greatest virtue

The Eastern ascetic tradition never lost this distinction, because its working language never lost the prefix. In Cassian’s record of the desert fathers, διάκρισις — discretio, discernment — is argued to be the mother of all the virtues: the one that keeps every other virtue from tipping into excess. Zeal without it burns people. Mercy without it dissolves. It is not a cold faculty; it is the steering.

Осуждение — the characteristic sin

And in the same tradition, осуждение — condemning another person — is named repeatedly as a besetting spiritual danger, precisely because it counterfeits the virtue. It feels like discernment. It uses the same evidence. It arrives at a person instead of at a fact, and it exempts the one holding it. The two words are one prefix apart, and so, in practice, are the two acts.

09

The part that stings“Don’t judge” is a verdict.

Say it out loud and listen to the grammar. It is a ruling, handed down about a person, usually within seconds, usually without hearing the case, and usually with the speaker standing outside the standard they have just applied. Greek has a word for a verdict reached before the hearing. It is πρόκριμα.

Paul saw it coming.

Διὸ ἀναπολόγητος εἶ, ὦ ἄνθρωπε πᾶς ὁ κρίνων· ἐν ᾧ γὰρ κρίνεις τὸν ἕτερον, σεαυτὸν κατακρίνεις, τὰ γὰρ αὐτὰ πράσσεις ὁ κρίνων.

“You have no defence, whoever you are, when you judge — for in judging another you condemn yourself, because you, the judge, do the same things.” Romans 2:1

Read the clause Paul actually gives as the reason. Not because judging is wrong. Because you do the same things. Remove that clause and the argument collapses; most popular readings remove exactly that clause.

An honest inventory of one ordinary day.

You judged the weather and took a coat. You judged a route. You judged which emails deserved an answer. You judged a price as too high, a headline as unreliable, a restaurant as clean enough, a stranger on the platform as someone to stand nearer or further from. You judged a friend’s new partner within about four minutes.

Then you said — sincerely — “I try not to judge people.” That sentence is not humility. It is an inaccurate description of a life, and the inaccuracy is doing work: it keeps the judgments running while removing them from review.

Judgment you admit to can be corrected, argued with, apologised for and improved. Judgment you deny making cannot. The unexamined verdict is the only kind that never gets overturned — which is precisely why Scripture regulates judging instead of banning it. A ban would not have stopped anyone. It would only have driven the practice underground, where it now largely lives.

And there is a cost, paid by real people. When discernment is renamed as sin, the members of a community who go on discerning openly are the ones with the least scruple about it. Warning about a wolf gets you rebuked for judging; the wolf does not get rebuked at all. This is not a hypothetical failure mode — it is the failure mode Matthew 7:15 exists to prevent, and it is defeated by a misreading of Matthew 7:1 that lives fourteen verses above it. The instruction to spot false shepherds was given in the same breath as the warning about hypocritical judging, by the same speaker, to the same crowd.

10

The payoffNine things that went missing.

None of this is hidden knowledge or a fringe reading. It is all sitting in the plain text, in the lexicons, and in older translations. It went missing because one English word had to carry nine Greek ones, and the loudest of the nine won.

Judgment is a sieve before it is a gavel.

The root means to separate. The courtroom is one late, narrow application of a farming word. Every time you read “judge” in the New Testament, try “sort” first and see how many supposed contradictions evaporate.

Latin cernō, from the same root, gave us crībrum — a sieve — and also certain, discern, secret and crime.

Matthew 7 is a manual for judging, not a ban on it.

One warning about method, six instructions that require discrimination, zero prohibitions on assessment. The chapter ends by asking you to compare two builders.

καὶ τότε διαβλέψεις ἐκβαλεῖν τὸ κάρφος — “and then you will see clearly to remove the speck.” Matt 7:5

The final judgment is a light being switched on.

John does not describe a sentence being read. He describes illumination arriving, and people sorting themselves by what they do with it. The verdict is not imposed on the scene — it is the scene.

αὕτη δέ ἐστιν ἡ κρίσις, ὅτι τὸ φῶς ἐλήλυθεν εἰς τὸν κόσμον — “And this is the judgment: the light has come into the world.” John 3:19

Discernment is a trained muscle, not a temperament.

Hebrews says the mature have faculties gymnasium-trained by practice. Which means the ability to judge well is built by judging, badly, repeatedly, and being corrected — the exact process a blanket ban prevents.

τὰ αἰσθητήρια γεγυμνασμένα Heb 5:14

Self-judgment cancels judgment.

The one court where you are both defendant and judge is the court that keeps you out of the other one. This is stated as a mechanism, not as a piety.

εἰ δὲ ἑαυτοὺς διεκρίνομεν, οὐκ ἂν ἐκρινόμεθα — “If we judged ourselves, we would not be judged.” 1 Cor 11:31

You are being trained for a bench.

Paul’s argument for settling small disputes is that the people involved have a much larger docket coming. Whatever that means in full, it does not mean judgment is beneath a Christian.

οὐκ οἴδατε ὅτι ἀγγέλους κρινοῦμεν; — “Do you not know that we will judge angels?” 1 Cor 6:2–3

In Hebrew, to judge is to rescue.

The Judges saved people. Dîn means to plead the case of someone who has none. Jeremiah identifies doing it with knowing God. The entire emotional register is relief, not dread.

וַיָּקֶם יְהוָה שֹׁפְטִים וַיּוֹשִׁיעוּם — “The LORD raised up judges, and they saved them.” Judges 2:16

Refusing to judge is half of an abomination.

Proverbs names both failures in one line and gives them the same verdict. Letting the guilty walk is not the merciful side of the ledger; it is on the ledger, in the same column, as condemning the innocent.

מַצְדִּיק רָשָׁע וּמַרְשִׁיעַ צַדִּיק תּוֹעֲבַת יְהוָה גַּם־שְׁנֵיהֶם — “…the LORD detests them both.” Prov 17:15

The word for the bad judge is itself a judging word.

ὑποκριτής — one who answers from under a mask, a stage actor. Jesus does not accuse the man in Matthew 7 of judging. He accuses him of performing a judgment he has exempted himself from. The charge is theatre, not assessment.

The same root, one prefix away: ὑπο-κρίνομαι beside δια-κρίνω. Matt 7:5

Six questionsWhich of the nine words are you actually doing?

Hold one real situation in mind — a specific person, a specific thing you have concluded about them. Answer honestly rather than well. The result names the Greek word for the act you are performing, and the verse that governs it.

Question 1 — the object
What exactly am I ruling on?
Question 2 — the measure
Would I accept this same standard applied to me, today, by someone else?
Question 3 — the mirror
Have I dealt with my own version of this thing?
Question 4 — the jurisdiction
Is this person inside the house?
Question 5 — the domain
Is this a matter of righteousness, or of preference?
Question 6 — the aim
What do I actually want to happen next?

Where this comes fromSources, texts, and what is contested.

Greek text

Tischendorf’s Novum Testamentum Graece, 8th edition (1869–72), with Strong’s numbering. Where this edition prints συνκρίνω and ἐνκρῖναι, later critical editions print συγκρίνω and ἐγκρῖναι; the lemma forms given in the word list follow the standard spelling.

Hebrew text

Westminster Leningrad Codex, pointed, with Strong’s numbering. Proverbs is cited by Hebrew versification, which differs from the Synodal numbering in chapters 17–18.

Russian & Slavonic

Синодальный перевод; Новый русский перевод (НРП); and the Church Slavonic Bible of 1900. The Slavonic readings are quoted because they are the oldest surviving witness in this family to how the prefix problem was solved.

Septuagint correspondences

The Hebrew-to-Greek mappings — שָׁפַט → κρίνω, מִשְׁפָּט → κρίσις / κρίμα, דִּין → κρίνω, שֹׁפְטִים → κριταί — are taken from the lemmatised Septuagint text at the verses cited, not from a secondary list.

Etymology

PIE *krey- “to sift, separate” for both Greek κρίνω and Latin cernō (via Proto-Italic *krinō); Proto-Slavic *sǫdъ from *som-dʰh₁-os, “that which is placed together”; ὑποκριτής from ὑποκρίνομαι, “to answer, to play a part,” with the Attic sense stage actor.

What is contested

Two things, honestly. First, whether Matthew 7:1 forbids condemnation specifically or censorious judging more broadly — Luke’s parallel is strong evidence but not proof, and careful commentators differ. Second, сосуд: it is built on the same *sǫdъ element, though not every etymological dictionary treats the “vessel” and “court” senses as one word. Neither point changes the argument of this page, which rests on the prefixes.

One last thing worth saying plainly. Nothing here licenses being harsh. The same letters that command discernment command it in a spirit of gentleness, aiming at restoration, by people who have looked in a mirror first Gal 6:1. The argument is not that Christians should judge more. It is that they already do, constantly, and that pretending otherwise is what makes the judging bad.
