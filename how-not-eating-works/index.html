How Not Eating Works

Most of what feels complicated comes down to one loop, one backup fuel, and three minerals.

01 — THE LOOP

If you remember one thing

Why you're dizzy, why water isn't helping, why salt fixes it — all one chain.

  1. You stop eatingInsulin falls, and stays low.
  2. Kidneys stop holding sodiumInsulin is one of the signals telling them to keep it. Without it, sodium leaves in urine.
  3. Blood gets diluteSodium concentration drops.
  4. The water hormone switches offADH tracks concentration. Dilute blood reads as "dump water," so plain water runs straight through you.
  5. And takes more sodium with itEach turn of the loop deepens the deficit. Drinking more plain water accelerates it.

Salt is the valve. Not a supplement you add for completeness — the thing that lets water stay in you.

The same loop, read the other way. Clinicians who fast hypertensive patients describe this exact process as a benefit — the body shedding accumulated sodium, several pounds of fluid in the first days, blood pressure dropping with it. They aren't describing different physiology. They're describing the same mechanism with the opposite goal, in patients who are resting and monitored.

Which is worth holding onto: whether sodium loss is the point or the problem depends entirely on who you are and what you're doing while it happens.

02 — TIMELINE

What you're burning, by hour

Markers are approximate. Activity, body composition and your last meal shift them by many hours.

0–4 hFed

Insulin high. Burning the meal, storing the surplus.

4–16 hDrawdown

The liver releases stored glucose to hold blood sugar steady. Insulin falling. Sodium already leaving.

16–24 hSwitchover

Liver glycogen runs low. Fat oxidation ramps. First ketones appear.

The fast weight drop happens here. Glycogen binds roughly three grams of water per gram, so losing it dumps several pounds of water. Water, not fat.

24–48 hThe wall

Fat is primary fuel. Almost everyone feels worst here — headache, fog, weakness, dizziness standing, cold.

Most of that is the sodium deficit, not the absence of food. It's also where salt makes the most visible difference.

48–72 hSettled

Ketones supply a large share of the brain's fuel. Hunger usually drops off sharply and energy often returns — which is why day three frequently feels better than day two.

3–7 dDeepening

Muscle is spared more efficiently as the brain leans further on ketones. Electrolytes become the dominant variable — potassium and magnesium start to matter as much as sodium.

This is where a doctor should know what you're doing.

7–21 dClinical territory

Done in supervised settings with bloodwork. Sustained mineral depletion — particularly potassium and phosphate — can disturb heart rhythm.

~40 dThe documented edge

Humans have survived this with medical support and electrolyte replacement. People have also died attempting it without. Here because the question gets asked, not as a rung on a ladder.

What you do during a fast changes what you burn. Once glycogen is gone your body still needs some glucose, and it makes part of it by breaking down lean tissue. Activity raises that demand — so moving a lot during an extended fast means more weight lost and a larger share of it muscle. That's why supervised clinical fasting builds in rest as part of the protocol rather than as an afterthought.

This is genuinely contested. Others argue the opposite for shorter fasts: growth hormone is elevated, and resistance training late in a fasting window protects muscle rather than costing it. Both positions come from clinical experience, not head-to-head trials. The practical middle: light movement is fine at any length, hard training is a reasonable bet up to about 24 hours, and past 48 the case for taking it easy strengthens — not least because orthostatic dizziness makes a loaded barbell a bad idea whatever the metabolism is doing.

03 — FORMATS

The actual schedules

The physiology is identical at every length. What changes is how much of it you get, and how hard it is to keep doing.

12:12The baseline

Twelve hours overnight, achieved mostly by not eating in the three hours before bed. Nearly everyone can hold this, and it's the most commonly recommended daily floor — not dramatic, but it's the one that compounds.

16:8The usual entry point

Skip breakfast or skip dinner. Reaches the fuel switch most days. The best-studied window, tested in normal-weight adults, obese adults and even children.

18:6Tighter

Still sustainable for a lot of people, and a common step up once 16:8 feels easy.

20:4 / OMADDiminishing returns

Shorter is not automatically better. Windows of four to six hours commonly backfire, because people compress the same food — or more — into less time. An eight-hour window produces most of the documented benefit with far better adherence.

24–48 hPeriodic

Weekly or monthly rather than daily. A monthly 36-hour fast is a common recommendation for otherwise healthy people. Salt starts to matter here.

3–7 dExtended

Where everything in sections on minerals and refeeding stops being optional. One week once a year is a frequently suggested cadence for healthy people doing it preventively.

Rules that apply at every length

These matter more than which number you pick.

  1. Don't jump — taper inNarrow your eating window by about an hour a day over one to two weeks. Going straight from a 16-hour eating window to an 8-hour one is how people end up ravenous, irritable and quitting in four days. The hunger signalling systems need time to adjust.
  2. Regularity beats lengthA window that slides two hours later on weekends gives up a large share of the benefit, because the effect runs through circadian gene expression. A steady 8-hour window beats a wandering 6-hour one.
  3. Placement, then practicalityEarlier is metabolically better — a window ending mid-afternoon extends the sleep fast from both ends. It's also socially unliveable for most people. Roughly noon to 8pm is the usual compromise, and the window you'll actually keep beats the optimal one you won't.
  4. Guard both edges of sleepNothing in the first hour after waking, nothing in the two to three hours before bed. The fast that happens during sleep is the one doing most of the repair work.

Levers worth pulling

Small things with outsized effects on whether any of this works.

Best one
Walk after eatingTwenty to thirty minutes of easy walking after a meal dramatically shortens the time you spend metabolically fed. Sitting down after dinner can leave you in a fed state for five or six hours — meaning your "fast" started much later than your last bite did. Cheapest, most reliable lever here.
If muscle
Protein earlierEating protein early in the day appears to favor muscle building, and oddly this seems to hold regardless of when you actually train.
Upstream
SleepA single bad night measurably increases insulin resistance the next day. Sleep sits upstream of everything else in this guide — no fasting window compensates for chronically missing it.
Optional
A glucose monitorIf you're curious rather than anxious, a continuous monitor turns all of this from theory into your own data — which foods spike you, how long you actually take to come down, whether that walk does anything.

One lever to skip. Glucose-disposal agents — metformin, or berberine over the counter — get recommended as fasting mimetics, on the logic that they push the body toward the repair state without requiring you to skip meals. Taking berberine without carbohydrate on board can drop you hypoglycemic and produce a splitting headache; that warning comes from the same people recommending it. Whatever its merits, it isn't a beginner's tool, and a walk does a version of the same job with no downside.

04 — KETONES

The backup fuel

A correction worth making early: ketones aren't stored inside you waiting to be released. They're manufactured on demand.

01
The problemYour brain uses about a fifth of your energy and can't burn fat directly — fatty acids don't cross into it well. When glucose runs short, the brain has a supply problem fat alone can't solve.
02
The workaroundThe liver converts fatty acids into ketone bodies: small, water-soluble molecules that do cross into the brain. Fat, repackaged for delivery.
03
There are threeBeta-hydroxybutyrate (the main one, what a meter reads), acetoacetate, and acetone — acetone is volatile, which is where the distinctive fasting breath comes from.
04
What they're forWithout ketones you'd break down far more muscle to make glucose. More than anything else, ketones are a muscle-sparing mechanism. That's the evolutionary job.
Eating normally~0.1 mM
Ketogenic diet, well formulatedunder 1 mM
Prolonged water fast5–7 mM
Ketones a healthy adult can make per dayup to ~150 g

"Keto" and fasting reach the same state through different doors. Carb restriction keeps insulin low without removing food; fasting removes the food. Fasting gets you several times deeper into ketosis than a ketogenic diet does.

Why fasted people often report feeling sharper. There's a duller explanation than ketone magic, and it's probably the better one: eating refined carbohydrate spikes insulin, which drags blood sugar down afterward, and that trough is what fogs you. Fasting removes the oscillation entirely — glucose and insulin both sit flat. So the comparison isn't ketones versus normal, it's stable versus swinging. Which means the same clarity is available to someone who simply stops eating refined sugar, no fast required.

05 — MINERALS

Three that matter, in order

Ranked by how much difference each actually makes over a fast of a few days.

Barely matters under three days. Matters progressively more past that. Always measured, never eyeballed or free-poured.

Low looks likeHeavy full-body weakness · constipation · tingling · palpitations

Mostly sleep, twitches and cramps. Glycinate is the gentle form and is fine on an empty stomach.

The honest part: those three symptom lists overlap almost completely. You can't reliably tell which mineral you're short on by how you feel, and anyone who says otherwise is guessing confidently. That overlap is exactly why the method in the next section is dose-and-observe rather than diagnose-and-treat.

Also: if you take blood pressure medication — ACE inhibitors, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics — potassium supplementation carries real risk and belongs in a conversation with your doctor, not a guide.

Why some clinics give no electrolytes at all. Supervised programmes running long water-only fasts sometimes provide nothing but water. That isn't a competing view of the physiology — it's that they draw bloodwork, check patients twice a day, and end the fast if sodium or potassium crosses a threshold. They can skip supplementation because they're measuring it.

Fasting on your own, you have no instrument. Symptoms are the only readout you get, and they arrive late. That gap is the whole argument for dosing salt on a schedule — not that the clinicians are wrong, but that they can see something you can't.

06 — DELAYS

Why you wait thirty minutes

Nothing you swallow acts instantly. Knowing roughly how long each thing takes is what makes testing possible at all.

15–30 min
Salt in waterLeaves the stomach and reaches the blood quickly. If it's going to help, it helps inside half an hour.
30–60 min
CaffeinePeaks in this window. Tolerance drops while fasting, so the usual cup hits harder.
Fast / slow
PotassiumInto the blood quickly, into cells slowly. You won't feel it arrive the way you feel salt.
Hours–days
MagnesiumCumulative, not acute. A nightly habit, never a rescue for how you feel right now.
2–4 hrs
A mealTime to clear the stomach. Longer with fat, longer still on a gut that hasn't worked in days.

The test, in four steps

  1. A symptom shows up.
  2. Take one measured dose of salt in a full bottle of water.
  3. Wait 30 minutes. Don't dose again, don't stack anything on top.
  4. Better? It was sodium. No change? Stop dosing and eat. More salt won't fix something salt isn't causing.

Skip the wait and eat now if: you faint or nearly do, feel confused, notice an irregular or pounding heartbeat at rest, or tingling spreads or turns to numbness. Those aren't things to test — and don't drive.

Dizziness on standing deserves its own line, because supervised programmes name it as the single most common problem they watch for. It's usually salt-responsive. It's also a fall risk, which is why stairs, showers and driving are worth avoiding until it clears.

07 — COMING BACK

Reopening the gut

After days idle, enzyme output and motility both drop. A full normal plate on a cold-started gut is what causes the misery people blame on the fast itself.

Under 24 hours
Just eat

No ramp needed. Maybe skip the deep-fried feast, but there's no protocol here.

24–72 hours
One gentle first meal, then normal

Something liquid and warm, wait, then a modest real meal. Ordinary eating by the second or third meal.

3–7 days
Ramp across two days

Liquids, then soft foods, then normal portions. Rushing this is how people end up doubled over.

Over 7 days
Medically supervised refeeding

Not a home protocol. Bloodwork tracks phosphate, potassium and magnesium as insulin returns. Clinics running long water fasts budget roughly half the length of the fast for controlled refeeding — a 20-day fast gets 10 days of it, starting with liquids and introducing fiber last.

Liquid first
Opening hour
  • Clear broth of any kind — meat, vegetable, miso, dashi
  • Plain kefir or drinkable yogurt
  • Strained, warm, no solids
  • Then wait 30–60 minutes
Then soft
Rest of day one
  • Protein: eggs, fish, chicken, tofu, yogurt, soft cheese, well-cooked lentils
  • Carbs: congee, soft rice, potatoes, oatmeal, bananas
  • Veg: cooked rather than raw — sautéed, roasted, in soup
  • Fat: avocado, olive oil, modest amounts
  • Half portions before full ones
  • Salt freely — still rebuilding
Give it a day
Not meal one
  • Deep-fried anything, heavy oils
  • Large sugary items and sweet drinks
  • Big bowls of raw fibrous veg
  • Very large portions of anything
  • Alcohol
  • Coffee before real food

The principle underneath all of it: liquid before solid, small before large, cooked before raw, fat and fiber last. Any cuisine works — congee and miso do the job as well as broth and eggs. None of this is about which foods are virtuous; it's about texture, volume, and how much work something is to digest.

On "refeeding syndrome": real, but a different thing from day-one discomfort. It's a dangerous mineral shift when insulin returns after prolonged starvation, concentrated in long fasts, malnourishment, alcoholism and significant underweight. At two or three days in a well-nourished person it isn't the concern. The concern is just a sluggish gut meeting a big plate.

08 — WHAT CHANGES

Where the weight goes

The most common worry about extended fasting is muscle loss. The numbers are better than people expect, with a real caveat attached.

Total body weight≈ −10%
Total body fat≈ −20%
Visceral fat specifically≈ −40%
Lean tissue≈ −6%

Those are scan figures from roughly two-week supervised fasts, and lean tissue recovered fully within about six weeks of refeeding while fat loss continued. Read them carefully, though. They come from a clinic's own patients — resting, monitored, many with metabolic disease to begin with — not from a general-population trial. Someone active and already lean should expect a worse lean-tissue ratio than that.

Why visceral fat moves fastest is the interesting part. The fat around your organs is metabolically active in a way that fat under your skin isn't — it releases inflammatory signals continuously. It's also the first drawn on. That's the strongest single argument for extended fasting over ordinary calorie restriction, and it's reasonably well supported, though calorie restriction gets there too, more slowly.

What actually breaks a fast

Less absolute than people treat it. The real variable is whether something moves insulin, and that depends on context.

Doesn't break it
  • Water, sparkling or still
  • Plain tea
  • Black coffee
  • Caffeine on its own
  • Salt in water
Depends
  • A trivial amount of food — negligible deep in a fast, not negligible an hour after carbs
  • MCT oil — has calories but is ketogenic; opinions genuinely split
  • Bone broth — usually counts as breaking it
Breaks it
  • Anything with sugar
  • Juice, including fresh
  • Milk or cream in coffee
  • Any real quantity of food
09 — EVIDENCE

Solid, promising, oversold

Worth separating, because fasting content blends all three constantly — and the blending is what makes the topic feel confusing.

Solid
Textbook physiology, repeatedly measured
  • Insulin's role in how kidneys handle sodium
  • Glycogen binding water, and the rapid early weight loss from losing it
  • The glucose-to-fat switch, and ketone production in the liver from fatty acids
  • Ketones crossing into the brain and sparing muscle breakdown
  • Electrolyte deficiency symptoms, and how quickly sodium corrects them
  • Hyperinsulinemia preceding diabetes by years — high fasting insulin with normal blood sugar is a real and under-tested warning sign
  • Short-term safety at moderate lengths. In one study, healthy people water-fasted 72 hours and MRI showed no brain damage or dysfunction, with brain chemistry stable
Promising but unsettled
Real signal, important caveats
  • Ketones and cognition. A 2026 meta-analysis pooled 38 studies across roughly 1,600 participants and found a positive overall effect on cognitive measures. The catch: largely exogenous ketone supplements taken alongside normal carbohydrate — not fasting. Evidence about a molecule, not about skipping meals
  • Preferential loss of visceral over subcutaneous fat
  • Blood pressure reduction with extended fasting — consistent across clinic series, but those series mostly lack control groups
  • Gut microbiome shifts during and after fasting, including drops in some species associated with disease
  • Improvements in metabolic flexibility and short-term insulin sensitivity
  • BDNF rising with fasting. The measurement is real. The leap from "BDNF is elevated" to "you are smarter, your reflexes and vision are better" is not supported
Oversold
Where confidence outruns data
  • Specific hour thresholds for autophagy — the popular numbers come largely from rodent work; human timing isn't established
  • "72 hours reboots your immune system." Traces to one lab's work, largely in mice plus a small number of chemotherapy patients. The version where systemic stem cells "go anywhere and repair anything" is not what was shown
  • Regrowing pancreatic beta cells through fasting — that result was in mice, and gets repeated as though it were human
  • Fasting making you more creative. No human study demonstrating it
  • Menstruation as a detoxification route. Menstrual blood is not a clearance pathway — the liver and kidneys are. Under-fuelling can genuinely disrupt cycles, but that mechanism is invented
  • Fasting protocols timed to menstrual phase — plausible as a hypothesis, essentially untested in trials
  • Most longevity extrapolation from short human trials
10 — DISAGREEMENTS

Where the experts split

Listen to four fasting authorities and you'll hear four confident voices. They contradict each other on nearly every practical question — which is more useful to know than any single answer.

How long can you safely fast?
One view

40 days is the ceiling. Past that "everything gets more delicate" and electrolyte balance becomes precarious. Most patients fast 2–4 weeks; 40 days is 1% of them.

Another

72-day and 183-day supervised fasts described as successful interventions.

A four-fold disagreement between clinicians who both claim medical supervision. That range is the tell: nobody has trial data here, so the numbers come from individual practice and confidence.
Electrolytes, or water only?
One view

Half a teaspoon of salt in water, or a commercial electrolyte mix, once or more daily.

Another

Water only, nothing else — but with twice-daily monitoring, and the fast terminated if potassium falls below a set threshold.

This one resolves. The water-only position depends on a blood test you don't have at home. Without it, schedule the salt.
Rest, or train?
One view

Rest is part of the protocol. Activity forces glucose production from lean tissue, so you lose more weight and more of it is muscle.

Another

Train at the peak of the fasting window. Growth hormone is elevated, and resistance work preserves muscle.

Both are arguing from what they've seen in their own patients. Neither has a trial. Fast length probably explains much of the gap — the first is describing multi-week fasts, the second a daily window.
Is sodium the villain or the fix?
One view

Salt drives overeating by making food palatable, raises blood pressure, and shedding it is one of the main benefits of a fast.

Another

Salt stabilises blood volume, and replacing it is what stops the lightheadedness people mistake for hunger.

Both are right about different questions. The first is about habitual diet. The second is about the hours you're actually fasting. They aren't in conflict once you separate them.
Why do you feel sharper fasted?
One view

Ketones are a cleaner fuel, raising BDNF, growing new cells, improving focus and reflexes.

Another

It may not be ketones at all. Refined carbohydrate spikes insulin, which drops blood sugar afterward, and that trough is what impairs you. Fasting just removes the swing.

The second explanation is the more modest one, and it comes from a fasting advocate declining to take the credit on offer. That's usually a signal worth weighting.

Where they all agree

  • The switch from glucose to fat happens somewhere around 12–24 hours
  • Ketones are made by the liver from fat, not stored and released
  • Insulin is the hormone driving the whole system
  • Visceral fat is mobilised before subcutaneous fat
  • How you come off a fast matters as much as the fast
  • Extended fasting belongs under medical supervision

Four questions for any fasting claim

1. Mice or people? Much of the most exciting research is rodent work. Not worthless — just not the same claim.

2. Fasting, or a ketone supplement? The cognitive data is mostly supplements taken alongside food. If someone slides between the two, the argument has a hole.

3. One person, or a trial? A self-scanned brain before and after a fast is an anecdote with a picture attached. Imaging looks authoritative and varies between sessions for reasons unrelated to the intervention — sleep, hydration, caffeine, time of day.

4. What's being sold? Clinic, book, supplement, or an episode sponsored by a ketone company. It doesn't make anyone wrong. It's just the frame, and it's usually disclosed if you listen for it.

None of which means fasting does nothing. The metabolic switch is real, the safety data at moderate durations is reassuring, and enough people report feeling clear on day three that it's worth taking seriously as an experience. It's the mechanism claims bolted onto that experience — this hour, this process, this benefit — that outrun what's been shown.

11 — BEYOND FASTING

Reading health claims generally

The same calibration problem, applied past fasting — because the genre runs on the same machinery whatever the topic.

The filter

From a practising physician whose whole beat is auditing this space.

  1. New studies get positioned, not crownedMedia treats a fresh result as the answer. The correct move is to place it against everything already known and see whether it shifts the picture slightly or overturns it. Usually slightly.
  2. Real science tries to break its own ideaA researcher with a hypothesis sets out to disprove it and invites people who disagree. Social media selects for the opposite — for whoever most confidently supports what they already believed.
  3. Extremes are almost never rightThe body runs on homeostasis. Anything can be overdone: water will throw off your electrolytes, carrots will turn you orange, antioxidants turn pro-inflammatory in excess. The enemy of balance is bad — but also perfect.
  4. Watch the gray zoneWherever science genuinely lacks an answer is exactly where confident claims sell best, because nothing can contradict them yet. Thirty years ago that zone was weight loss, and the ads were fat burners and grapefruit diets. Today it's longevity and anti-aging.
  5. Mild-sounding wrong ideas travel furthestObvious nonsense gets rejected. A claim that sounds reasonable and is only slightly off spreads unchecked and does more cumulative damage — the same reason a less lethal virus can kill more people than a more lethal one.

And the quiet one: the people most susceptible to health misinformation are often those who are excellent at business. The instincts that work there — find the leverage, find the shortcut, optimise hard — map badly onto a body that wants equilibrium. There is no hack that substitutes for the boring inputs.

What's actually well established

The unglamorous list. It's short, which is the point.

150 min
Moderate exercise weeklyTwo and a half hours. Moderate means you can manage about a sentence at a time, not a conversation. That's the whole target.
Not weight
What exercise is actually forWeight change comes from nutrition, not the gym — a cinema popcorn, candy and soda clears a thousand calories, more than most people can burn in a session. You can't out-exercise a bad diet. What exercise does deliver is mood, strength, social connection, lower cancer risk, longevity. If you could bottle its effects and sell them you'd be the richest person alive.
Upstream
SleepSits above everything else here. One bad night measurably raises insulin resistance the next day, and no protocol compensates for missing it chronically.
Both halves
Calories and nutrientsCalories in and out is true, and unglamorous, and insufficient on its own. Three grapefruits a day will lose you weight and wreck your nutrition. Track both or you end up solving one problem by creating another.
Good ≠ perfect
Stop optimisingChasing perfect produces diminishing physical returns and generates anxiety that is itself a health cost. Perfect isn't just an illusion here, it's a toxic one.

On supplements

Worth its own treatment, because it's where most of this content eventually lands.

The regulatory gap
  • Not reviewed by the FDA the way medications are
  • Independent testing repeatedly finds products missing ingredients listed on the label
  • Or containing five to ten times the stated amount
  • You cannot verify what's in the bottle
Real downsides
  • Fat-soluble vitamins accumulate — A in particular can do genuine harm at high doses
  • B vitamins turn pro-inflammatory in excess
  • Interactions with prescribed medication are common and under-discussed
  • "No side effects" is never true of anything
The subtle cost
  • They let you feel the problem is handled
  • So you skip the thing that would have worked
  • Nobody sells that part, because it can't be sold
  • The case above comes from someone who could make a fortune selling them and doesn't

None of which makes every supplement useless. Correcting a documented deficiency is ordinary medicine. The claim being made here is narrower and harder to argue with: absent a clear indication, the benefit is unproven, the contents are unverified, the harms are real, and the opportunity cost is the part nobody mentions.

General physiology, not medical advice. Individual variation is large, and medication, kidney function, blood pressure and heart conditions all change the picture substantially.

Anything past about three days is worth doing with a doctor's knowledge rather than in spite of it.
