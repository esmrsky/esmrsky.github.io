A map, not a ranking

This guide describes changing capacity, not changing worth. Use it to name the kind of formation a life-domain may need — never to assign a person a spiritual caste.

Given whole

Standing is not earned

Grace, belonging, and adoption are not prizes waiting at the end. The map concerns lived capacity: how steadily a person can receive, process, and express what has already been given.

Domain-specific

One life holds several seasons

A person may carry deep peace in prayer while facing Egypt-like crisis in finances or Wilderness-level rebuilding in relationships. There is no honest single score.

Nonlinear

Growth circles and revisits

Transitions include plateaus, losses, sudden insight, and old patterns resurfacing under pressure. Revisiting an earlier strategy is data for care, not proof of lost identity.

01 · The tension

Ladder, or unveiling?

Every teacher of maturity stands somewhere on one question. Answer it wrong and everything downstream bends.

Position A

Maturity is climbed

There are developmental differences. Children become young men become fathers; practice and testing enlarge what can be carried. Classical mysticism, some apostolic traditions, and developmental readings of Hebrews all lean this way — though none describes precisely the same ladder.

vs
Position B

Maturity is unveiled

Nothing is climbed, because everything was given at the start. You are as righteous, as loved, and as complete on day one as you will ever be. What changes is not your position but your awareness of it. Grace theology lives here — and calls the ladder a subtle return to works.

The honest answer may be both: position given whole, capacity grown slowly. The New Testament refuses to drop either thread — and every framework below is an argument about which thread leads.

02 · The language

Scripture uses developmental language

One influential reading hears John address a single church in three registers — children, young men, and fathers. The text supplies a pastoral vocabulary, not a complete institutional curriculum.

I

Little children

1 John 2:12–13
"Your sins are forgiven… you know the Father."

Defined by what they've received, not what they've done. Forgiveness and belonging — the two things a child must know before anything else is possible.

II

Young men

1 John 2:13–14
"You are strong… and you have overcome the evil one."

Defined by conflict. The word is no longer external instruction but internal structure — and it has been tested against something and held.

III

Fathers

1 John 2:13–14
"You know him who is from the beginning."

Defined by intimacy — the same phrase repeated twice, as if nothing else needs adding. Not more victory. Deeper acquaintance, and others growing in their shade.

1 Cor 3:1–3

Paul withholds solid food from believers who could not receive it — capacity, not favoritism, sets the menu. Jealousy and strife are the diagnostic of infancy.

Heb 5:12–14

Maturity has a mechanism: "powers of discernment trained by constant practice." Not conferred. Trained.

Matt 9:17

The new-wine image is often mapped onto readiness: a container may need to change to hold unfamiliar content. That is a theological analogy, not the parable's only accepted meaning.

Titus 1:15

Within this framework, "to the pure, all things are pure" pictures an internalized purity that can engage complexity without relying only on external taboo.

Eph 4:13–14

The endpoint is stability: "no longer children, tossed to and fro." Maturity is measured against the fullness of Christ, not other believers.

03 · The map

Egypt, wilderness, land

Christian teachers have long read Israel's exodus as a typological map of deliverance, formation, and stewardship. Used carefully, the pattern can illuminate more than one domain of a life.

Fig. 01 — The Exodus Arc One night out · Forty years through
Territory I

Egypt

Bondage that feeds you. The system is cruel but predictable: bricks, quotas, leeks and onions. Nothing is yours — but nothing is asked of your faith either. Exit costs one night, and a death passing over.

Territory II

Wilderness

Free, but unformed. Daily manna, no storehouses; guidance by cloud, not map. Where identity is tested and Egypt's thinking is starved out. Its danger is nostalgia — the freed slave who misses the menu.

Territory III

The Land

Inheritance — cities you didn't build, vineyards you didn't plant. The gift still has to be inhabited and cultivated: giants remain, and every believer holds a map larger than their present stewardship.

God took Israel out of Egypt in one night. Taking Egypt out of Israel took forty years.

The wilderness holds consequence, testing, provision — and formation
04 · The domains

One life, several territories

Spiritual identity, relationships, work, money, health, and leadership rarely mature in lockstep. The map is most useful when applied locally — never as one total score for a whole person.

FinancesUrgent stabilizationRebuilding habitsGenerative provision

This person is not “Stage II.” They are receiving, rebuilding, and stewarding in different places at once. Pastoral care should meet the actual domain rather than flattening the person.

When a domain resembles Egypt

Seek deliverance and safety

Name the bondage, reduce immediate harm, receive concrete help, and establish belonging before demanding complex performance.

When a domain resembles Wilderness

Practice daily formation

Ask which old strategy has stopped working. Build small rhythms, remain in community, and let testing reveal what still depends on fear.

When a domain resembles the Land

Cultivate entrusted fruit

Turn freedom outward through sustainable stewardship, shared authority, rest, and investment in the growth of others.

05 · The model

Stages as capacity

Not ranks — capacities. What each season can receive, what it can carry, which strategy eventually stops working, and what begins to emerge on the far side.

Transitions often begin when a formerly useful strategy can no longer produce life. Classical mystics call some of these passages “dark nights.” This guide treats breakdown-before-integration as a recurring pattern, not a requirement for every person or crossing.

I

The Child

Egypt-exit · Little children
Can receive

Forgiveness, belonging, milk — truth pre-digested by others. Clear commands, external structure. Love that asks nothing back yet.

Can carry

Simple obedience. Dependence without shame. Enthusiasm that hasn't yet met resistance.

What stops working

Borrowed faith as a permanent home. Rule-keeping cannot quiet shame, and rigid structure cannot answer every ambiguity.

What begins emerging

"You know the Father." Relationship becomes firsthand; belonging can survive questions that once felt dangerous.

II

The Young Man

Wilderness · Young men
Can receive

Testing. Solid food — truth that must be chewed. The word as internal structure ("abides in you"), not external rule.

Can carry

Conflict and resistance. Daily dependence without visible reserves. Delay between promise and possession.

What stops working

Striving, giftedness, and victory as self-proof. Even successful strategies become brittle when identity still depends on winning.

What begins emerging

"You have overcome." Identity settles. Conflict becomes service rather than self-definition, and surrender can replace control.

III

The Father

The Land · Fathers
Can receive

Inheritance — provision beyond effort. Intimacy without agenda. Freedom the earlier stages couldn't hold: to the pure, all things are pure.

Can carry

Ambiguity, authority, other people's growth. Rest and warfare in the same season. The weight of territory not yet taken.

What stops working

Possession as a private achievement. Ruling instead of fathering, protecting legacy, and treating limitless responsibility as faithfulness.

What begins emerging

Generativity. Maturity turns outward through shared authority, durable structures, rest, and the growth of others.

A proposed fourth stage

Stewardship beyond the ladder

One research synthesis separates an “Integrated-Apostolic” stage from generative fatherhood: rare capacity for systemic vision, selfless governance, and costly public stewardship. The evidence is thinner here, and the language can easily sanctify status. This guide therefore treats it as a possible mature expression within the Land — never as a higher class of believer, and never as authority proved by title, charisma, or institutional rank.

06 · Drift & return

When formation bends sideways

A believer can keep moving publicly while becoming stagnant inwardly. The darker pattern is not simply “less maturity.” It is substitution: the form remains, but something secondary quietly takes the place of first love.

01 · First love

Communion

Being known and loved by God before trying to produce anything for God.

02 · Response

Obedience

Practice grows from relationship instead of bargaining, fear, or image management.

03 · Integration

Integrity

Public role and private life increasingly tell the same truth.

04 · Overflow

Fruit

Love, truth, justice, self-control, and generative care become visible over time.

Where the thread breaks

A good practice, role, gift, or insight detaches from living communion. The person keeps the motion because it still supplies control, belonging, certainty, appetite, or admiration.

Drift 01 · Motion

Religious autopilot

Recognize it: Service continues, but tenderness disappears. Prayer becomes a task, worship becomes a set, and disruption produces irritation rather than dependence.

The substitute: Motion, control, and reputation take the place of communion.

The return: Remember what love was like before the machinery took over. Simplify, tell the truth, recover hidden prayer, and let some activity stop.

Revelation 2:2–5 · Matthew 23:25–28 · Luke 10:38–42
Drift 02 · Visibility

Platform outruns formation

Recognize it: Gift and access expand faster than character. Admiration is read as approval from God; people close to the leader quietly absorb the cost.

The substitute: Influence and “anointing” take the place of integrity and accountable love.

The return: Step out of visibility when necessary. Invite truthful oversight, accept limits and consequences, and let character recover without a deadline for returning to platform.

1 Corinthians 13:1–3 · 1 Timothy 4:16 · Matthew 7:21–23
Drift 03 · Belonging

The Sunday self

Recognize it: Faith activates in the room but has little authority over ordinary decisions, money, speech, sexuality, repair, or private attention.

The substitute: Christian belonging, language, and respectability take the place of knowing and following Christ.

The return: Begin with small unseen obedience. Bring faith into weekday choices, honest relationships, and practices no audience rewards.

James 1:22–25 · Matthew 7:21 · Titus 1:16
Drift 04 · Certainty

Spiritual side-quest fixation

Recognize it: Every difficulty becomes an altar, demon, bloodline, prophetic puzzle, or hidden conspiracy. Attention narrows while love, patience, teachability, and ordinary responsibility shrink.

The substitute: Secret knowledge, power, and certainty take the place of holding fast to Christ.

The return: Test the fixation by its fruit. Widen the circle of counsel, distinguish discernment from obsession, and re-center on Jesus rather than the thrill of decoding.

Colossians 2:18–19 · 1 John 4:1 · Galatians 5:22–23
Drift 05 · Mastery

Knowledge without knowing

Recognize it: The vocabulary is advanced, the positions are correct, and contempt for “less mature” people keeps growing. Information no longer produces wonder, repentance, or love.

The substitute: Being right and feeling initiated take the place of relationship, humility, and practiced truth.

The return: Obey what is already known. Listen before correcting, welcome interruption, and measure insight by the love it makes possible.

John 5:39–40 · 1 Corinthians 8:1–3 · James 3:13–18
Drift 06 · Secrecy

Gift without integrity

Recognize it: Public ministry and private life split apart. An affair, financial deception, coercion, or another hidden betrayal is rationalized because the gift still “works.”

The substitute: Image, appetite, entitlement, and continued access take the place of truth and wholeness.

The return: Stop the harm, bring what is hidden into appropriate light, accept consequences, protect those affected, and pursue restitution. Restoration to God is not a shortcut back to leadership.

Psalm 51:6 · Ephesians 5:11–13 · 1 Timothy 3:2–7
The thread underneath

Displaced love

The “idol” is often not an obviously evil thing. Ministry, purity, insight, belonging, spiritual power, impact, or rest becomes an altar when it must supply identity, safety, or worth. The common return is not fresh hype. It is a movement back into truth.

RememberFirst love
RevealLeave secrecy
RepentChange direction
RepairAccept the cost
RemainRebuild slowly

Use this mirror on yourself first. One sign proves nothing, and fatigue, doubt, disability, or a hard season is not hypocrisy. Look for sustained patterns: secrecy, harm, contempt for correction, public–private fracture, and fruit deteriorating over time. When abuse or safety is involved, protection and qualified help come before reputation or rapid restoration.

07 · The voices

Six traditions, one question

Each takes the same raw material — the stage texts, the exodus arc — and builds something different. Where they conflict is more instructive than where they agree.

Classical mysticism

Purgative · Illuminative · Unitive

The oldest formal map: purgation strips attachment, illumination reorders love, union rests in God. John of the Cross describes dark nights in which an earlier way of relating to God fails and is purified. Growth may feel less like graduation than relinquishment.

Strong stages · experiential

Apostolic traditions

African Initiated churches · Western networks

“Apostolic” names more than one stream. Selected African Initiated Churches organize prophetic vocation, communal discipline, and authority through formal offices; some Western networks emphasize spiritual fathering and governmental authority. They should not be collapsed into one tradition. Their shared warning is the same: office can witness capacity, but cannot prove maturity.

Strong stages · structural

Identity teaching

Mohler · White · Sonship

Maturity is subtraction, not accumulation: losing sin-consciousness, unlearning the orphan mindset, agreeing with who you already are in Christ. You don't climb toward sonship — you wake up inside it. Stages exist only as degrees of agreement with a finished identity.

Soft stages · awareness

Grace theology

Paul Ellis · Finished work

The counter-position. You are as righteous on day one as you'll ever be; growing in grace means growing in the knowledge of grace, never in its possession. Tiered maturity, on this reading, smuggles works back in — growth is real, but it's fruit, never rank.

Anti-ladder · positional

Developmental psychology

Fowler · Stages of Faith

The academic outsider: faith develops through changing structures of meaning — conventional belonging, critical individuation, then a conjunctive faith that holds paradox. Descriptive rather than prescriptive, Fowler's model helps explain why some transitions feel like losing faith before reconstructing it.

Strong stages · cognitive

The stage texts themselves

John · Paul · Hebrews

Scripture uses developmental images — children, young men, fathers; milk and solid food; trained senses — without combining them into one formal curriculum. They work most safely as pastoral diagnosis of readiness, not as titles or institutional rank.

Stages as diagnosis

Where they agree

  • Change over time is real and expected — no tradition celebrates permanent infancy
  • Formation involves renewal, unlearning, practice, or deeper surrender
  • Readiness affects how teaching, freedom, and responsibility are received
  • Maturity becomes visible in stable love, discernment, and generative care
  • Many transition models describe loss before integration

Where they conflict

  • Is the ladder positional (grace: no) or experiential (mystics: yes)?
  • Do stages confer authority over others, or only capacity within oneself?
  • Is striving a necessary season — or always a counterfeit?
  • Can stages be skipped, or is the wilderness non-negotiable?
  • Who verifies a stage: the self, the fruit, or the fathers?
  • Is there one journey, or a different territory in every life-domain?
08 · The brain

New wine, old wineskins, real neurons

The maturity teachers all claim the mind as the mechanism. Some of that is established science, some is honest analogy, and some outruns the evidence. Every claim below is graded.

Experience can reshape the brain

Neuroplasticity across the lifespan is well established, though its mechanisms and limits vary by age, person, and context. Whatever else “renewing the mind” means, lasting learning has a physical substrate.1

Established

Repetition supports automaticity

Repeated behavior can become less dependent on deliberate attention. Connecting this habit research to the “trained senses” of Hebrews 5 is illuminating, but the biblical application remains interpretive.2

Science + analogy

Working memory constrains new learning

Novel information meets limited working-memory capacity, while prior knowledge helps organize complexity. Applying that instructional principle to milk and solid food is a pedagogical analogy — not neurological proof of spiritual stages.3

Science + analogy

Old frameworks resist new paradigms

Schema theory holds that we assimilate new information into existing mental structures — truly incompatible input forces either restructuring or rejection. Reading "old wineskins" as entrenched schemas is a fair mapping. But it is a mapping, not a discovery.

Analogy

Some memories can be updated after retrieval

Human reconsolidation research suggests that retrieved memories may become modifiable before being stored again. The boundary conditions and clinical implications remain active questions; applying the process to spiritual transformation is a hypothesis.4

Promising

Romans 12:2 describes neuroplasticity

The renewed mind and the plastic brain rhyme beautifully — but Paul is making a theological claim about transformation, not a neurological one. Treat the pairing as illumination, not proof: the science can't confirm the theology, and the theology doesn't need it to.

Analogy

Certain truths are meant for certain brains

Developmental readiness is real in children, and expertise research shows perception itself changes with mastery. But the strong claim — that specific revelation is neurologically gated to specific stages — goes well past anything measured. Hold it loosely.

Speculative
Established — well-supported science Science + analogy — evidence, then interpretation Promising — active research Analogy — fair mapping Speculative — beyond data
09 · Open tensions

What no framework resolves

The honest ending is a set of live questions. Any teaching on maturity that closes all of these is selling something.

01

Can the wilderness be skipped?

Grace says the work is finished; the exodus says formation takes forty years. If both are true, what exactly is the wilderness forming — position, or the capacity to inhabit it?

02

Is the journey one-way?

Israel voted to return to Egypt more than once. Whether a believer can regress a full territory — or only visit old thinking from new ground — divides every tradition with an opinion.

03

What happens when the domains disagree?

A person may show mature theological understanding and immature relational patterns, or deep love alongside financial crisis. Communities need care specific enough to name the domain without reducing the person to it.

04

Does maturity confer authority over others?

Some apostolic streams build structure on that claim; grace theology hears the makings of spiritual caste. The stage texts describe capacity and intimacy more clearly than institutional entitlement.

05

Who measures the stage?

Self-assessment inflates, fruit takes years, and leaders can be wrong. Every framework needs a verification story, and none has a clean one.

06

What if regulation is neurologically different?

Trauma, autism, ADHD, brain injury, chronic illness, and disability can shape attention, affect, communication, and stress response. A stage model that reads those differences as spiritual inferiority becomes harmful.

07

Does grace collapse the ladder — or fund the climb?

The deepest tension of all. If identity is given whole, stages can only be the unfolding of a gift; if stages are real, grace must be what makes the crossing survivable. The traditions have not finished this argument — and neither has this page.

Do not use this map to…

  • Assign a permanent level to a whole person
  • Diagnose trauma, neurodivergence, disability, or mental illness
  • Turn spiritual office, gifts, confidence, or vocabulary into proof of maturity
  • Demand submission by claiming access to a higher stage
  • Withhold belonging, dignity, or care until someone “advances”
10 · Notes & sources

What is evidence, what is interpretation

This page is a synthesis, not a claim that theology can be proved by neuroscience or that several traditions teach the same system. Its confidence labels grade the claim being made — not the worth of a tradition.

Biblical text

Primary language

Children, milk, solid food, trained senses, exodus, and inheritance come from Scripture. Combining them into one developmental map is an interpretive act.

Tradition

Distinct voices

Grace theology, classical mysticism, Fowler, African Initiated Churches, and Western apostolic networks overlap without becoming interchangeable.

Science

Evidence before analogy

A brain finding may be established while its pairing with a biblical image remains analogy. The theological claim is not upgraded by borrowing scientific vocabulary.
