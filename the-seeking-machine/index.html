The Seeking Machine Field guide · 2026 · Every claim graded

Why nobody can sit still

You were never chasing the thing. You were chasing the gap.

Restlessness is not a character flaw, and it is not one mechanism. It is four, stacked: a neuron that reports surprise, a schedule that exploits it, an ecology that rewards leaving, and a crowd that tells you what to want next.

How to read this page

Popular writing on this subject fuses robust animal neurophysiology with shaky social psychology and sells the mixture as one story. Every claim below carries its grade.

Replicated

Reproduced across labs, species, or decades. Safe to build on.

Supported

Good converging evidence, but narrower — one species or one paradigm.

Single study

One good experiment, not broadly replicated. Interesting, not load-bearing.

Theory

A formal or philosophical model. Explains much, predicts little.

Contested

Famous, widely repeated, and either failed replication or shrank on meta-analysis.

Neuron

The signal is about surprise, not pleasure

Nearly everything people believe about dopamine is a mistranslation of one very well-replicated finding.

Replicated

Dopamine reports the error, then moves to the cue

Record from a midbrain dopamine neuron while an animal gets an unexpected drop of juice and it fires a burst. Repeat the pairing with a tone that reliably precedes the juice, and something strange happens: the burst at the juice fades, and a new burst appears at the tone. Deliver the tone and then withhold the juice, and the cell drops below baseline at exactly the moment the juice was due.

The cell is not measuring how good the juice is. It is measuring the difference between what was expected and what arrived. Once the world is fully predicted, the error goes to zero and the signal goes quiet. This is the mechanism behind every “it was better the first time” you have ever had.

Schultz, Dayan & Montague, Science, 1997; causally confirmed with optogenetics, Steinberg et al., 2013.

Supported

Wanting and liking are separate systems

Deplete dopamine in a rat and it will starve beside food it still visibly enjoys — the facial reactions that index pleasure survive intact. Sensitise the same system and the animal pursues harder without any increase in the pleasure it takes. Wanting is dopaminergic, attached to cues, and large. Liking runs on opioid and endocannabinoid hotspots, and is much smaller and more fragile.

This is why craving survives disappointment. The two are not measuring the same thing, so there is no reason for one to update the other.

Berridge & Robinson, 1998–2016. Strong in rodents; the human translation is inference.

Pleasure is a small, slow, fragile system. Wanting is a large, fast, durable one. They were never in balance.

Supported

Novelty is paid like a reward

Genuinely novel stimuli activate the same midbrain regions as reward — before the organism knows whether the novel thing is any good. In learning-theory terms this is an exploration bonus: unfamiliarity is assigned a fictitious value so the agent will go and find out. The bonus is paid up front, and it is not refunded when the new thing turns out to be mediocre.

Bunzeck & Düzel, Neuron, 2006; Kakade & Dayan, 2002.

Replicated

Information itself is a currency

Monkeys will give up water for advance information about a reward they are going to receive anyway — information with no practical use whatsoever. A subset of dopamine neurons signals the value of that information using the same code they use for the water. Curiosity is not a metaphorical appetite.

This is the load-bearing fact for everything screen-shaped. A feed does not need to deliver anything. It needs only to be uncertain about what it will show next.

Bromberg-Martin & Hikosaka, Neuron, 2009; human analogues Kang et al., 2009.

Behaviour

Uncertainty is the schedule that never ends

Feed the prediction-error machine on an unpredictable timetable and it will not stop.

Replicated

Variable ratio is the most persistent schedule known

Reinforce a behaviour every fifth time and you get a steady rate with a pause after each payoff — the animal learns the shape of the contract. Reinforce it on average every fifth time, at random, and you get a high, unbroken rate with no pauses at all.

Then cut the rewards off. Under the fixed schedule responding collapses quickly: the absence of the expected fifth payoff is itself information. Under the variable schedule there is no such information — a long drought is indistinguishable from bad luck — so the animal keeps going, sometimes for thousands of unrewarded responses. This is the partial reinforcement extinction effect, and it is the entire business model of the pull-to-refresh gesture.

Ferster & Skinner, 1957; Humphreys, 1939. Replicated across species for eight decades.

Theory

The gap has to be the right size

Curiosity is triggered not by ignorance but by an attended gap between what you know and what you want to know — and it behaves like a deprivation: aversive, intensifying as resolution approaches, satisfying only briefly on closure. Total ignorance produces no curiosity, because you cannot feel the shape of what is missing. Complete knowledge produces none either. The pull lives in the middle, which is exactly where a headline is engineered to place you.

Loewenstein, Psych Bulletin, 1994. Formally clean; the predicted inverted-U has mixed support.

Replicated

Value collapses steeply near the present

People discount the future hyperbolically rather than exponentially, which means the discount rate is steep close to now and shallow further out. That curvature has one famous consequence: preferences reverse. In January you sincerely prefer the long-term outcome; at 11pm on Tuesday, with the immediate option physically present, you sincerely prefer the other one. Neither self is lying. The curves crossed.

Ainslie, 1975; Laibson, 1997. Behaviourally robust; the mechanism behind it is model-dependent.

Contested

“We adapt back to baseline no matter what”

The hedonic-treadmill claim rests heavily on a 1978 study of 22 lottery winners — a sample far too small to carry the weight placed on it. Large longitudinal panels since show adaptation is real but partial and highly uneven: near-complete for some events, incomplete for divorce or unemployment, barely present for some disabilities. Keep the mechanism, discard the slogan.

Brickman et al., 1978 (n=22) vs. Lucas et al., 2003–2007 panel studies.

Ecology

Your patience is set by the background, not by you

The best model of “when do I leave this and go find something else” was written for birds eating insects.

Replicated

The marginal value theorem

An animal foraging in a patch faces diminishing returns — the easy berries go first. Charnov's theorem gives the optimal moment to leave: quit when the rate of return in this patch falls to the average rate of the environment as a whole, travel time included.

The consequence is the part nobody expects. Your leaving threshold is not a property of the patch. It is a property of everything else available to you. Enrich the environment — make alternatives plentiful and cheap to reach — and the optimal moment to abandon any given patch arrives earlier, automatically, for a perfectly rational forager. Nothing about the patch changed. Nothing about the forager changed. The background did.

Charnov, 1976; human and monkey patch-leaving tracks a ramping signal in anterior cingulate cortex, Hayden et al., 2011.

Supported

People usually overstay, not understay

This is the finding that should complicate the whole premise. In controlled foraging tasks, humans and monkeys systematically remain in depleted patches longer than optimal. The bias runs in the opposite direction from restlessness. So the flightiness you notice in yourself is unlikely to be an intrinsic disposition to leave early — it is far more consistent with a background reward rate that has been driven artificially high. In a genuinely rich environment, a rational agent leaves everything quickly and looks impulsive doing it.

Hayden et al., 2011; Constantino & Daw, 2015. Consistent across paradigms; effect size varies.

You are not more impatient than your grandparents. You are foraging in an environment with a thousand times the background yield.

Supported

Two kinds of exploration, and they come apart

Every choice about staying or switching is formally a bandit problem: exploit the option you know, or explore one you don't. People solve it two ways. Directed exploration adds an explicit bonus to uncertain options — you choose the unknown because it is unknown. Random exploration injects noise into the decision itself. They dissociate behaviourally, respond differently to how much time is left to exploit what you learn, and change with age.

Wilson et al., 2014; Gershman, 2018; Daw et al., 2006.

Contested

“Too many options paralyses people”

The 24-jams study is one of the most repeated results in popular behavioural science. A meta-analysis of 50 experiments found a mean effect indistinguishable from zero, with enormous heterogeneity — choice overload appears under specific preconditions and vanishes otherwise. Abundance is doing something to you, but “paralysis by choice” is not the reliable finding it is presented as.

Iyengar & Lepper, 2000 vs. Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd, 2010 (k=50).

Social

Waiting for someone else to want it first

The part of the question with the least neuroscience behind it and the most explanatory power.

Theory

Desire is triangular

Girard's claim is that we do not desire objects directly. Desire runs subject → model → object: we want what someone we take seriously appears to want, and the object acquires its glow from the model's attention rather than from any property of its own. It explains why enthusiasm is contagious in a way that argument is not, why an item becomes desirable the moment someone else reaches for it, and why so many people are functionally idling — waiting for a model to indicate what is worth wanting.

It also explains the ugly part. If desire is borrowed, the model is simultaneously the guide and the obstacle, and admiration converts into rivalry with no change in the underlying relationship.

Girard, 1961. Literary and philosophical, not experimental — a lens, not a measurement.

Supported

Watching a choice changes what the watcher values

The empirical shadow of the above. Observing someone else choose an object raises the observer's own valuation of it and engages ventral striatum — the same circuitry used for first-hand reward. Separately, discovering your rating differs from a group's produces a signal resembling a prediction error, and the size of that signal predicts how much you later shift toward the group. Conformity is not politeness. It is value updating.

Lebreton et al., 2012; Klucharev et al., 2009. Modest samples; usual fMRI caveats.

Supported

Cascades: rational people abandoning their own evidence

The sharpest version needs no irrationality at all. Each person gets a weak private hint about which option is better and decides in sequence, able to see everyone before them. After two people happen to choose the same way, the third person's own hint is outweighed by the public record — so they follow, and in following they add a data point that is not independent evidence. Everyone behaves optimally, and the group locks onto an answer that almost nobody's private information supports.

Cascades are therefore both powerful and brittle. They form on almost no evidence, and one credible contrarian releasing real private information can collapse one instantly. That is the formal reason trends turn over so fast.

Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer & Welch, 1992; lab confirmation Anderson & Holt, 1997.

Theory

Thresholds, not preferences, decide what a crowd does

Granovetter's model gives each person a threshold — the number of others who must act before they will. A crowd of a hundred with thresholds 0, 1, 2 … 99 cascades completely. Change one person's threshold from 1 to 2 and nothing happens at all. Same people, same average willingness, opposite outcome. Collective behaviour is not readable from individual attitudes, which is why “everyone secretly wanted this” and “nobody wanted this” can both be false descriptions of the same event.

Granovetter, AJS, 1978.

Single study

Social influence makes hits bigger and less predictable

The MusicLab experiment gave 14,341 people the same 48 unknown songs. Some downloaded in isolation; others saw download counts, split across eight independent parallel worlds. Social influence increased inequality — winners won much bigger — and simultaneously increased unpredictability: the same song landed in very different positions in different worlds. Quality set a floor and a ceiling; within that band, early accident decided everything.

Salganik, Dodds & Watts, Science, 2006. One experiment, one domain.

Supported

Costly behaviour needs several sources, not one

A rumour spreads like a virus: one exposure can do it. A behaviour with real costs — a diet, a career move, a commitment — usually does not. It needs reinforcement from multiple independent contacts before adoption, which means it spreads better through clustered, redundant networks than through the wide-reaching weak ties that carry simple information fastest. This is why seeing something everywhere produces no movement at all until two people you actually know do it.

Centola & Macy, 2007; Centola, Science, 2010 (controlled network experiment).

Design

The assembly

None of these mechanisms was invented by a product team. All of them are now deliberately engaged by one.

Supported

What the interface is actually doing

The gesture is a lever and the schedule beneath it is variable ratio. The badge is a cue that has inherited the value of what it predicts, so it pulls a response before it is even read. The feed never terminates, which removes the natural end-of-patch signal that would trigger a leaving decision. Visible counts on every item are cascade fuel, converting other people's clicks into evidence. And the whole environment runs at a very high background reward rate — which, per the marginal value theorem, makes leaving any individual thing quickly the correct move.

Each mechanism is well evidenced in the lab. That they are combined on purpose is documented from the industry itself. What follows at population scale is a separate and much less settled question.

Mechanisms as cited above; industry practice documented in Eyal, Hooked, 2014.

Contested

The size of the damage is not established

Here the honest answer is uncomfortable for everyone. Large specification-curve analyses across three big datasets found associations between digital technology use and adolescent wellbeing that are real but tiny — comparable in size to trivial variables in the same datasets, and dwarfed by sleep and bullying. Other work with different designs finds larger effects. The mechanisms on this page are solid. The claim that they are producing a mental-health catastrophe is genuinely disputed, and anyone who tells you it is settled — in either direction — is ahead of the evidence.

Orben & Przybylski, Nature Human Behaviour, 2019, vs. Twenge et al. Active dispute as of 2026.

Single study

The unoccupied mind is unpleasant enough to avoid

Asked to sit alone with their thoughts for six to fifteen minutes, a substantial share of participants chose to self-administer an electric shock they had earlier paid money to avoid. The headline figure — two-thirds of men — is usually quoted without the rest: it was a quarter of women, and one participant shocked himself 190 times, badly distorting the mean. Real and striking. Also one paper, and routinely overstated.

Wilson et al., Science, 2014.

Consequences

What the model says to do

Graded by how much the derivation is worth. Most advice in this genre is theory dressed as replicated.

Supported

Change the situation, not the impulse

Every mechanism here is cue-driven and automatic. Almost none of it is reachable by in-the-moment resistance, and the willpower literature that promised otherwise has not held up. What does hold up is situational strategy: removing cues, adding friction, and precommitting while the curves have not yet crossed. Save More Tomorrow — committing future raises to savings in advance — remains the strongest real-world demonstration that binding your later self beats persuading them.

Thaler & Benartzi, 2004; Duckworth, Gendler & Gross, 2016. Note: ego depletion, which made willpower sound like a fuel tank, failed a 23-lab replication (Hagger et al., 2016).

Supported

Make the schedule predictable

The persistence comes from variability, not from reward. A source that pays on a known timetable produces pauses after each payoff — the behaviour the fixed schedule generates in Figure 2. Scheduled checking is not a discipline trick; it is a direct removal of the mechanism that makes checking self-sustaining.

Direct application of Ferster & Skinner, 1957. The schedule effect is replicated; this translation is inference, though a tight one.

Theory

Lower the background, not the foreground

If the marginal value theorem describes you, your inability to stay with anything is a function of the average yield of your whole environment. Concentrating harder on the book fights the wrong term in the equation. Reducing the richness of everything else raises the value of every remaining patch by the same act. The cleanest prediction on this page, and the least directly tested in humans outside foraging tasks.

Inference from Charnov, 1976. Well motivated, not demonstrated.

Theory

Choose your models on purpose

If desire is borrowed, the only real leverage is upstream, in who you take seriously. You do not get to opt out of mimetic transmission by noticing it — noticing changes nothing about where the glow comes from. What you can change is the small set of people whose attention you treat as evidence.

Girard, 1961; applied version in Burgis, Wanting, 2021.

The seeking system was never built to be satisfied. It was built to keep an animal moving in a world where standing still was the only real failure.

Supported

And it is not a malfunction

The final correction. Every component here is an adaptation working correctly. Prediction error is how anything is learned at all. The exploration bonus is what stops an agent settling permanently on the first mediocre option it finds. Patch-leaving is optimal foraging. Copying high-status models is the fastest known route to skills you cannot evaluate in advance, and a large part of why humans accumulate culture at all. None of this is broken. It is a well-tuned system running in an environment deliberately re-engineered to keep every one of its inputs saturated.

Boyd & Richerson on cultural transmission; Henrich, The Secret of Our Success, 2016.

A closing correction

Restlessness is usually explained with one mechanism, and it takes at least four. A neuron that reports surprise and then hands its excitement to whatever predicts the good thing. A schedule that removes the signal telling you when to stop. An ecology in which the right moment to leave is set by how rich everything else is. And a crowd that supplies the target, because in the absence of private information, following is the rational move.

These interact, but they are separately caused. Collapsing them into a single word — dopamine, addiction, attention span — is why so much writing on this subject explains everything and predicts nothing.

Citations are given so they can be checked. Grades are a judgement of the current literature and are arguable. Nothing here is clinical advice.
