Feels Like Learning

Rereading, rewatching and having AI explain it again all feel like learning. Pulling it back out of your own head is what makes it stick. This page lets you test that on yourself, then shows how to make AI do the second thing instead of the first.

01 · Predict

Make a guess first

Guessing before you're told is itself a study technique, so start there. Two groups of students get the same short science passage and the same total time with it.

Group A reads the passage four times. Group B reads it once, then three times writes down everything they can remember, with no feedback.

Roediger & Karpicke (2006), Psychological Science, Experiment 2: 180 college students, 30 per group, short science passages, recall written from memory without feedback.

ConditionAfter 5 minutesAfter 1 weekLost
Read 4×83%40%−43 pts

Rereading wins at five minutes. A week later it has lost about half of what it had, while the recall group lost about a seventh. The lines cross. That crossing is the whole problem: the moment you check yourself is usually the moment rereading looks best.

There's a second result in the same study. Asked how much they'd remember in a week, the rereaders rated their chances highest (4.8 on a 1–7 scale, against 4.0 for the recall group). They had been through the passage about fourteen times; the recall group, about three. All that reading felt like knowing it.

02 · Test yourself

Run the experiment on yourself

The same design researchers use, shrunk to twelve Swahili words. You'll see each word once. Then you'll reread half of them and try to recall the other half. Then the page tests you, ideally tomorrow.

Your words and answers stay in this browser. Nothing is sent anywhere. With six words in each group, one lucky guess can swing your result. The research effect comes from many people and many items, so treat yours as a demonstration, not a measurement.

03 · Why easy misleads

Why the easy way feels better

Reading something the second time is smooth. It's familiar, so you process it faster, and that speed registers as "I know this." Researchers call it a fluency illusion: you're measuring how easy the page is to read, not whether you could produce it without the page.

The illusion is easy to trigger. In one study, people shown words in a large font predicted they'd remember them better than words in a small font. Font size made no difference to what they actually remembered. Anything that makes the material easier to take in feels like learning.

Recall is the opposite. It's slow, you get things wrong, and it feels like failing. But digging something out of memory strengthens the route back to it. Recall doesn't just read a memory out; it changes the memory. That is the video's central claim, and it's right.

One correction to the strong version: rereading isn't useless. It helps a little; it just does much less than recall for the same time.

Rule of thumb: if it feels easy, you're probably taking in. If it feels a bit too hard, you're probably learning. Not every difficulty helps (a badly written textbook is just hard). The useful kind is having to produce the answer yourself.

Sort ten study habits

For each one: does it mainly build memory that lasts, or does it mainly feel productive? Then check against the research.

    Checkpoint

    In the study in section 01, why does rereading win at five minutes but lose at a week?

    At five minutes you're still running on familiarity, and rereading tops that up best. After a week, what survives is what you practiced pulling out of memory, and the rereaders never practiced that.

    04 · When to review

    When you review matters as much as whether

    Memory fades fast at first, then more slowly. Hermann Ebbinghaus measured this on himself in the 1880s, timing how long it took to relearn lists of nonsense syllables, and a 2015 replication (again one researcher, on himself) got nearly the same curve. Each successful recall makes the next fade slower, and a recall that takes some effort buys more than an effortless one. So the same four reviews do very different things depending on when you do them.

    A schematic, not data. The curve shape is the one the FSRS flashcard scheduler uses, with the numbers set by hand to match the shapes in the research. Compare plans with it; don't read your own percentages off it.

    Start with Plan A, four reviews crammed the night before a day-7 test, against Plan B, the same four spread over two weeks. Set the check day to 7: the crammer looks fine. Now drag it to 30.

    How far apart? One study of more than 1,300 people found the best gap grows with how long you need to remember. To remember something for a week, review it after about 3 days. For a month, after about 8 days. For a year, after about 4 weeks. As a share of the time you need it for, that's 20–40% for a week, falling to 5–10% for a year. If you'd rather not do the arithmetic, let a flashcard app schedule it (more in section 07).

    Checkpoint

    You have a test in ten days and time for four reviews. Roughly when do you do them, and what do you do in each one?

    Spread them, with gaps that grow and the last one a day or two before the test, something like days 1, 3, 6 and 9. Each one is a recall attempt, not a reread: cover the answers, try, then check. Cramming all four the night before gets you through the test and leaves little a month later.

    05 · Ten jobs for an AI

    Ten jobs to give an AI

    Most people use AI as an explainer, the one job that's pure taking in. The video names nine more. Here they are, each with a prompt to copy, how much of the work it leaves to you, and where it goes wrong.

    Where are you with the subject?

    The roles are the video's. The prompts are rewritten with guardrails (one question at a time, attempt before answer, grade against a source). "You do" is this page's judgment of how much of the producing the role leaves to you, not a measured number.

    Checkpoint

    You've just read a chapter and think you get it. Which two jobs would you give the AI, and why not the explainer?

    The Socratic questioner (it asks, you answer) and the listener (you explain it from memory, it grades you against the chapter). Either one makes you produce. The explainer would hand you another smooth read, which is exactly the thing that feels like knowing it.

    06 · The catch

    The same AI can make you better or worse

    The video saves its most important point for the end: learning happens in the struggle, and AI makes it very easy to skip the struggle. Here's the evidence behind that, and the rules that follow from it.

    Plain chatbot vs. hint-only tutor

    −17%

    High-school math students who practiced with a plain chatbot did much better during practice (+48%), then scored 17% lower than students with no AI on an exam without it. They didn't notice the harm. A version built to give hints instead of answers lifted practice even more (+127%) and removed the exam harm, though it added no gain.

    Bastani et al. (2025), PNAS. Nearly 1,000 students in one Turkish high school, randomised by class. Short-term results.

    A tutor built to make you work

    2×

    Physics students using an AI tutor designed around good teaching practice (one step at a time, the student does the thinking) had median learning gains more than double those from an active-learning class, in less time: 49 minutes against 60.

    Kestin et al. (2025), Scientific Reports. 194 Harvard students, crossover design. Tested right after the lesson, so it says nothing yet about retention.

    The GPS comparison

    3 years

    Drivers who used GPS more had worse spatial memory in a virtual navigation test. Thirteen of them retested about three years later showed steeper decline with heavier use. And the heavy users didn't think their sense of direction was any worse.

    Dahmani & Bohbot (2020), Scientific Reports. 50 drivers; 13 followed up. Correlational.

    Elsewhere, both directions. A World Bank trial in Nigeria found six weeks of after-school English tutoring with a GPT-4 assistant raised test scores by about 0.3 standard deviations (working paper, 2025). A 2026 working paper with about 2,400 US undergraduates found that access to an AI tutor lowered final grades (not yet peer-reviewed). The widely shared "Your Brain on ChatGPT" EEG study is a preprint with 54 people, and its authors have asked readers not to describe it as brain damage.

    The difference isn't the model. It's who does the work. When the AI produces the answer, you get a smooth read and a good feeling. When it makes you produce the answer, you get the learning.

    1. Attempt first. Ask only once you're stuck, and say exactly where.
    2. Ask for the next hint, not the answer. "Give me one hint" keeps the last step yours.
    3. Redo it without help. After you've got there with AI, go back to the start and do the whole thing alone. The video says this takes 10–15% more time. It's the step that makes it yours.
    4. Grade against a source, not the AI's opinion. Chatbots tend to agree with you when you're half right, and they can invent numbers. Give it the text and tell it to grade against that.
    Checkpoint

    In the high-school study, why did the plain chatbot hurt exam scores when the hint-only version mostly didn't?

    Students used the plain chatbot to get answers during practice, so they skipped the attempts and mistakes that build the skill. With the AI gone, the skill wasn't there. The hint-only tutor kept the work with the student.

    07 · Tools

    Tools, and what each one actually trains

    Every tool below can be used to take in or to produce. The feature you click decides which.

    The underrated one

    Gemini Notebook (NotebookLM until July 2026)

    Gemini Notebook answers from the sources you give it (a chapter, a lecture transcript, your notes, a web page) and cites the passage behind each claim. That fixes the two biggest problems with using a chatbot to study: it inventing things, and it grading you against its own opinion instead of your material.

    A study session in six steps

    1. One notebook per subject. Load only sources you trust: the chapter, the lecture transcript, your notes. This page works too; add its address as a source and try the steps on it.
    2. Open the mind map and pick the branch you know least. That's your map.
    3. One pass of taking in. Read it, or play the Audio Overview once. You can steer the overview with a prompt ("chapters 3 and 4, assume I'm a beginner"). This is the only consumption step.
    4. Close everything and explain it. Type your explanation into the chat from memory and ask it to grade you against the sources, with citations. There's no built-in grader for this; the prompt below does the job.
    5. Get questioned. Set the chat to Learning Guide, or make a quiz on hard difficulty and answer without peeking. Use "Explain" on what you miss, retake only the missed ones, then ask the chat where your misses cluster.
    6. Keep it. The app has flashcards but no stated review schedule. Download them as a CSV, import them into Anki, and let it schedule them.

    Prompt for step 4

    I'm going to explain this topic from memory. Grade my explanation only against the sources in this notebook, not general knowledge. List what I got right, what I left out, and anything I said that the sources don't support, citing the passage each time. Don't rewrite my explanation for me.

    Three cautions. A citation shows where an answer came from, not that the answer reads it correctly, so click through on anything that matters. Notebooks opened inside the regular Gemini app may also use web search; for grading against your sources only, use Gemini Notebook itself. And an Audio Overview is a podcast about your material: pleasant, and as passive as rereading. The free tier allows 50 sources per notebook.

    Others worth knowing

    Gemini: Guided Learning

    A mode in the Gemini app that breaks a problem into steps, asks you open questions, and checks your understanding with quizzes instead of handing over the answer. Gemini's newer study notebooks start with a diagnostic quiz, which does the interviewer's job for you.

    Socratic questioner, examiner, interviewer

    ChatGPT: Study mode

    On every plan. Type @study or pick Study from the + menu, and ChatGPT asks questions and gives hints instead of answers. OpenAI notes it runs on instructions, so it can slip; tell it to stay in role. It now makes flashcards too, without a review schedule.

    Socratic questioner, examiner

    Claude: learning mode

    Guides you with questions instead of handing over answers: the Socratic questioner as a setting. It started in Claude for Education and reached everyone as a response style in 2025; Anthropic has since been moving styles into skills, so look in the style or skills menu. Claude Code has a matching Learning style that leaves the key pieces of code for you to write, then reviews them. Can't find the switch? Put the Socratic prompt from section 05 in a project's instructions.

    Socratic questioner, checker

    Anki

    Free flashcard software (AnkiMobile on iPhone is paid) that schedules each card for just before you'd forget it. Switch on FSRS at the bottom of the deck options; it's the modern scheduler behind the curve in section 04. Have AI draft the cards, then you answer them.

    Spaced review

    Google: Learn Your Way

    A Google Labs experiment that rewrites textbook chapters for your level and interests, with quizzes built in. In Google's own small study (60 students), it beat a standard digital reader on a test 3–5 days later, 78% to 67%.

    08 · Checking the video

    What the video gets right, and what it oversells

    The core is solid and mainstream. Some of the numbers are sales talk. Here's each claim against the research.

    Solid

    "You learn through production, not consumption."

    Recalling, explaining and attempting beat rereading and re-watching. One of the best-replicated findings in learning research.

    Solid

    "Reread four times: 40% after a week. Read once, test three times: 61%."

    Accurate to the study (section 01). Worth knowing it was short science passages and 180 college students, and that rereading won at five minutes.

    Solid

    "Spaced repetition: review just as you're about to forget."

    The spacing effect is as solid as the testing effect, and the two stack.

    Oversold

    "Spaced repetition makes you five to ten times more efficient."

    No source given, and no standard figure like it. The benefit is real; its size depends on the material, the gaps and how long you need to remember.

    Oversold

    "Learn in a day what takes others weeks." "We use 10–20% of our potential."

    Opening hooks, not findings. Nobody has measured a percentage of potential.

    Mixed

    "Engage all your senses. Say it, draw it, write it."

    Drawing and explaining in your own words do help, because both make you produce. But the idea that people learn best in their own preferred style (visual, auditory) has not held up when tested.

    Mixed

    "Lectures were developed thousands of years ago and don't scale."

    Lecture-only teaching does lose to active learning on average: across 225 studies in science courses, failure rates were 34% under lecturing against 22% with active learning. "Thousands of years" is decoration.

    Promising

    "GPS weakens your mental map, and AI may do the same to your reasoning."

    The GPS study is real but small and correlational, run on virtual mazes. For AI, the best evidence so far (section 06) says it depends on whether the tool lets you skip the work.

    Promising

    "AI can examine you like a thesis defense."

    Yes, with a catch the video mentions only once: chatbots lean toward agreeing with you. Ground the examiner in a source (section 07).

    Mixed

    "Have the AI read your last hundred conversations and find your recurring mistake."

    Depends on whether your tool can search its chat history at all, and it can see patterns that aren't there. Pasting in your own list of mistakes is more reliable.

    09 · More moves

    Seven moves the video didn't cover

    All of them come back to the same idea, making yourself produce, applied from different angles.

    Guess before you're taught

    Trying to answer before you've learned something, even getting it wrong, makes the later lesson stick better. In one study, questions people had attempted (and missed) before reading were answered 75% of the time afterward, against 56% for extra reading time. You did this in section 01.

    Try itBefore reading a chapter, have AI write you five questions on it. Answer them cold.

    Richland, Kornell & Kao (2009); Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009)

    Mix the problem types

    Practising A, B, C mixed together beats doing all the A's, then all the B's, because you have to work out which method each problem needs. In a trial with 787 seventh-graders, mixed practice scored 61% against 38% a month later. It also feels worse: in another study, most learners did better mixed yet rated blocked practice as just as good.

    Try itAsk for a quiz that mixes three topics in random order, without labels.

    Rohrer et al. (2020); Kornell & Bjork (2008)

    Ask "why is that true?"

    Stopping to explain why a fact holds, in terms of what you already know, ties it to more of your memory.

    Try itAfter each paragraph, answer "why would that be?" before reading on.

    Dunlosky et al. (2013): moderate utility

    Explain each step to yourself

    Learners who explain worked examples to themselves line by line learn more than those who just read them.

    Try itCover a worked solution and say what each step does and why before uncovering it.

    Chi et al. (1989, 1994)

    Draw it from memory

    Sketching how something works, without looking, is a form of recall. It also exposes gaps that words cover over.

    Try itDraw the forgetting curve from section 04 with both plans. Then scroll up and compare.

    Fiorella & Mayer (2016): generative learning

    Predict your score first

    Before any quiz, write down how many you'll get. The gap between your guess and your score is your fluency illusion, measured.

    Try itYou did this in section 02. Compare your prediction with your result.

    Koriat & Bjork (2005); Kornell et al. (2011)

    Put the questions inside the reading

    An essay can carry its own spaced review: questions embedded in the text that come back days, then weeks later. Quantum Country, a quantum-computing primer, was built this way, with 112 questions in its first essay. It's the model for a learn mode across this site.

    Try itRead the first chapter of quantumcountry.com and answer its cards when they come back.

    Matuschak & Nielsen (2019)
