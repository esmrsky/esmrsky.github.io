Forty-nine stories · Genesis to Revelation

The stories, read both ways

How to use this file. The stories run in Bible order, from Genesis to Revelation, with a few extras added where they matter. Each one has the same five parts: the story, the inside-time reading, the outside-of-time reading, the Father angle, and the pressure point. Quotes are short and marked (ESV) or (NIV). The two sides are called “inside-time” and “outside-of-time” throughout. The inside-time side says God lives through the story with his people, in real sequence, and some of the future is still open. The outside-of-time side (the classical side) says God sees all time at once, does not change, and speaks to us in human language so we can understand him.

Two things both sides agree on, so the hosts can say them early: the Bible itself says God is faithful and does not lie, and the Bible itself says God “relents” and is “grieved.” The whole debate is about how to hold those two kinds of verses together.

Genesis 2–3

The garden, “Where are you?”, and the guarded tree

God shapes a man from the dust and breathes into him. He plants a garden and brings the animals to the man “to see what he would call them.” He notes that “it is not good that the man should be alone,” and then he builds a partner for him. In the cool of the day, God walks in the garden. The man and woman have eaten the fruit and are hiding in the trees. God calls out, “Where are you?” (ESV). He asks questions: “Who told you that you were naked?” He sews clothes for them. Then he sends them out and puts angels and a flaming sword at the east of the garden, “to guard the way to the tree of life” (ESV), so they cannot eat and live forever in that state.

Read from inside time

God works in steps, the way a person works. He makes, he waits, he watches what the man will name the animals, he sees what is “not good.” He walks, he calls, he asks. The text reads like a Father looking for hiding children, not a Father who already stares at them. The guarded tree shows God responding to a new situation, a world he did not want, with a new plan.

Read from outside time

The walking and the question are the way a God who is spirit shows up in a story we can picture. He asks “Where are you?” to draw the man out and make him confess, not to find out. The “not good” is about the creation’s incompleteness, a stage in a plan God already had. The curse comes with a promise that the woman’s offspring will crush the serpent’s head, so the rescue was in view from the start.

The Father angleA father searching for a hiding child, then making clothes for the child he is about to send away, is the first picture of God’s tenderness mixed with discipline.

“Where are you?” is hardest for the outside side, because a question is the plainest sign of someone who wants an answer. It is hardest for the inside side that the same chapter has the promise of the serpent-crusher right after the fall, which looks like a plan already in hand.

Genesis 6

God’s grief before the flood

People fill the earth and the violence grows. The text says “the wickedness of man was great in the earth” and every thought of the heart was “only evil continually.” Then comes the line that startles readers: “the LORD regretted that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him to his heart” (ESV). God decides to wipe the slate clean. But “Noah found favor in the eyes of the LORD” (ESV). God tells Noah to build a boat, gives the measurements, and Noah does it.

Read from inside time

This is the plainest picture of a God whose heart can be hurt. He made humans, watched what they did, and felt pain. The verb is the same one used when God “regrets” making Saul king. The story moves in order: creation, corruption, grief, judgment, rescue through one family.

Read from outside time

The word for regret describes how God looks from the human side when he turns from blessing to judgment. It names his real sorrow over sin without saying he was surprised or wrong. Many on this side add that the same chapter is already planning the rescue: Noah, the covenant, the boat. God’s judgment and his mercy come from one unchanging holy love meeting a changing world.

The Father angleThe text says it “grieved him to his heart.” This is a Father who is wounded by what his children have done and still saves a household.

Hardest for outside“it grieved him to his heart” is about as close to a real inner hurt as the Hebrew can say.

Hardest for insideif God regretted making people because he did not see this coming, why does the same chapter show him ready with a rescue plan and a covenant?

Genesis 11

Babel and “came down to see”

The whole world speaks one language. People find a plain and say, “Come, let us build ourselves a city and a tower with its top in the heavens” (ESV). They want a name for themselves, and they fear being scattered. Then the text turns, almost with a smile: “The LORD came down to see the city and the tower” (ESV). The tower they built to reach the sky is so small that God has to come down to look at it. He sees what a united humanity could do, mixes their speech, and scatters them over the earth.

Read from inside time

God comes down, looks, and then decides. He reacts to what people are doing today. The story shows a God who watches a project grow and steps in at a chosen moment.

Read from outside time

“Came down to see” is a figure of speech, the same one used with a gentle edge of irony: the tower is small in God’s eyes. It tells readers that nothing human is hidden from him. The Lord was never unaware; the language teaches that God judges after examination, not by whim.

The Father angleA Father who refuses to let his children build their own substitute for him, and who breaks up a pride that would destroy them, even though it looks like an unkind act.

Hardest for outsideGod “came down” is travel language, right before the ones in Genesis 18.

Hardest for insidethe text is so ironic about the size of the tower that it is hard to picture a God who needed to travel to find out about it.

Genesis 18

Abraham bargains for Sodom

Three visitors come to Abraham’s tent. Abraham runs to meet them, Sarah bakes bread, and one of the men says Sarah will have a son in a year. Sarah laughs inside the tent; the visitor says, “Why did Sarah laugh?” Then the Lord says, “Shall I hide from Abraham what I am about to do?” (ESV). He says the outcry against Sodom is great, and “I will go down to see whether they have done altogether according to the outcry that has come to me. And if not, I will know” (ESV). The men walk toward Sodom. Abraham stays standing before the Lord and starts bargaining: fifty righteous people? Forty-five? Forty? Thirty? Twenty? Ten? Each time the Lord says he will spare the city. Then the Lord goes his way.

Read from inside time

God thinks aloud, shares his plan with a friend, and lets the friend push back. Abraham’s argument moves God’s stated terms down from fifty to ten. “I will go down to see... and if not, I will know” sounds like a God who gathers information by going where the events are.

Read from outside time

God invites Abraham into his purposes, which is a way of teaching Abraham, and the reader, what justice looks like. The bargaining draws out of Abraham a character God already knew, and shows that God’s judgment is never careless. The “go down to see” language is a judge making his examination public, the way a king might in a courtroom. And Sodom is not saved in the end, so the plan stands.

The Father angleGod treats Abraham as a friend, not a servant. A Father tells his son what he is about to do and lets him argue.

Hardest for outsideGod says “if not, I will know,” which is a future-tense gap in knowledge.

Hardest for insidethe city is not saved. The bargain gets Lot out but does not change the fire, which looks like God was never going to be talked out of it.

Genesis 22

Abraham and Isaac, “now I know”

God tells Abraham to take his only son, Isaac, whom he loves, and offer him on a mountain. Abraham gets up early and goes. Isaac carries the wood and asks, “Where is the lamb for a burnt offering?” Abraham answers, “God will provide for himself the lamb” (ESV). Abraham ties Isaac up and lifts the knife. The angel of the Lord cries out, “Abraham, Abraham!” Abraham answers, and the angel says, “Do not lay your hand on the boy.” Then the line: “now I know that you fear God, seeing you have not withheld your son, your only son, from me” (ESV). Abraham sees a ram caught in the thicket and offers it.

Read from inside time

“Now I know” means God learned something he did not know in that form before: what Abraham would do when the knife was in his hand. The test was real because the outcome was not fixed in advance. God is on the road with Abraham, waiting.

Read from outside time

“Now I know” is the language of a proven fact: now it is shown, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to every reader. The test did not inform God; it formed Abraham and revealed him. God had already promised that Isaac would be the line of descent, and Hebrews says Abraham reasoned that God could raise the dead. God’s real command and his real mercy sit inside a plan that was always going to provide the ram.

The Father angleThe deepest picture in the Bible of a father giving up a beloved son, and a God who provides a substitute. For both sides this story points to the cross.

Hardest for outside“now I know” comes after the test, and “seeing you have not withheld” is a report of what has just happened.

Hardest for insideAbraham told Isaac that God would provide the lamb before any lamb was in sight, and it came, as if God had it in place all along.

Genesis 37–50

Joseph: “You meant evil against me, but God meant it for good”

Joseph dreams that his brothers’ sheaves bow to his. His brothers hate him, throw him into a pit, and sell him for twenty pieces of silver. In Egypt he serves Potiphar, is falsely accused, is jailed, interprets two prisoners’ dreams, and is forgotten for two more years. Then Pharaoh dreams, and Joseph is raised to second in command and stores grain for seven lean years. His brothers come to buy food. Joseph tests them and weeps in secret. When their father Jacob dies, they fear revenge. Joseph says, “As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring it about that many people should be kept alive” (ESV). Earlier he had told them, “it was not you who sent me here, but God” (ESV).

Read from inside time

God does his good work in and through free, wicked choices. The brothers really chose to sell him. God is a steady hand who keeps turning bad moves toward good ends, adjusting as the story unfolds. Nothing in the text says God forced the brothers.

Read from outside time

This is the favorite proof of this side. The same event has two authors, the brothers who “meant evil” and God who “meant it for good.” The dreams in chapter 37 show the end from the start. God planned the famine and the rescue, and the brothers were fully guilty while carrying out his plan.

The Father angleA Father who works through a son’s suffering to save a whole family, and a son who forgives as his Father forgave.

Hardest for outside

Hardest for insidethe dreams at seventeen foretell, without condition, a future that takes twenty-two years of other people’s free choices to complete. Hardest for the outside side: if God “meant” the selling, how do the brothers remain guilty? The text says both and does not explain.

Exodus 3–4

Moses argues, “if they will not believe,” and Aaron

A bush burns and is not consumed. God calls Moses by name, tells him that he has seen the misery of his people, and sends him to Pharaoh. Moses objects five times. Who am I? Who are you? “they will not believe me or listen to my voice” (ESV). God gives him signs, a staff that becomes a snake and a hand with leprosy. Then God says, “If they will not believe you,” God said, “or listen to the first sign, they may believe the latter sign” (ESV). Moses says he is slow of speech. God says, “Who has made man’s mouth?” Moses begs, “Oh, my Lord, please send someone else.” Then “the anger of the LORD was kindled against Moses” (ESV), and God says Aaron, his brother, is coming out to meet him and will speak for him.

Read from inside time

The if-then talk is natural if the future is partly open. “If they will not believe... they may believe” reads like a plan with branches. Moses’s refusal changes the plan: Aaron is added after Moses balks. God is angry, a feeling that responds to what Moses just said, and then he adjusts.

Read from outside time

God already told Moses at the bush that Pharaoh would not let them go (Exodus 3:19) and that Aaron was coming (4:14: “he is coming out to meet you”). The signs are given for Israel’s sake, not because God is unsure. The back-and-forth between God and Moses shapes Moses, and the Aaron arrangement is part of how God worked out the plan in time.

The Father angleA Father who is patient with a frightened son’s excuses, gets angry at the fifth, and then still gives him a brother for company.

Hardest for outsideGod’s anger is reported right after Moses’s last refusal, and the new help follows it, in that order.

Hardest for insideGod says in the same breath that “he is coming out to meet you,” which sounds like Aaron was already on his way.

Exodus 4–14

Pharaoh’s heart, in sequence

Before Moses leaves for Egypt, God says, “I will harden his heart, so that he will not let the people go” (ESV). Then the plagues begin. Through the first five, the text says Pharaoh hardened his own heart, or that his heart “was hardened.” Starting with the sixth plague, boils, it says, “the LORD hardened the heart of Pharaoh” (ESV). Pharaoh bargains, promises, takes it back. After the death of the firstborn he lets Israel go, then changes his mind and chases them to the sea. God says he will gain glory over Pharaoh. The sea opens, and closes.

Read from inside time

The sequence matters. Pharaoh hardens himself first, again and again. Only after repeated refusals does God confirm the hardening, handing him over to the road he chose. God is dealing with him one plague at a time, and Pharaoh could have turned at each step.

Read from outside time

God announced the hardening before the first plague. The point is that God holds the story, using Pharaoh’s stubbornness to show his own power. Paul quotes this in Romans 9: “For this very purpose I have raised you up.” The two kinds of verses, Pharaoh hardening himself and God hardening Pharaoh, describe one event from two sides.

The Father angleGod calls Israel “my firstborn son” in Exodus 4:22 and asks for him back. The whole contest is a Father demanding his son be released.

Hardest for outside

Hardest for insideGod says “I will harden” at the very start, before Pharaoh has done anything. Hardest for the outside side: the sequence in the text, with self-hardening first, looks like cause and effect over time.

Exodus 32–34

The golden calf, Moses intercedes, God relents, and “show me your glory”

Moses is on the mountain forty days. The people get tired of waiting and ask Aaron for gods. Aaron melts their gold into a calf. God tells Moses, “Go down, for your people, whom you brought up out of the land of Egypt, have corrupted themselves.” Then: “Now therefore let me alone, that my wrath may burn hot against them and I may consume them, in order that I may make a great nation of you” (ESV). Moses pleads: remember your promise, and what will Egypt say? Then: “the LORD relented from the disaster that he had spoken of bringing on his people” (ESV). Moses comes down, sees the dancing, and smashes the tablets. After the people are punished, he goes back and offers to be blotted out for them. He then asks, “Please show me your glory” (ESV). God hides him in a rock and passes by, proclaiming “The LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness” (ESV).

Read from inside time

This is a picture of a God who invites a human to change his course. “Let me alone” sounds like God telling Moses that he can stop him. Moses argues, and God relents. The revelation of God’s name at the end shows that this is who God is: ready to forgive when asked.

Read from outside time

“Let me alone” is a rhetorical invitation that draws out Moses’s role as mediator. God was never going to make the whole nation vanish, since he had sworn the promise to Abraham. His intention to spare was fixed, and Moses’s prayer is the means he chose to carry it out. “Relented” describes how mercy looks in time.

The Father angleThe proclamation, “merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness,” is the Father’s name, spoken to a son who asked to see him.

Hardest for outsideMoses argues from God’s own promise, and God turns. It is the clearest prayer-changes-things scene in the Torah.

Hardest for insideGod offered Moses a “great nation” instead; if the offer was real, it conflicts with the oath to Abraham, and if not real, then God was testing Moses.

Numbers 14

Moses intercedes again

Twelve spies enter Canaan. Ten bring back fear: giants, walled cities. The people weep all night and say it would have been better to die in Egypt. Caleb and Joshua say, “Do not fear.” The people pick up stones. God says, “How long will this people despise me?” and again offers to strike them and make Moses a greater nation. Moses pleads and recites the Lord’s own words from Exodus 34. God answers: “I have pardoned, according to your word” (ESV). But the generation will not enter the land. They will wander forty years, one year for each day of the spying, until the adults die.

Read from inside time

Moses prays; God pardons; and God still carries out a consequence. The result is a negotiated middle path that neither of them had at the start. This is a relationship in motion.

Read from outside time

God’s mercy and his discipline both come out of his settled character, and Moses prays with God’s own words, so he prays inside God’s will. The forty years were in the plan, and the journey still gets Israel to the land.

The Father angleA Father who forgives the whole household but does not take away the consequence, the way a parent does with a child who has done real damage.

Hardest for outside“according to your word” credits Moses’s prayer with the pardon.

Hardest for insideeven though the prayer was heard, the punishment still stood. Prayer got mercy, not a reversal.

Numbers 22–24

Balaam: “a son of man, that he should change his mind”

The king of Moab hires the prophet Balaam to curse Israel. God tells Balaam not to go. The king sends more princes and more money. God says, “rise, go with them,” but warns him to say only what he is told. On the road Balaam’s donkey sees the angel with the drawn sword three times and refuses to move. Balaam beats her. The donkey speaks. Balaam’s eyes are opened. Three times he stands on a hill and tries to curse; three times blessing comes out. He says, “God is not man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his mind. Has he said, and will he not do it?” (ESV). He sees a star coming out of Jacob.

Read from inside time

God’s answer to Balaam shifts from “You shall not go with them” to “go with them,” so even here God responds to persistence. Balaam’s line about “changing his mind” is true in the narrow sense that God does not make promises and then break them. It is not a ban on all responding.

Read from outside time

This is the other side’s proof text. God’s purposes for his people are fixed, and no one can reverse them with money or magic. God “does not change his mind” because he already knows what he will do, and what he has said to Abraham will come to pass.

The Father angleA Father’s promise to his children stands even when an enemy hires a prophet to curse them, and even when they don’t know what is being done.

Hardest for outside

Hardest for inside“or a son of man, that he should change his mind” is stated as a general truth about God. Hardest for the outside side: the same God told Balaam no, then yes, and the speech sits right between Exodus 32 and 1 Samuel 15 where God is said to relent and regret.

Kings and prophets

1 Samuel 2:30

“I promised... but now”

Eli the priest is old. His sons Hophni and Phinehas take the best meat from the sacrifices and sleep with the women at the tent door. Eli rebukes them weakly. A man of God comes to Eli with a message. The Lord says: “I promised that your house... should go in and out before me forever” (ESV). Then comes a turn: “but now the LORD declares: ‘Far be it from me’” (ESV). Those who honor him he will honor, and those who despise him will be lightly esteemed. Eli’s house will lose its place, his sons will die on one day, and God will raise up a faithful priest.

Read from inside time

This is a promise God made and then withdrew because the people it was made to broke faith. The promise was “forever,” but with an assumed condition: that they stay faithful. God says “I promised... but now,” which sounds like a God whose actions follow from what people do.

Read from outside time

God’s promise was always conditional on faithfulness, as the verse itself explains: “those who honor me I will honor.” Nothing was withdrawn. The promise was a real offer, and its terms were met by Eli’s sons, who despised the Lord. God also knew, and had told Eli’s line through earlier prophecy, how it would end.

The Father angleA Father’s promise to a house is not a blank check to the children who dishonor him.

Hardest for outsidethe word “now” marks a change from what had been promised.

Hardest for insidethe principle that God honors those who honor him is as old as the law, so the “forever” had a hidden condition that was visible all along.

1 Samuel 13:13–14 and 15

Saul’s kingdom and “I regret”

Saul is waiting at Gilgal for Samuel to come offer the sacrifice before battle. Samuel is late. The men are slipping away. Saul offers the burnt offering himself. Samuel arrives and says, “You have done foolishly.” The Lord “would have established your kingdom over Israel forever. But now your kingdom shall not continue” (ESV). God has sought “a man after his own heart” (ESV). Later, in chapter 15, Saul spares the best of the Amalekite animals against God’s order. God says to Samuel, “I regret that I have made Saul king” (ESV). Samuel is angry and cries to the Lord all night. He tells Saul that “the Glory of Israel will not lie or have regret, for he is not a man, that he should have regret” (ESV).

Read from inside time

“Would have established your kingdom over Israel forever” is a conditional statement about a future that did not happen. The kingdom was Saul’s to keep. God regrets, and then in the same chapter the text says again that God is “not a man, that he should have regret.” That tension is the heart of the passage: God’s regret is real, and his word to Saul is also fixed.

Read from outside time

The two uses of the word in one chapter show that the first describes God’s response in time to Saul’s disobedience, and the second describes God’s unchangeable decision that the kingdom is torn from him. God had already prepared David, and he had spoken about a king from Judah in Genesis 49. The regret is feeling, not a surprise.

The Father angleA Father’s grief over a son he raised up and who failed, and a prophet who stays up all night in sorrow with him.

Hardest for both: the same Hebrew verb, said to be true of God in verse 11 and denied of him in verse 29. Neither side can skip either verse.

1 Samuel 23:1–13

Keilah and what would happen if David stayed

The Philistines are raiding the town of Keilah. David asks the Lord, “Shall I go and attack these Philistines?” and the Lord says go. David’s men are afraid, so he asks again, and God says go. David rescues the town. Saul hears David is in a walled city and thinks, “God has given him into my hand.” David learns Saul is coming. He asks the priest to bring the ephod and prays: “Will Saul come down as your servant has heard? ... will the men of Keilah surrender me and my men into the hand of Saul?” The Lord says, “He will come down... They will surrender you” (ESV). David and his six hundred men leave. Saul hears and calls off the trip.

Read from inside time

This is a favorite for the open view. God tells David what Saul and the men of Keilah would do if David stayed. Because David left, they did not do it. God knows what would have happened, and he knows it as a conditional possibility; it never became actual. The future has live branches, and God tells David which one leads where.

Read from outside time

God’s answer shows his knowledge of what free creatures would do under circumstances that never come about. That is the point: God knows the paths not taken. This is exactly what the other side calls “middle knowledge” or knowledge of counterfactuals. God also told David things so that David could act, and David’s departure was part of how God protected him.

The Father angleA Father who answers a hunted son’s questions one at a time and gets him out of the city before the trap shuts.

Hardest for outsideHardest for the outside side, if it holds that the whole future is a fixed line: this passage speaks of a future that is explicitly not going to happen.

Hardest for insideGod knew exactly what the men of Keilah would do, including the “would” of their free choice.

2 Samuel 12

David’s child and “who knows”

David takes Bathsheba, kills her husband Uriah, and thinks the matter is closed. The prophet Nathan tells him the story of a rich man who takes a poor man’s only lamb. David burns with anger, and Nathan says, “You are the man!” (ESV). David confesses, “I have sinned against the LORD.” Nathan says the Lord has put away his sin, but the child will die. The child becomes ill. David fasts, lies on the ground all night, and will not eat. On the seventh day the child dies. David gets up, washes, worships, and eats. His servants are puzzled. David says: “While the child was still alive, I fasted and wept, for I said, ‘Who knows whether the LORD will be gracious to me, that the child may live?’ But now he is dead” (ESV).

Read from inside time

David prays as if the outcome were open. “Who knows” is an honest hope that God might relent. David had been told the child will die and prays anyway, and the text lets us feel that prayer might have mattered. God is the kind of God who might be gracious.

Read from outside time

David prays because God told him to seek him, not because he can see the plan. The child dies because God said it would. David’s “who knows” is the right attitude of a creature who does not know the decree. He prays and then submits.

The Father angleA grieving father who begs for his child’s life, and a God who also loses a child in the end. David gets up and says, “I shall go to him, but he will not return to me.”

Hardest for outside

Hardest for insideGod had already said, through Nathan, that the child would die, and it did. Hardest for the outside side: David’s seven days of prayer are not shown as foolish; the text lets “who knows” stand.

2 Samuel 24 / 1 Chronicles 21

The census and the stopped plague

God’s anger burns against Israel, and David counts his fighting men. Joab protests. David feels guilty after the count and confesses. Through the prophet Gad, God gives David three choices: three years of famine, three months fleeing his enemies, or three days of plague. David chooses to fall into the Lord’s hands, “for his mercy is great.” The plague kills seventy thousand. The angel stretches out his hand over Jerusalem, “the LORD relented from the calamity and said to the angel who was working destruction among the people, ‘It is enough; now stay your hand’” (ESV). David sees the angel by the threshing floor of Araunah, buys the place, and builds an altar. The plague stops. That place becomes the site of the temple.

Read from inside time

In the middle of an act of judgment, God stops. The text says plainly that he “relented from the calamity.” The judgment was heading to Jerusalem, and God turned it back. God is a judge who decides when enough is enough, in real time.

Read from outside time

The place God stops is the place where he will put the temple. The sparing of Jerusalem serves a plan that runs through the whole story of Israel. The regret is God’s compassion expressed in time.

The Father angleDavid says, “Let us fall into the hand of the LORD, for his mercy is great,” a son choosing his Father’s hand over a stranger’s.

Hardest for both: the two accounts disagree on who moved David. Samuel says the Lord’s anger incited David; Chronicles says Satan did. The sovereignty-and-guilt question is inside the story itself.

1 Kings 13:2

Josiah named three hundred years ahead

King Jeroboam stands at the altar at Bethel, burning incense. A man of God comes from Judah and cries against the altar: “O altar, altar, thus says the LORD: ‘Behold, a son shall be born to the house of David, Josiah by name, and he shall sacrifice on you the priests of the high places’” (ESV). The king stretches out his hand and orders him seized. The hand withers. The altar splits and the ashes pour out. The king asks the man to pray for him, and the hand is restored. Three centuries later, in 2 Kings 23, King Josiah tears down that altar and burns bones on it, and the text points back to the man of God’s word.

Read from inside time

Some on this side say God can foretell by his own decision: he declares what he will do and then does it. A specific name is a promise God made, kept by raising up that very king. It need not be fixed by a timeless view.

Read from outside time

A name given three hundred years before the birth is the clearest case of detailed foreknowledge. God sees all of time, including a boy not yet born, and speaks of him. The prophecy proves that God’s knowledge reaches into the future.

The Father angleA Father who is working generations ahead, setting up a faithful son to clean up what an earlier line ruined.

Hardest for outside

Hardest for insidea name, a deed and a place, given across three hundred years, through thousands of free choices. Hardest for the outside side: the withered hand heals when the king asks, so the same chapter shows God responding to a request.

1 Kings 21:27–29

Ahab humbles himself and the disaster is postponed

King Ahab and Jezebel have Naboth killed to take his vineyard. Elijah meets Ahab in the field and says, “I will bring disaster upon you” (ESV). Ahab’s house will end, and dogs will eat Jezebel. Then something unexpected: “when Ahab heard those words, he tore his clothes and put sackcloth on his flesh and fasted... and went about dejectedly” (ESV). The word of the Lord comes to Elijah: “Have you seen how Ahab has humbled himself before me? Because he has humbled himself before me, I will not bring the disaster in his days; but in his son’s days I will bring the disaster upon his house” (ESV).

Read from inside time

A declared judgment is softened because a wicked man shows humility for a day. This is a clean picture of God responding: he sees, he evaluates, he shifts the timing. It looks like prayer without words, and it worked.

Read from outside time

All warnings of judgment carry a built-in condition of repentance, as Jeremiah 18 says. God knew Ahab would humble himself briefly, and he knew the final outcome, which does fall on Ahab’s son and then on Ahab himself in chapter 22. The delay is a real mercy inside a plan that still ends in judgment.

The Father angleEven the worst son’s small sorrow gets noticed. A Father who reacts to a flicker of repentance.

Hardest for outsidethe Lord asks Elijah, “Have you seen?”, pointing to Ahab’s act as the reason for the change.

Hardest for insidethe humility did not last, and Ahab died in battle in the very next chapter, so the postponement bought little.

1 Kings 22

Micaiah and the lying spirit

Ahab and King Jehoshaphat of Judah plan to attack Ramoth-gilead. Four hundred prophets say, “Go up, for the LORD will give it into the hand of the king.” Jehoshaphat wants one more voice. Micaiah is brought, is told to agree with the others, and at first says sarcastically, “Go up and triumph.” Ahab demands the truth. Micaiah describes a scene in heaven: the Lord asks, “Who will entice Ahab, that he may go up and fall at Ramoth-gilead?” One spirit says, “I will go out, and will be a lying spirit in the mouth of all his prophets” (ESV). The Lord says, “You are to entice him, and you shall succeed.” Ahab throws Micaiah in prison, goes to battle in disguise, and is killed by an arrow shot “at random” (ESV).

Read from inside time

The throne room scene shows God asking for ideas, hearing options, and choosing one. It reads as a conversation, a council that works through a problem. The result is a chosen course for a king who had already refused the truth many times.

Read from outside time

God decreed Ahab’s end in advance, through Elijah in chapter 21, and here he uses the king’s own appetite for flattery to carry it out. The random arrow shows that nothing is random with God. God ordains the means as well as the end.

The Father angleMicaiah is the faithful voice who tells the hard truth to a king who wants a comfortable one, the way a true parent does.

Hardest for outsideGod sends a lying spirit, and that looks like him causing deception.

Hardest for insideMicaiah announces the death in advance, and the king, hiding in disguise, is still killed by a stray arrow that finds the gap in his armor.

2 Kings 13:14–19

The arrows and “you should have struck five or six times”

Elisha is dying. King Joash of Israel comes to his bed and weeps. Elisha tells him to take a bow and arrows. He puts his hands on the king’s hands and tells him to open the window to the east and shoot. “The LORD’s arrow of victory” (ESV). Then Elisha says, “Take the arrows,” and tells the king to strike the ground. Joash strikes three times and stops. The man of God is angry: “You should have struck five or six times” (ESV), he says, and then Syria would have been finished; now Joash will win only three times. Joash wins three times, as predicted.

Read from inside time

The size of the victory depends on the size of the king’s response. A half-hearted strike gets a partial win. The text treats the outcome as something the king could have changed. It is the clearest “the future is shaped by what you do” scene in the prophets.

Read from outside time

The three strikes reveal the king’s heart and the extent of his faith, and the prophecy matches the result exactly: three victories. Elisha’s anger is the prophet’s grief. God knew what Joash would do, and the lesson is aimed at the reader: do not stop short when God gives you a sign.

The Father angleAn old spiritual father, a prophet whom the king calls “my father,” puts his hands over the king’s hands to teach him how to shoot.

Hardest for outside“you should have struck five or six times” speaks of a better result that was available and missed.

Hardest for insidethe prophet predicts exactly three wins, so was the number open or set?

2 Kings 20 / Isaiah 38

Hezekiah’s fifteen years

Hezekiah falls ill. Isaiah comes with a message: “Set your house in order, for you shall die, you shall not recover” (ESV). Hezekiah turns his face to the wall and prays with weeping: “Now, O LORD, please remember how I have walked before you in faithfulness.” Before Isaiah has left the middle court, the word of the Lord comes to him again: “I have heard your prayer; I have seen your tears. Behold, I will heal you... I will add fifteen years to your life” (ESV). A sundial’s shadow moves back ten steps as a sign. Hezekiah recovers. In those fifteen years his son Manasseh is born, who later does terrible evil.

Read from inside time

A plain, unconditional-sounding word, “you shall die,” is reversed by prayer within minutes. God says he heard and saw. This is the proof text for the claim that prayer changes what God does.

Read from outside time

The first word was a warning aimed at drawing out Hezekiah’s prayer, and God intended to heal him all along. A sentence of death was God’s way of getting a king to cry out. The fifteen years were in the plan, as was the sign of the shadow.

The Father angle“I have seen your tears” is the line that makes it personal. A Father who is moved by a grown son’s crying.

Hardest for outsidethe first message is flat and has no “unless.”

Hardest for insidethe extra years included a foolish act (showing the treasury to Babylon) and the birth of the worst king in Judah’s history. Did the prayer really bring good?

Isaiah 44:28–45:1

Cyrus named ahead

God speaks of the temple in ruins and says of Cyrus, “He is my shepherd, and he shall fulfill all my purpose, saying of Jerusalem, ‘She shall be built,’ and of the temple, ‘Your foundation shall be laid’” (ESV). Then: “Thus says the LORD to his anointed, to Cyrus, whose right hand I have grasped” (ESV). He says he calls Cyrus by name, “though you do not know me.” Isaiah speaks about 150 years before Cyrus lived. The prophecy comes in the middle of a long argument that Israel’s God, unlike the idols, can tell what is coming.

Read from inside time

Some on this side say God can see far down the road because he sets the large moves himself: he promises to raise up a ruler who will free the exiles, and then he works through many events so it happens. The name is a promise, kept by God’s hand.

Read from outside time

The text itself makes foreknowledge the proof of God’s deity: “Who told this long ago?” God sees what has not yet happened and declares it. A pagan king, a name, a decree, all stated beforehand, is exactly the kind of evidence this side points to.

The Father angleGod calls a stranger “my shepherd” for the sake of his scattered children, working through a man who does not know him in order to bring them home.

Hardest for outside

Hardest for insidea specific name and deeds, a century and a half out, in a text that says this is what makes God different from idols. Hardest for the outside side: Isaiah also says the Lord says, “My glory I will not give to another,” yet he gives credit for the exile’s end to a man who did not know him.

Jeremiah 18

The potter

God sends Jeremiah to the potter’s house. The potter is working at the wheel. The vessel he is making is spoiled in his hands, “and he reworked it into another vessel, as it seemed good to the potter to do” (ESV). Then God speaks: “Can I not do with you as this potter has done?” Then comes the rule: if God declares a nation will be destroyed, “and if that nation... turns from its evil, I will relent of the disaster that I intended to do to it” (ESV). And the other way: if a nation does evil, God will relent of the good he planned. The people answer, “That is in vain! We will follow our own plans.”

Read from inside time

This is the clearest statement of how God works with people: it is his stated policy. His words of judgment and blessing depend on how a nation responds. The clay can change what the potter makes.

Read from outside time

The rule explains how prophecy works. Every threat has an implied condition. The potter is sovereign over the clay, and in the story he decides what to make of the spoiled vessel. God’s “relenting” is the use of his authority, not a loss of it. The potter picture is the same as Romans 9.

The Father angleA craftsman with a ruined pot is not angry at the clay; he remakes it. God treats his people as work still being shaped.

Hardest for outsidethe rule is stated as a general principle with “if... I will relent,” about the future of nations.

Hardest for insidethe picture emphasizes that the potter is in control and the clay is not choosing.

Jeremiah 26 and Micah 3:12

A prophecy that did not happen because of repentance

In the temple court, Jeremiah says that if the people do not listen, God will make this house like Shiloh, and the city a curse. The priests and prophets seize him: “You shall die!” The princes sit as judges. Jeremiah says, “mend your ways... and the LORD will relent of the disaster that he has pronounced against you” (ESV). Then some elders stand up and remember Micah of Moresheth, who said in Hezekiah’s day: “Zion shall be plowed as a field” (Micah 3:12, ESV). They ask whether Hezekiah put him to death, or instead entreated the Lord, “and did not the LORD relent of the disaster that he had pronounced against them?” (Jeremiah 26:19, ESV). Jeremiah is spared.

Read from inside time

A flat prophecy of the city’s end, spoken in God’s name, was not carried out because the king and people repented. The Bible itself says so. The elders treat this as proof that Micah was a true prophet.

Read from outside time

Prophecies of judgment are calls to repent. They are written as plain statements because they work as warnings. The fact that Micah’s word did not come true in Hezekiah’s day shows that conditions are always attached, spoken or unspoken. It does not show that God lacked knowledge.

The Father angleA Father’s threat is often a plea, and the best outcome is that he never has to carry it out.

Hardest for outsidethe elders read a prophecy with no stated condition as an event that did not happen, and call that a mark of a true prophet.

Hardest for insideJeremiah’s own later prophecies, including exile, did come true, so the pattern is that it depends on the response.

Ezekiel 22:30

“I sought for a man”

God lists Jerusalem’s sins: bloodshed, bribes, oppression of widows and orphans, priests who ignore the law, prophets who whitewash, princes who tear their prey. Then the Lord says: “And I sought for a man among them who should build up the wall and stand in the breach before me for the land, that I should not destroy it, but I found none” (ESV). So he pours out his indignation. The picture is a city wall with a hole in it, and a defender who should stand there.

Read from inside time

God looked for an intercessor and did not find one. The verse implies that if one had stood in the gap, the outcome might have been different. Moses did stand there at Sinai. Here no one does.

Read from outside time

The search language is a vivid way of showing that the nation had no righteous advocate and that the judgment was just. Ezekiel is writing as God’s ordained instrument, and the real man in the breach, who comes later, is Christ. It is a literary picture, not a report of a failed search.

The Father angleA Father looking for one child to stand with him on the side of mercy, and finding none.

Hardest for outside“that I should not destroy it” says what the man could have achieved.

Hardest for insideEzekiel’s own book promises the return and a new heart, all fixed for the future.

Daniel 4

Nebuchadnezzar

The king of Babylon dreams of a great tree cut down to a stump. Daniel tells him that the tree is the king, who will lose his mind and eat grass like an ox for seven periods, “until you know that the Most High rules the kingdom of men and gives it to whom he will” (ESV). Daniel adds advice: “break off your sins by practicing righteousness... that there may perhaps be a lengthening of your prosperity” (ESV). A year later the king stands on his palace roof and boasts about “this great Babylon, which I have built.” While the words are in his mouth, a voice falls from heaven. He is driven out and lives like an animal. At the end he lifts his eyes and his sanity returns. He praises God, whose dominion is everlasting: “none can stay his hand or say to him, ‘What have you done?’” (ESV).

Read from inside time

Daniel’s advice has an “if” in it: a chance to avoid the judgment, or at least lengthen the king’s good years. The year of waiting looks like a window of mercy. The king’s own pride closes it.

Read from outside time

The chapter ends with the strongest sovereignty statement in the Old Testament. God rules kings and does as he wills. The humbling was set, the period was set (“seven periods of time”), and the end, restoration, was set. Daniel’s advice was a call, and the king chose not to heed it.

The Father angleA Father who lets a proud son crawl in the dirt until he looks up, and then gives him back his throne.

Hardest for outsidethe “perhaps” in Daniel’s advice.

Hardest for inside“he does according to his will among the host of heaven,” with nothing said about God waiting on anyone.

Daniel 9–10

Daniel’s prayer and the answer delayed twenty-one days

Daniel reads in Jeremiah that the exile will last seventy years. He takes it as a reason to pray, not a reason to relax. He fasts in sackcloth and confesses the sins of his people. “While I was speaking and praying... the man Gabriel... came to me.” The angel says, “At the beginning of your pleas for mercy a word went out” (ESV). In chapter 10 Daniel mourns for three weeks. A radiant messenger comes: “your words have been heard, and I have come because of your words. The prince of the kingdom of Persia withstood me twenty-one days” (ESV). Michael, one of the chief princes, came to help him.

Read from inside time

Daniel prays because he read a promise. The answer is sent at once but is held up by a conflict in the unseen world. Prayer opens real moves in a real struggle, and the timing is real.

Read from outside time

God had given the seventy-year promise, and Daniel’s prayer was the means that God ordained to bring it about. The delay shows the war behind history and teaches that the answer arrives, though not on a human clock. God is not hindered; the story shows creatures serving his purposes.

The Father angleThe words “your words have been heard” the first day are a Father’s reassurance to a son who has been wondering for three weeks.

Hardest for outsidea messenger of God is “withstood” for twenty-one days.

Hardest for insideDaniel prays because God promised the end of the exile; the prayer asks for what is already given.

Jonah

The whole book

God tells Jonah to go to Nineveh and cry against it. Jonah runs the other way, boards a ship to Tarshish, and sleeps below deck while a storm hits. The sailors cast lots, the lot falls on Jonah, and he says to throw him in. They do, and the sea goes calm. “The LORD appointed a great fish to swallow up Jonah” (ESV). From inside, Jonah prays. The fish vomits him on land. He goes to Nineveh and preaches one short line: “Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown!” (ESV). The king and city repent in sackcloth, even the animals. “When God saw what they did... God relented of the disaster that he had said he would do to them, and he did not do it” (ESV). Jonah is furious: “I knew that you are a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love” (ESV). He sulks outside the city. God grows a plant, then sends a worm, and ends with a question about the city’s 120,000 people “who do not know their right hand from their left, and also much cattle.”

Read from inside time

A prophet announces a flat judgment with a date. The city repents, and God relents. Jonah says he knew God would. This is the book-length proof that God’s threats depend on the response, and that God wants it that way.

Read from outside time

Jonah’s complaint shows that he knew the pattern: God’s warnings are meant to bring repentance. God was never going to destroy a city that repented, and Jonah knew this from Exodus 34. The book is built around God’s unchanging character: mercy to the repentant is the constant. What changes is the situation, and God’s actions follow his nature.

The Father angleThe ending is a Father arguing with a pouting older son who resents the mercy shown to others. It is an open question, with no reply from Jonah.

Hardest for outside“When God saw what they did... God relented.” The book ties the change to what the Ninevites did.

Hardest for insideJonah’s line shows that this “relenting” is the very nature of God, known in advance. If it was predictable, is anything really open?

Wisdom

Job 1–2 and 38–42

The wager and the whirlwind

Job is a good man with seven sons, three daughters, and large herds. In heaven, the Lord asks the accuser, “Have you considered my servant Job?” The accuser says Job only fears God because he is paid. The Lord says, “Behold, all that he has is in your hand” (ESV). In one day, raiders and fire and wind take his livestock, servants, and all ten children. Job says, “The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD” (ESV). Then boils. His friends say he must have sinned. Job says he did not and demands a hearing. For thirty-seven chapters they argue. Then the Lord answers from a whirlwind: “Where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth?” (ESV). Job puts his hand over his mouth. The Lord restores him and rebukes the friends for not speaking rightly about him.

Read from inside time

Heaven’s opening scene is a conversation that unfolds in order: the Lord asks, the accuser answers, a limit is set, then a second round. God lets real suffering enter Job’s life, to see what he would do. The story is a drama with a real outcome, a faithfulness proved in time.

Read from outside time

The whirlwind answer says that God’s purposes are beyond the creature’s view. Nothing happened to Job outside God’s rule: the accuser acts on a leash. The point of the book is that the right response to suffering is trust, not understanding, since Job never learns about the wager.

The Father angleAfter the storm, Job gets his children back (new ones) and prays for his friends. The Lord is described as “compassionate and merciful” in the New Testament’s summary of Job’s story (James 5:11).

Hardest for outside

Hardest for insidethe Lord points the accuser to Job, and the first chapter starts with God sure that Job will hold. Hardest for the outside side: God never explains himself, and the friends, who defended God’s justice with neat arguments, are the ones he rebukes.

Esther 4:14

“Who knows”

The decree goes out to destroy all Jews in the empire on a single day. Mordecai tears his clothes and tells Queen Esther to go to the king. She says that anyone who enters the king’s court without being called may be put to death. Mordecai answers: “If you keep silent at this time, relief and deliverance will rise for the Jews from another place, but you and your father’s house will perish. And who knows whether you have not come to the kingdom for such a time as this?” (ESV). Esther asks all the Jews to fast three days and says, “If I perish, I perish.” She goes in. The king extends the scepter. The book never uses God’s name, and yet the reversal is complete.

Read from inside time

Mordecai speaks of rescue coming “from another place” if Esther refuses, so God’s goal is secure but her part is a real choice with real stakes. “Who knows” is honest uncertainty about God’s design. The hidden God works through ordinary coincidences and courage.

Read from outside time

Mordecai is confident that deliverance will come, which is faith in a promise, and his “who knows” is the human side of providence. God is silent in the book because he rules in the details: the queen is dethroned, a Jewish girl is chosen, and the king cannot sleep on the right night. The whole thing was arranged, and Esther’s courage was part of the arrangement.

The Father angleA hidden Father who is never mentioned but cares for his scattered children in a foreign land.

Hardest for outsideMordecai says God’s help will come “from another place” and Esther would “perish,” so her choice carries a threat.

Hardest for insideMordecai’s confidence that deliverance is certain.

Kings and prophets

Hosea 1–3 and 11

The betrayed husband and the father’s heart

God tells Hosea to marry a woman who will be unfaithful. Gomer bears children, whose names are painful: Jezreel, No Mercy, Not My People. She leaves for other lovers. God tells Hosea to go and love her again, and he buys her back for fifteen shekels of silver and some barley. Then in chapter 11 the picture changes: “When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son” (ESV). The Father taught Ephraim to walk, “took them up by their arms,” and bent down to feed them. But they went away. The judgment is pronounced. Then God stops himself: “How can I give you up, O Ephraim? ... My heart recoils within me; my compassion grows warm and tender. I will not execute my burning anger” (ESV).

Read from inside time

This is a God whose heart goes back and forth. He announces judgment and then feels his compassion rise and changes the verdict. “My heart recoils within me” is a literal turning in his own inner life. Love, not rule, is what makes him adjust.

Read from outside time

The marriage and the father pictures are metaphors for a love that does not give up. God’s decision to spare Ephraim is rooted in who he is: “for I am God and not a man, the Holy One in your midst” (ESV), a line in the very same verse. The inner struggle is shown to us so we can see the depth of his love.

The Father angleThis is the passage on God as Father: teaching a toddler to walk, lifting him to the cheek, stooping to feed him, and being unable to give him up.

Hardest for outsidethe heart “recoils” and the Father debates himself.

Hardest for insidethe reason given for not destroying is that he is “God and not a man,” which sounds like a defense of an unchanging nature.

Gospels

Matthew 11:20–24

Tyre and Sidon would have repented

Jesus begins to scold the towns where he did most of his miracles: Chorazin, Bethsaida, Capernaum. “Woe to you, Chorazin! Woe to you, Bethsaida! For if the mighty works done in you had been done in Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented long ago in sackcloth and ashes” (ESV). He says it will be more bearable for Tyre and Sidon on judgment day. Of Capernaum he says: “if the mighty works done in you had been done in Sodom, it would have remained until this day” (ESV).

Read from inside time

Jesus states what would have happened under conditions that did not occur. It is a plain statement that God knows what free people would do in different circumstances, a theme the open view and the middle view both note. It also shows that the future has “if” branches.

Read from outside time

Here the classical side sees God’s complete knowledge, including of possible worlds. God chose not to send those miracles to Tyre and Sidon, and that choice was part of his plan. It also shows the weight of privilege: more light, more responsibility.

The Father angleJesus speaks with grief, not triumph, over towns he cared about. He weeps over cities elsewhere.

Hardest for outsideHardest for the outside side, if it holds that everything unfolds as God decreed: if God knew Tyre would repent with the miracles, why not give them?

Hardest for insideJesus does not say the world could have been otherwise; he says these towns were given the light and said no.

Matthew 15:21–28

The Canaanite woman changes the outcome

Jesus goes to the region of Tyre and Sidon. A Canaanite woman shouts after him, “Have mercy on me, O Lord, Son of David; my daughter is severely oppressed by a demon” (ESV). He does not answer a word. His disciples urge him to send her away. He says, “I was sent only to the lost sheep of the house of Israel.” She kneels: “Lord, help me.” He answers, “It is not right to take the children’s bread and throw it to the dogs.” She says, “Yes, Lord, yet even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from their masters’ table.” Then Jesus says, “O woman, great is your faith! Let it be done for you as you desire.” Her daughter is healed that hour.

Read from inside time

Jesus starts out saying no and ends by saying yes because of her persistence. The conversation moves him. It shows that a human voice can change what the Lord does, and that Jesus himself is a living participant who is moved by faith.

Read from outside time

The delay was a test that drew out her faith and taught the disciples. Jesus knew from the start that he would heal her, and the “no” was a way to reveal her heart. His mission to Israel first is real, and the exception shows the door to the nations opening, as Genesis 12 had said.

The Father angleA mother’s daughter is healed because a mother would not go away. The Father hears a mother’s cry.

Hardest for outsideJesus’s final statement, “Let it be done for you as you desire,” credits her words.

Hardest for insidethe stated purpose of his mission to Israel shows a plan in place, which she does not overturn so much as stretch.

Matthew 26:36–46

Gethsemane

Jesus takes his disciples to a garden. He says, “My soul is very sorrowful, even to death” (ESV). He goes a little farther, falls on his face, and prays: “My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will” (ESV). He returns and finds the disciples asleep. He prays again, the same words. A third time. Then: “Rise, let us be going; see, my betrayer is at hand.” Judas arrives with a crowd and swords.

Read from inside time

Jesus asks the Father if there is another way. “If it be possible” means that for the Son in that moment, it was an open question. He prays three times, as a man who wants a different path and yields. The relationship between Father and Son runs through time with a real struggle in it.

Read from outside time

Jesus had said many times that he would be handed over and killed, and he set his face to Jerusalem. The prayer shows the full reality of his human will and the weight of what he was about to bear. “If it be possible” is the language of a man who knows the answer and still feels the dread. The decree, “not as I will but as you will,” is the same cross planned before time.

The Father angleThe Son calls God “My Father” in the garden. The cup is not passed. The Father’s silence here is the hardest thing in the Gospel.

Hardest for outsidethe Son asks for a different outcome and does not get it, but the asking is real and the question “if it be possible” is real.

Hardest for insideJesus said earlier “for this purpose I have come to this hour,” so he knew the road.

Mark 6:5–6

“He could do no mighty work there”

Jesus comes to his hometown and teaches in the synagogue. People are astonished and then offended: “Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary?” (ESV). Jesus says, “A prophet is not without honor, except in his hometown.” Then the text says: “And he could do no mighty work there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people and healed them” (ESV). And he “marveled because of their unbelief” (ESV).

Read from inside time

Mark says “could not.” Unbelief limited what Jesus did. He also “marveled,” which is a sign of being surprised by what people do. This is the plainest New Testament example of human response shaping divine action.

Read from outside time

“Could not” is a moral inability, not a lack of power: Jesus would not do mighty works where they would be scorned. Matthew’s version (13:58) says “he did not do many mighty works there.” “Marveled” belongs to the real human mind of Jesus, which grew and was amazed, as Luke 2:52 says. The Son took on a human way of knowing in time.

The Father angleA son who comes home to the people who raised him and finds them closed. A hometown is where it hurts the most.

Hardest for outsidethe verb “could not,” and the surprise.

Hardest for insidehe still healed some, and Matthew softens the wording, so the limit looks chosen.

Luke 15

The lost sheep, the lost coin, and the two sons

Pharisees grumble that Jesus welcomes sinners and eats with them. He tells three stories. A shepherd has a hundred sheep and loses one; he leaves the ninety-nine and goes after it until he finds it, puts it on his shoulders, and calls his friends to celebrate. A woman loses one of her ten silver coins, lights a lamp, sweeps the house, and searches until she finds it. Then a man has two sons. The younger asks for his share, goes to a far country, wastes it, and ends up feeding pigs. “But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him” (ESV). The father puts a robe and ring on him and kills the fattened calf. The older brother stands outside, angry, and will not come in. The father goes out to him too: “Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours” (ESV). The story ends without telling us if he goes in.

Read from inside time

All three stories are about searching and waiting in time. The shepherd goes out, the woman sweeps, the father watches the road. When the son comes home, the joy is new. The father saw the son “while he was still a long way off,” which suggests he had been looking.

Read from outside time

The stories show the heart of God toward the lost, and nothing in them says God was unaware of where the lost thing was. The shepherd knew where the sheep was; the story just tells the search from the human side. Salvation is the Father’s initiative at every step: he runs first.

The Father angleThis is the passage on God as Father. He gives the inheritance, lets the son go, watches the road, runs, kisses, and then goes out the door again for the older son.

Hardest for outsidethe father’s “running” is a response to the son’s return.

Hardest for insideall three are stories of finding what was lost, which is God’s initiative from first to last.

Luke 18:1–8

The persistent widow

Jesus tells his disciples a story “to the effect that they ought always to pray and not lose heart” (ESV). A judge in a city neither fears God nor respects people. A widow keeps coming to him: “Give me justice against my adversary.” For a while he refuses. Then he says to himself, He gives in, saying she will “beat me down by her continual coming” (ESV). Jesus applies it: “Will not God give justice to his elect, who cry to him day and night?” (ESV). He ends by asking whether the Son of Man will find faith on earth.

Read from inside time

Persistent prayer gets results. The story links the widow’s repeated coming to the judge’s change of mind. Jesus tells it so that we will pray without giving up.

Read from outside time

This is an argument from the lesser to the greater: if even an unjust judge gives in, how much more a loving God will answer. God is not like the judge. Nobody has to wear God down. The point is about persevering faith, and God has ordained prayer as the way his answers come.

The Father angleThe judge is the opposite of the Father. The parable works by contrast: a God who is not reluctant.

Hardest for outside

Hardest for insideJesus says God will answer “speedily,” yet the widow’s story is about delay. Hardest for the outside side: the whole point of the story is that her coming back changed the outcome.

John 11

Lazarus: Jesus weeps knowing he will raise him

Lazarus is sick. Mary and Martha send word. Jesus says, “This illness does not lead to death. It is for the glory of God” (ESV). Then he stays two days where he is. He tells his disciples that Lazarus has died and says, “for your sake I am glad that I was not there, so that you may believe.” When he arrives, Lazarus has been in the tomb four days. Martha says, “Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died.” Mary says the same thing. She is weeping. Jesus “was deeply moved in his spirit and greatly troubled.” He asks where they laid him, and “Jesus wept” (ESV). Some say, “Could not he who opened the eyes of the blind man also have kept this man from dying?” At the tomb he prays, and cries out, “Lazarus, come out.” The dead man walks out, still wrapped in his grave clothes.

Read from inside time

Jesus weeps even though he knows what he will do in a few minutes. Grief is not a mistake. God enters the sorrow of the moment as a real feeling in real time, with the mourners.

Read from outside time

Jesus knew from the start. He stayed two days on purpose, and told the disciples why. His tears show the human heart of the Son, who shares the sorrow. The delay was part of a plan to show the glory of God and to point to his own resurrection.

The Father angleJesus thanks the Father: “I knew that you always hear me.” The Son and Father are in constant contact, and this prayer is a model of trust.

Hardest for outside

Hardest for insideJesus knew Lazarus was dead and would be raised, and still wept. Hardest for the outside side: the weeping and “deeply moved” are strong words, and the question the crowd asks, “Could not he who opened the eyes of the blind man also have kept this man from dying?” stays hanging.

Matthew 26:34 and John 13

Peter’s denial and Judas foretold

At the supper Jesus washes his disciples’ feet. He says, “One of you will betray me.” He hands a piece of bread to Judas, who goes out; “and it was night” (ESV). Peter says he will lay down his life. Jesus answers, “Truly, I tell you, this very night, before the rooster crows, you will deny me three times” (ESV). Peter insists he will not. In the high priest’s courtyard, a servant girl, then another, then bystanders, ask if he is with Jesus. Three times he says no. The rooster crows. Jesus turns and looks at him, and Peter goes out and weeps bitterly. Later, by the lake, Jesus asks him three times, “Do you love me?” and gives him his work back.

Read from inside time

Jesus read these men well. A close friend who knows his disciples can predict what a particular person will do under pressure without having every future move fixed. Peter’s denial was still his own free act, and so was Judas’s. Jesus’ warning also left room for Peter to be restored.

Read from outside time

A prediction with a number and a time of night is detailed foreknowledge. Jesus says of Judas that it was to fulfill Scripture (Psalm 41). The prediction came true to the letter, though Peter swore it would not.

The Father angleJesus says to Peter in Luke 22, “I have prayed for you that your faith may not fail.” A Father’s prayer goes ahead of a son’s failure.

Hardest for outside

Hardest for insidethe detail of “before the rooster crows, you will deny me three times,” told to a man who insisted he would not. Hardest for the outside side: Jesus prays that Peter’s faith will not fail, as though his prayer would make a difference.

Church and the end

Acts 2:23 and 4:27–28

The cross: plan and guilt together

On Pentecost, Peter tells a crowd in Jerusalem that Jesus of Nazareth was “delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God, you crucified and killed by the hands of lawless men” (ESV). The people are cut to the heart and ask what to do. Peter says to repent and be baptized. Three thousand do. Later, when the believers are threatened, they pray that Herod, Pilate, the Gentiles and Israel gathered against Jesus “to do whatever your hand and your plan had predestined to take place” (ESV).

Read from inside time

God had a plan and foreknew it, and the crowd was guilty. This side says God foresaw the shape of the plan, that humanity would reject the Son, and set a rescue in place for that. The people were free, and their freedom was real, since Peter calls them to repent.

Read from outside time

This is the central case. The most evil act in history was foreordained and also fully the guilt of those who did it. “According to the definite plan” and “by the hands of lawless men” sit in one sentence. God’s plan includes the free acts of sinners without making him the author of their sin.

The Father angleThe Father gave up his Son, according to a plan made before the world, and invited the killers to be forgiven.

Hardest for outside

Hardest for inside“predestined to take place” is strong. Hardest for the outside side: Peter calls the same people to repent, which presumes they could do otherwise.

Acts 27:22–31

Paul’s shipwreck: promise and “unless these men stay”

A ship carrying Paul to Rome is caught in a violent storm for fourteen days. All hope is gone. Paul stands up and says an angel of God stood by him last night: “Do not be afraid, Paul; you must stand before Caesar, and behold, God has granted you all those who sail with you” (ESV). Paul says, “I have faith in God that it will be exactly as I have been told.” On the fourteenth night, the sailors try to escape in the lifeboat. Paul tells the centurion: “Unless these men stay in the ship, you cannot be saved” (ESV). The soldiers cut the ropes of the boat and let it fall away. Everyone reaches land, the ship breaks apart, and all 276 are saved.

Read from inside time

God promised everyone would live. Paul still says they cannot be saved unless the sailors stay. The promise and the human action go together, and the action is a real condition. Prayer, faith and sailing skill are all in the same story.

Read from outside time

This is the model for how the outside view sees the means. God ordained the end and the means together. The promise was certain, and the sailors’ staying was how it was fulfilled. Paul’s warning was one of the means by which God kept his word.

The Father angleA Father who sends an angel to a frightened son in the dark and then gets him through, along with everyone near him.

Hardest for outside“you cannot be saved” unless they stay is stated as a condition on a promise God had made unconditionally.

Hardest for insidethe promise had no “if” in it, and the sailors’ attempt to flee was stopped by soldiers acting on Paul’s word.

Romans 9–11

Jacob and Esau, the potter, and Israel’s future

Paul says he has “great sorrow and unceasing anguish” for his own people who have not believed. He tells how God chose Isaac, not Ishmael, and Jacob, not Esau, “though they were not yet born and had done nothing either good or bad” (ESV). “Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated” (ESV). He recalls Pharaoh: “For this very purpose I have raised you up.” The question comes: “Why does he still find fault? For who can resist his will?” Paul answers: “Who are you, O man, to answer back to God? Will what is molded say to its molder, ‘Why have you made me like this?’” (ESV). The potter has the right to make some vessels for honor and some for dishonor. But Israel stumbled because they pursued righteousness by works, not by faith. Chapter 11 turns: God has not rejected his people. Branches were broken off, and wild branches grafted in. “And in this way all Israel will be saved.” Paul ends with praise: “Oh, the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God!”

Read from inside time

Paul is writing about corporate roles and God’s plan for nations. God’s choice of Jacob was about the line of the promise, not about each person’s eternal fate. The graft-in imagery has real conditions: “provided you continue in his kindness. Otherwise you too will be cut off” (11:22). God deals with branches in time.

Read from outside time

Here is the strongest classical text. God chose before birth, before any deed, “in order that God’s purpose of election might continue.” Mercy is not owed. The potter’s rights are absolute. The end of the section is a doxology to an unsearchable God, whose ways are past finding out.

The Father anglePaul’s heartbreak for his people and the picture of the olive tree show a Father who has not discarded his firstborn.

Hardest for outside

Hardest for inside“though they were not yet born and had done nothing either good or bad” and the potter. Hardest for the outside side: the warning to Gentiles, “otherwise you too will be cut off,” and Paul’s own unceasing anguish.

Revelation 3:5 and 22:19

Names blotted out

Jesus speaks to seven churches. To Sardis, a church with a reputation for being alive that is really dead, he says: “I will never blot his name out of the book of life. I will confess his name before my Father” (ESV). At the end of the book there is a warning: “if anyone takes away from the words of the book of this prophecy, God will take away his share in the tree of life and in the holy city” (ESV).

Read from inside time

A name in a book that can be blotted out suggests that standing with God is a living relationship that depends on staying faithful. The warnings to the churches are meant to be taken at face value.

Read from outside time

“I will never blot his name out” is a promise to the one who conquers. The warnings are the means by which God keeps his own safe: he warns them, and they persevere. In Revelation 17:8 names have been “written in the book of life from the foundation of the world.”

The Father angleThe Son says he will confess the name of the faithful “before my Father,” like a child introduced with pride.

Hardest for outsidethe book can be taken away from, and a share can be “taken.”

Hardest for insidenames that were written from before the foundation of the world.

Revelation 5 and 13:8

The Lamb slain from the foundation

In John’s vision, a scroll sealed with seven seals lies in the hand of the One on the throne. No one can open it. John weeps. An elder says, “Weep no more; behold, the Lion of the tribe of Judah... has conquered.” John turns and sees “a Lamb standing, as though it had been slain” (ESV). The Lamb takes the scroll, and every creature sings, “Worthy is the Lamb who was slain.” In 13:8 the text speaks of those whose names have not been “written from the foundation of the world in the book of life of the Lamb who was slain” (ESV).

Read from inside time

The Lamb opens the scroll after his death and victory; the song is new. The story moves in order from the weeping to the opening. “From the foundation of the world” is read by many on this side as the names being written from the foundation, or as a plan God made in view of what he foresaw, without the plan being a fixed script.

Read from outside time

Here the text puts the cross before creation. The sacrifice of the Lamb is not an emergency fix. It is the plan, in the mind of the Father before the world was made (1 Peter 1:20). The Lamb stands at the center of a history that was always going to end in worship.

The Father angleThe One on the throne holds the scroll, and the Son comes and takes it from his hand. The whole book runs on the relationship between them.

Hardest for outside

Hardest for insideputting the Lamb and “the foundation of the world” in one verse puts the cross before Genesis 3. Hardest for the outside side: John weeps because no one can open the scroll, and the Lamb appears after the weeping.

Kings and prophets

Joshua 10:12–14

The sun stands still and “no day like it”

Five Amorite kings attack Gibeon, Israel’s new ally. Joshua marches all night. The Lord says, “Do not fear them, for I have given them into your hands.” Hailstones fall on the enemy. Joshua says before all Israel, “Sun, stand still at Gibeon” (ESV). The sun stops in the middle of the sky for about a whole day. The text says: “There has been no day like it before or since, when the LORD heeded the voice of a man, for the LORD fought for Israel” (ESV).

Read from inside time

God “heeded the voice of a man.” It reads as a clear case of God responding to a human request in the middle of a battle.

Read from outside time

God had already promised the victory, and Joshua’s prayer was the form his promise took. The wonder fits a plan in which God moves creation to serve his people, and the phrase “heeded the voice” is a way of saying God answered.

The Father angleA Father who listens to a soldier’s cry and bends the sky for his family.

Hardest for outsidethe word “heeded” and the writer’s claim that this day was unique.

Hardest for insidethe victory was already promised (“I have given them into your hands”).

The Law

Genesis 32

Jacob wrestles all night

Jacob is returning home, afraid of his brother Esau, who is coming with four hundred men. He splits his camp, prays, and sends gifts ahead. Alone at night by a ford, a man wrestles with him until daybreak. The man touches his hip and wrenches it, but Jacob will not let go: “I will not let you go unless you bless me” (ESV). The man gives him a new name, Israel: “you have striven with God and with men, and have prevailed” (ESV). Jacob names the place Peniel: “I have seen God face to face, and yet my life has been delivered.” He limps away as the sun rises, and meets Esau, who runs and hugs him.

Read from inside time

God lets himself be gripped, and Jacob’s persistence gains a blessing. Wrestling with God is a picture of prayer as struggle. The relationship is open enough for a human to hold on.

Read from outside time

The man could have ended the fight with a touch, and he did, to show who was stronger. The blessing was promised to Jacob long ago. The wrestling forms him and gives him a new name. The injured hip shows that he won by weakness.

The Father angleA Father who comes in the dark to a frightened son, lets him hold on, and changes his name.

Hardest for outside“you have striven with God and with men, and have prevailed.”

Hardest for insideGod dislocated his hip with a touch, so the match was never even.

Kings and prophets

Isaiah 5:1–7

The vineyard song: “What more was there to do?”

The prophet sings a song for his beloved about a vineyard on a fertile hill. He dug it, cleared the stones, planted the best vines, built a watchtower and a winepress, and waited for grapes. It yielded wild grapes. “What more was there to do for my vineyard, that I have not done in it?” (ESV). He looked for grapes, and it yielded wild ones. So he will take away its hedge and let it be trampled. The vineyard is the house of Israel. “He looked for justice, but behold, bloodshed; for righteousness, but behold, an outcry.”

Read from inside time

God “looked for” good grapes and was disappointed. The question “why?” is asked as a real puzzle. This is a God who invested, waited and hoped, and was let down.

Read from outside time

The question is a legal one posed to the people: what charge can you bring against me? God is showing the justice of his judgment. Looking for grapes is the language of a farmer, used so the people will see they have no excuse.

The Father angleA Father who did everything a child could ask, and watches the child choose bitterness. The song is a lament in a lover’s voice.

Hardest for outsidethe farmer “looked for” good grapes and got bad ones.

Hardest for insidethe story leads to a clear and certain sentence, so the farmer was never surprised into helplessness.

Amos 7:1–6

Locusts, fire, and “The LORD relented”

Amos sees a vision: the Lord is forming locusts after the king’s cutting of the hay. They strip the land. Amos cries out, “O Lord GOD, please forgive! How can Jacob stand? He is so small!” (ESV). The text says, “The LORD relented concerning this: ‘It shall not be,’ said the LORD.” Then a vision of fire that dries up the deep. Again Amos pleads: “O Lord GOD, please cease!” Again, “The LORD relented concerning this: ‘This also shall not be.’” The third vision, the plumb line, gets no plea, and no relenting.

Read from inside time

The prophet’s prayer stops the judgment twice. A prophet’s courage in intercession is a part of how God governs. The third vision shows what happens when the case is closed: the intercession ends.

Read from outside time

God showed Amos the visions in order to draw out his prayer and form him into an intercessor. God’s mercy was his own purpose, and the third vision shows that mercy has a boundary set by his justice.

The Father angle“He is so small” is the cry of a son pleading for a smaller brother.

Hardest for outside“The LORD relented” is said twice, directly after Amos’s prayers.

Hardest for insidethe plumb line vision, where there is no prayer and no relenting.

Gospels

Luke 19:41–44

Jesus weeps over Jerusalem

As Jesus rides down the Mount of Olives on a donkey, the whole crowd shouts for joy. He sees the city and weeps over it: “Would that you, even you, had known on this day the things that make for peace! But now they are hidden from your eyes” (ESV). He says days are coming when enemies will surround the city and not leave one stone on another, “because you did not know the time of your visitation” (ESV). In Matthew 23:37 he says: “How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!” (ESV).

Read from inside time

Jesus says “would that” and “you were not willing,” which is the language of a will that was refused. A heart that longs and is turned down is a picture of God inside the story.

Read from outside time

The words “now they are hidden from your eyes” show that judgment was already settled, and the tears are the sorrow of the Son who comes in love. “You would not” is true on the human side and sits beside the plan, which includes the cross in that very week.

The Father angleA mother hen is the image of protective love that is rejected. It is one of the tenderest pictures of God in the Gospels.

Hardest for outside“how often would I... and you were not willing.”

Hardest for inside“hidden from your eyes.”

The whole Bible, told once each way

Told from inside time

In the beginning God makes a world and invites people to share in running it. He brings the animals to the man to see what he will call them. He walks in the garden in the evening. When the man and woman hide, he calls, “Where are you?” and the story of a broken family begins. God does not give up. He clothes them, promises that a child of the woman will crush the serpent, and starts the long work of getting his children back.

The world gets worse, and God is grieved to his heart. He starts again with Noah, and then with Abraham. He tells Abraham his plans, listens as Abraham argues for Sodom, and tests him on the mountain. When Isaac is on the altar, God stops the knife and says, “now I know.” The family grows through Jacob and Joseph. Evil is done by free people, and God turns it toward good, adjusting as he goes. A pit becomes a throne room, and a famine becomes a rescue.

In Egypt, God hears the groaning, calls Moses, and gives him signs with “if” in them. Pharaoh hardens his own heart first, again and again, until it sets. At Sinai, the people make a calf, and God tells Moses to leave him alone. Moses will not. God relents. Over and over in the desert, the pattern holds: God threatens, someone prays, and God pardons, though some consequences stay. The kings follow. God regrets making Saul king. He tells David what the men of Keilah would do. A plague stops at the threshing floor. Hezekiah turns to the wall and weeps, and fifteen years are added to his life. Ahab humbles himself for a day, and the judgment is put off. The prophets say it plainly: if a nation turns, “I will relent.”

God is wounded by Israel’s faithlessness, and he is a husband and father: “How can I give you up, O Ephraim?” He looks for someone to stand in the gap. When none is found, he decides to stand there himself.

In the fullness of time, the Son arrives. He grows in wisdom. He is moved by a Canaanite mother’s argument and marvels at his hometown’s unbelief. He weeps at a tomb. In Gethsemane he asks if there is another way, and the Father does not give one. The cross is a real cost to the Father and the Son, and it is the most daring move in history: God takes the worst that free creatures can do and makes it the way home.

Now the church prays, and prayer is real work in a real war. Answers can be delayed. Persistence matters. God knows his goal and is sure to reach it: a new heaven and a new earth, where he lives with his people. The Lamb opens the scroll. The Father wipes every tear. He is the Father who ran down the road, and the story ends with a banquet. Along the way he has been shaping, answering, hoping, and winning, and the future he promises is certain because he is wise enough and strong enough to win in any situation his children create.

Told from outside time

Before there was a world, there was God: Father, Son and Spirit, complete in love, needing nothing. He is not stretched out in time with a past and a future. He holds all of it at once, the way an author holds the whole novel. He decides to make a world, not out of need, but out of overflowing goodness. In that decision the whole story is already in view: the creation, the fall, the cross, and the new creation. The Lamb was marked for slaughter before the world was made (Revelation 13:8; 1 Peter 1:20).

Inside the story, God appears as he must for creatures who live in time. He walks in the garden and asks, “Where are you?” so that the man will speak. He is grieved over a wicked world, and he sends a flood and saves a family. He goes down to see Babel and Sodom, to show that his judgments are not careless. He tests Abraham, not to learn anything, but to bring out a faith he knew was there, and he provides the ram. He tells Abraham, Moses and Hezekiah what he will do, and then he does it. The story of Joseph is the model: his brothers meant evil, and God meant it for good. Two authors, one act, no contradiction.

God is not a man that he should change his mind. When the Bible says he relents, it speaks of how his steady mercy looks from our side of the story. He announced judgment on Nineveh so that Nineveh would repent, and Jonah knew it. He said “I will harden his heart” before the first plague, and Pharaoh was fully guilty. He named Josiah three hundred years ahead and Cyrus a century and a half ahead, to show that he alone knows the end from the beginning. Prayer is real. God ordains the prayer as well as the answer, and he uses Moses, Daniel and a persistent widow as the means of his mercy. When the sailors want to leave the ship, Paul says they cannot be saved unless they stay, because God ordains means with ends.

The cross is the center. Peter says Jesus was handed over “according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God,” by the hands of lawless men. Both are true: the plan is God’s and the guilt is theirs. In Gethsemane the Son’s human will shrinks from the cup, and he yields to the Father’s will, the will they shared before the world began. Jesus weeps at the tomb of Lazarus, knowing what he is about to do, because the Son took on a human heart and a human grief.

The Father who chose a people before they were born is the same Father who ran down the road to a prodigal. His election is mercy, not a cold decree. His foreknowledge is not a spectator’s, it is a Father’s. Nothing that happens to his children happens outside his hand, and every tear is counted. The story ends where it was always going to end: the throne, the Lamb, the river and the tree of life. Those who are his were written in the book from the foundation of the world. The Father who knew the end from the beginning, and loved them there, is the God who wipes every tear.

Before the Mountains sets the two answers side by side and lets the texts argue. It does not pick a winner. A companion to A Father Inside Time and its Cross-Examination.
