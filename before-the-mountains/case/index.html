The case, claim by claim

Does God live through time with his people, step by step? Or does he stand outside time and see every moment at once? The question sounds abstract. It is not. It decides how we read God’s anger and his tears. It decides what we think happens when we pray. It shapes what we say to a person in pain. It changes how we think about free choices and about whether the future is already written.

Two positions are on the table. Each one is argued from scripture. Neither is presented here as the winner.

The inside-time view (the relational view). God has always lived in succession. He has never been without a “before” and an “after.” He is eternal in the sense that he always was and always will be. He is not eternal in the sense of standing outside time. His character never changes. His love, holiness, wisdom, power and faithfulness are fixed forever. But his plans, his feelings and his responses move as he deals with real people. He relents. He grieves. He answers prayer with a different action than he would have taken otherwise. He is the Father in the living room, not the king behind a wall. On this view God knows everything that can be known. Some free choices that have not yet been made are possibilities, not yet facts, so they are not yet there to be known. God knows every possibility, every human heart and every plan of his own. He can be moved, and he can be surprised by how far freedom can go.

The outside-of-time view (the classical view). God is the Creator of time, so he is not a prisoner of it. He has no before and after. His whole life is present to him at once. That is why he knows the future exhaustively: for him there is no “not yet.” He is unchanging in his being, his purposes and his knowledge. The scriptures that show God relenting, regretting or grieving speak from our side of the story. They tell us truly how God deals with us. They do not tell us that something new happened inside God. God ordains the prayer and the answer together. He governs all things, including the free choices of people, and he is never the author of sin. On this view the Bible’s tender language is true, and the Bible’s language of fixed purpose is true, and the second tells us how to read the first.

Why it matters.

  • Prayer. If God is inside time and responds, prayer can change what he does next. If God is outside time, prayer is the means he uses to bring about what he eternally willed. Both sides say prayer matters. They disagree about what it does.
  • Free will. If the future is partly open, your choice is one of the things that settles it. If the future is fully known and governed, the question becomes how a known choice can still be yours.
  • Trust. An unchanging God who sees everything promises firm comfort. A God who responds and shares the risk promises company. Each side says its picture is the more trustworthy one.

Ground rule. Both sides argue from scripture and from reasoning about scripture. Both sides read some texts plainly and others as figures. Both sides bring a philosophy of time to the page, whether they say so or not. Each part below gives the strongest case for each side, then shows where each side’s own texts are weaker than they look. Where one side’s best argument is a question the other side has to answer, the question is put plainly.

Shared ground. Before the disagreement starts, notice what both sides say. God is eternal in the sense that he always was and always will be. God is love. God is good. God is not the author of sin. Prayer matters. God made heaven and earth, and the created heavens have their own order and duration. God’s approach to people changed across the story of the Bible. The disagreement is narrower than it first sounds. It comes down to a few questions. Is God’s inner life, including his purposes and his knowledge, fixed, or only his character? Does he live in a real sequence? Does he know free choices before they are made?

Each claim opens to the inside-time case, the outside-of-time case, and where each side is weaker than it looks. No teacher or author is named here; the texts carry the argument.

Part 1

God and time itself

Is time a created thing, or the measure of succession?

The inside-time case

Time is not an object God made. It is the name we give to progression, one thing after another. An inch is not a thing. It is a unit for measuring a laptop. In the same way “time” is the unit we use to measure sequence. The length is real whether or not anyone names it. Sequence is simply what it is to be a living agent who thinks, speaks, waits and acts. If God has always thought one thing and then another, he has always lived in sequence. On this view “eternal” means “always was and always will be.” It does not mean “outside time.”

Texts used:

  • Revelation 1:8 (NKJV): “I am the Alpha and the Omega, the Beginning and the End.” Also 21:6 and 22:13. The reading: you can go back as far as you like, and God is there, in progression, at every point.
  • Genesis 1:1 (ESV): “In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth.” The verse says the heavens were made. It does not say that sequence was made. Scripture says nothing of where God was or what he did “before” the heavens.
  • Psalm 102:27 (ESV): “you are the same, and your years have no end.” The phrase “your years” is the language of duration without end.
  • Many scientists say time runs differently in different places. The inside-time side welcomes that. Progression itself is the uncreated truth, and time is the name for how we measure it.
  • Define four terms before arguing. Temporary means it lasts a while and ends. Temporal means ordered in sequence. Timeless means no passing of time at all. Transcending means acting on time from inside, as when the sun stood still. On this view God is temporal and forever, not temporary. He can transcend time without being exempt from it. Joshua 10:13 says “the sun stood still,” and the inside-time side reads this as God acting on time from inside it.

The outside-of-time case

Genesis 1:1 begins with “In the beginning.” If time is the measure of change in created things, then time began when creation began. Genesis 1:14 supports this: the lights in the sky are made “for signs and for seasons, and for days and years.” Measured time comes with the heavens. If time was made, its Maker is not inside it by nature. Psalm 90:2 says “from everlasting to everlasting you are God,” which can mean “no beginning” without meaning “an endless line of moments.” Psalm 102 contrasts God with a creation that wears out like a garment, and Hebrews 1:10-12 applies those verses to the Son. The reasoning is that a cause of all change cannot itself be changed by what it causes.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The inside-time side offers “time is just a measurement” as if it were a reading of scripture. No verse says it. It is a claim in the philosophy of time, and philosophers still argue about whether time is a dimension, a relation, a measure or an illusion. The inside-time side is doing philosophy, and the claim has to be argued, not quoted.
  • The inside-time side owes an answer about what time is, if it is uncreated. Is it a second uncreated thing next to God? Or is it part of God’s own life? The second answer is possible but is a larger claim than “time is a ruler.”
  • The outside-of-time side also uses philosophy. “Time began with creation” is a reading of Genesis 1, not a statement in it. Genesis 1:1 says the heavens and earth were created. It does not say that duration was created.
  • Psalm 90:2 can be read either way. “From everlasting to everlasting” is natural Hebrew for “forever back and forever forward.” It does not need to mean timeless.

Do speech, memory and relationship require succession?

The inside-time case

Any speech needs order. If a being spoke a whole sentence “outside time,” the hearer would take in every word at once and understand none of it. Meaning needs one word after another. Memory needs a before. Relationship needs memory. The Father, the Son and the Spirit were in relationship before creation, in communication and in memory. So some succession was in God’s life before anything was made. The same point is made from the created heavens: demons moved out of a man and into pigs (Mark 5:13); there is a struggle against spiritual rulers (Ephesians 6:12). Motion and struggle need sequence.

The outside-of-time case

The classical reply is that God’s life is not empty of relation. It is fully and eternally actual: Father, Son and Spirit all at once, not a series of moments. Human speech needs order. Divine knowing and loving do not. A timeless cause can produce a sequenced effect. One eternal decree can appear in history as a story, the way a single act of an author appears in a book as chapters. “You would hear everything at once” confuses how God is with how God speaks to us. Our words like “hear” and “answer” are true of God, but not in the way they are true of a creature. This is called analogical language. It also notes that the inside-time side already grants that God can transcend time, stop the sun and act on time in ways we cannot.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The inside-time argument rests on logic, not on a verse. No text says “the Father and the Son spoke in sequence before creation.” The argument may be good, but it should be named as an argument.
  • The inside-time side has a cost. If the Trinity lived in sequence before creation, then a “before” and “after” bound God even without a creation. It is fair to ask whether that is a limit on God, a series he must pass through.
  • The outside-of-time side has a cost too. If God’s whole life is one simultaneous act, it is hard to say how he truly responds to a prayer prayed today, rather than only appearing to. “The effects change, God does not” is a coherent answer. Whether it is enough is the live question.
  • The pigs and the spiritual struggle prove that created spiritual beings move in sequence. Nobody on the classical side disputes that. They say nothing about God himself.

Do the “before the ages” texts show that God is outside time?

The inside-time case

Each of these verses can be read as God’s plan, made early. “Before the ages began” in 2 Timothy 1:9 and Titus 1:2 describes what God purposed before creation. It fits an inside-time God who planned ahead. The Greek word aiōn in these verses means “age” or “ages,” so they speak of eternity, not timelessness. Isaiah 57:15 says God “inhabits eternity,” which can simply mean “dwells forever.” John 17:24 is about love “before the foundation of the world,” which is the ordinary language of a Father whose love had no beginning. None of these texts says “God has no succession.”

The outside-of-time case

This is the classical side’s strongest scriptural ground, and it does not depend on the four famous proof texts (examined in the next section). It rests on a group of verses that speak of something God did, loved, gave or promised before time or before the ages.

  • 2 Timothy 1:9 (ESV): grace “given us in Christ Jesus before the ages began.” NKJV: “before time began.”
  • Titus 1:2 (ESV): “God, who never lies, promised before the ages began.”
  • Jude 25 (ESV): “before all time and now and forever.”
  • 1 Corinthians 2:7: a wisdom God decreed “before the ages.”
  • Ephesians 3:11: an “eternal purpose.”
  • John 17:5: the glory the Son had with the Father “before the world existed.”
  • John 17:24: “you loved me before the foundation of the world.”
  • Psalm 90:2: “from everlasting to everlasting you are God.”
  • Isaiah 57:15 (ESV): “the high and lofty one who inhabits eternity, whose name is Holy.”
  • Isaiah 46:9-10 (ESV): “declaring the end from the beginning and from ancient times things not yet done.”

The reasoning: if there were a time before the ages, it would be an age. A God who acts “before the ages” is not located in the sequence the ages make up. The text of John 17:24 also fits: the Father’s love for the Son is not a mood that began. It is eternal.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The outside-of-time reading of “before the ages” depends on a choice. A verse that says God acted before the ages can mean “before any age existed,” which places God before the ordered flow of created time. It can also mean “early.” The English word “before” is itself a time word. Both sides have to explain why God’s “before” is not a real before.
  • The inside-time side owes an answer to this question: if “before the ages began” only means “God planned early,” what is “early” measured against, when there was no age yet to be early in? It is a fair point against a pure planning reading.
  • The classical side owes an answer too. If “before the ages” means God has no succession, what does it mean that the Father “loved” the Son “before the foundation of the world” (John 17:24)? The verb is a past tense. Even the classical side must say that the language of “before” and “loved” is analogy.
  • Jude 25 in particular depends on a manuscript tradition. The phrase “before all time” is missing in the manuscripts behind the KJV. Versions differ between “before all time” and “before all ages.” Swapping the English word does not remove the “before,” but the verse is less secure than the others.

Do the four famous proof texts for timelessness hold?

The inside-time case

The four verses most often cited to show a timeless God are 2 Peter 3:8, Revelation 10:6, Jude 25 and the Alpha and Omega (Revelation 1:8; 22:13). Read in context, none of them says what it is used to say.

  • 2 Peter 3:8 (ESV): “with the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day.” The next verse explains it: “The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise... but is patient toward you” (3:9). The verse answers scoffers about delay. It says God’s patience is long, not that God has no sequence. If it were a formula for how God’s time works, creation would have taken seven thousand years.
  • Revelation 10:6. The KJV has “that there should be time no longer.” Modern versions read “there would be no more delay” (ESV). The Greek word is chronos. Verse 7 goes on to speak of “the days” when the seventh angel is about to sound his trumpet. A verse that ended time could not be followed by days.
  • Jude 25. The Greek word aiōn is better rendered “age” or “ages.” The verse says God is eternal, not that he is outside time.
  • Revelation 1:8 and 22:13. “The first and the last” is Isaiah’s title (Isaiah 41:4; 44:6). In Isaiah it claims rule over history against idols: “apart from me there is no God.” It says he was there at the beginning and will be there at the end. The classical reading, “he is at beginning and end at once,” comes from philosophy.

The outside-of-time case

The classical case never rested on these four. Clearing four weak arguments leaves the better ones standing. On 2 Peter 3:8, Peter does not say God is slow. He says time does not bind God the way it binds us. That is still a statement about God and duration, even though patience is the setting. The verse echoes Psalm 90:4. “A day is like a thousand years” works as an argument for patience only if God’s relation to duration is unlike ours. On Revelation 10:6, the classical side accepts “no more delay.” On Jude 25, even the translation “before all ages” places God before every age there has been.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • Three of the four readings are widely held on both sides. Jude 25 does not move, whichever English word is used.
  • The inside-time side often treats clearing the four texts as winning. It is not. It only removes some of the classical side’s weakest arguments. The “before the ages” texts in the section above are untouched.
  • The classical side should not rest on “Alpha and Omega” read as “beginning and end at once.” The text says he is the first and the last. It does not say “simultaneously.”
  • For the inside-time side the question stays: if these four fail, what is the best single verse, in its own context, that says God is outside time? And for the classical side: if the best texts are the “before the ages” ones, do they say God is outside time, or only that he preceded every age?

What does the name “I AM” say about God and time?

The inside-time case

Exodus 3:14 in Hebrew is ehyeh asher ehyeh. The verb is imperfect and can be rendered “I will be who I will be.” In Exodus 3:12 the same verb appears in God’s promise to Moses: “I will be with you.” The name is a name of ongoing presence and becoming. The Greek translation made centuries later rendered it as a static title, “I am the Being One.” That shift happened inside the Bible’s own translation history. Revelation 1:4 and 4:8 describe God as the one “who is and who was and who is to come,” which applies three tenses to God.

The outside-of-time case

“I AM WHO I AM” (Exodus 3:14, ESV) is read as the name of a God whose existence is not strung along a line. John 8:58 (ESV): “Before Abraham was, I am.” Jesus sets a past tense (“was”) against a present tense (“am”) and claims the divine name. A God with no beginning, no end and no parts does not gain or lose anything. The name suggests a fullness that needs nothing. Isaiah 41:4 and 44:6 call God “the first and the last.”

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The Hebrew verb really can be read as “I will be.” It really can be read as “I am.” Careful translators disagree. Neither reading by itself proves anything about time.
  • Revelation 1:4 uses temporal tenses in a Greek book. Scripture speaks about God in both registers: a God “who was and is and is to come,” and a God “who is” without qualification.
  • John 8:58 claims the divine name and pre-existence. It does not by itself claim timelessness. It also sits beside a Jesus who grew (Luke 2:52) and died at a point in history. How a timeless Son does both is the classical side’s burden.
  • The Greek translation point cuts both ways. Either Hellenizing began inside Judaism before Christ, which complicates a simple return to “the Hebrew view,” or the translators heard being-language in the Hebrew.

Does heaven run on a clock?

The inside-time case

Heaven is created and it runs in sequence. The belief in a timeless heaven and a temporal earth is a dualism that the Bible does not teach.

  • Colossians 1:16 (ESV): “all things were created, in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible, whether thrones or dominions or rulers or authorities.” Genesis 1:1: God created “the heavens and the earth.” Nehemiah 9:6 and Psalm 33:6 say the same of “the heaven of heavens.”
  • Revelation 8:1 (ESV): “there was silence in heaven for about half an hour.” A measured length of time in heaven.
  • Revelation 6:1-8:1: the seals are opened one after another. Revelation 6:9-11: martyrs under the altar cry “how long?” and are told to rest a little longer.
  • Isaiah 6:3: seraphim call to one another. Mark 5:13: unclean spirits move and enter pigs. Ephesians 6:12: the struggle against “rulers and authorities.” Psalm 103:19: the Lord “has established his throne in the heavens.” Daniel 10:12-13: an angel is delayed twenty-one days.
  • The dead who are with God ask “how long?” So time is present before the throne.

The outside-of-time case

The classical side agrees. Heaven is created and has sequence. It only denies that this proves anything about God himself. Created heaven can be in time while God is above it. 1 Kings 8:27 (ESV): “Behold, heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you.” God is not in heaven the way a creature is in a place. The “thrones” of Colossians 1:16 are a rank of created angelic powers (compare Ephesians 1:21), not God’s own throne. Scripture calls God’s throne eternal: Psalm 93:2 (“from of old”), Psalm 45:6 and Hebrews 1:8 (“forever and ever”), Lamentations 5:19. Revelation is a book of images: a lamb with seven eyes, seals, trumpets, a scroll. Taking the half hour as a literal clock for God’s own life is a weak step.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The inside-time side proves too little. Nobody denied that heaven has sequence. The claim in dispute is about God.
  • The inside-time side is selective about literal and symbolic. If the half hour in Revelation 8:1 is a literal measure, why is the lamb with seven eyes a symbol?
  • A folk belief gets cleared up: “there is no time in heaven” is not what the Bible says. The classical side agrees that heaven has time.
  • The classical side also faces a question. If heaven has seals opened in order and a silence for about half an hour, in what sense is God “outside” the scene he rules?
  • Dualism is a real danger in the history of ideas. A view where spirit is good and matter is bad leads away from the incarnation. John’s letter argues against it (1 John 1:1-3), and early church writers condemned it. But the classical side condemned it too. Most classical theologians never taught that heaven is timeless in the sense attacked here.

The Son became flesh: what does the incarnation say about time?

The inside-time case

John 1:14: the Word became flesh. Galatians 4:4: “when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son.” God acts at a date. An incarnate God has a before and after. If the Son has a body permanently (Luke 24:39: the risen Lord has flesh and bones), and a body is in time, then at least one Person of God has been in time since Bethlehem. This is the inside-time side’s strongest card. Luke 2:52 (ESV) says Jesus “increased in wisdom and in stature and in favor with God and man.” He learned obedience through what he suffered (Hebrews 5:8). Hebrews 2:17-18 and 4:15 say he was made like us in every respect. Mark 13:32 says that not even the Son knows the day or the hour. Mark 5:30-32 and Luke 8:45 have him ask who touched him; Mark 11:13 has him go to a fig tree for fruit. He went through each stage himself: submissive child (Luke 2:51), friend (John 15:15), brother (John 20:17), one with all authority (Matthew 28:18).

The outside-of-time case

Two natures, one person. As man the Son grew, learned and did not know the hour. As God nothing is hidden from him. John 2:24-25: “he knew all people.” John 16:30: “you know all things.” John 21:17: “Lord, you know everything.” Colossians 2:3, 9: all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden in him, and in him “the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily.” Matthew 9:4, 12:25 and Luke 6:8 say he knew their thoughts. John 1:47-48 and 4:17-18 show him knowing hidden facts. Luke 2:52 refers to Christ’s human nature. Mark 13:32 reflects the same split. Neither verse proves anything about the divine nature’s relation to time.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The two-natures answer is coherent but costly. It must say which nature is timeless and which is in time, and it must say how one person has both. The church has called this a mystery, not a logical contradiction.
  • The inside-time side cannot lean on the incarnation as a model for a God who is “both inside and outside time” by pure logic. It is also a mystery.
  • “Grew in favor with God” echoes 1 Samuel 2:26 about Samuel. It is a growth formula. It does not prove the Father approved of him more at thirty than at three.
  • The inside-time side must hold Luke 2:52 together with John 2:24, “he knew all people.” The classical side must say what Mark 13:32 means about the one person, not only about his human nature.

Is “timeless and responsive” a contradiction?

The inside-time case

Change needs time. Today the steak is good. In a week it is not. To change your mind about eating it, time has to pass. A God who changes his mind is a God in time. A timeless God cannot go from happy to sad or from angry to pleased. If scripture shows God changing, then God is in time. “Timeless and changes his mind” is a logical impossibility. Many Christians hold both without noticing. God does not do the illogical or contradict his own nature. The question “can God make a rock too heavy for him to lift?” is a bad question and does not limit his power. In the same way “a timeless being who changes” is not a limit on God; it is a phrase that does not describe anything.

The outside-of-time case

Timeless defenders agree that change needs time. They say God’s effects change, not God. God eternally wills that a prayer be heard at one moment and not another. The change is in the world. This is “one eternal act with temporal effects.” Many Christians who hold both do so on purpose, not by accident. Some thinkers hold a middle position: God is timeless without creation and temporal since creation. That takes “before the ages” seriously and takes Genesis 6:6 seriously. It nearly matches the admission that where God was before creation, the Bible does not say. Isaiah 55:8-9 says God’s thoughts are higher than ours, and Romans 11:33-36 praises the depth of his wisdom. Some tension may be a limit in us, not a contradiction in him.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • “God cannot do the illogical” is a rule the inside-time side takes from philosophy, not from a verse. It is the same kind of move it criticizes in the classical side. The only “cannot” statements in scripture are about God’s character: he cannot lie (Titus 1:2), cannot be tempted (James 1:13), cannot deny himself (2 Timothy 2:13).
  • The inside-time side says “temporal” is not a limit, because God can fast-forward, restore and do miracles with time (Joshua 10:13). But then “governed by time” has to mean something. If God is governed by time, what governs him? Is time above him?
  • “A timeless God cannot respond” is an assertion, not a demonstration. The classical side’s position says God does respond: his single eternal act has effects that come in order. What it must explain is what it means for Moses’ prayer to come before God’s relenting. Is the sequence real to God, or only to us?
  • A middle position (timeless without creation, temporal with it) is available to both. It is the most discussed way to hold both sets of texts without calling either a figure.

One world, two realms: where is God, and does “where” teach us about “when”?

The inside-time case

God made one creation with two overlapping realms, visible and invisible, not two worlds with a gap. He fills it, and he is closer than “up there.”

  • Jeremiah 23:24 (ESV): “Do I not fill heaven and earth? declares the LORD.” Psalm 139:7-10: there is nowhere to flee. “If I make my bed in Sheol, you are there!”
  • Acts 17:27-28: he is “not far from each one of us.” Ephesians 4:6: “over all and through all and in all.” Colossians 1:17: in him all things hold together. Ephesians 1:23: he “fills all in all.”
  • Ephesians 2:6: believers are “seated with him in the heavenly places.” The realms overlap.
  • 2 Kings 6:17: the servant’s eyes are opened to the army already around Elisha. Genesis 28:12: Jacob’s ladder. Daniel 10:12-13: angels delayed over Persia.
  • Omnipresence is not manifest presence. God is present equally everywhere, but he is felt differently in a cloud (Exodus 33:9), in a wrestler (Genesis 32:24-30), or in a messenger (Judges 13). Luke 3:22: the Spirit descends “in bodily form” like a dove. Genesis 18: Abraham’s visitors. The “angel of the Lord” accepts worship in some appearances.
  • Guardrail: God filling a pen does not make the pen God. This is not pantheism. Creation is “very good” (Genesis 1:31; 1 Timothy 4:4), and matter is not the problem.
  • Jesus “looked up” at Lazarus’ tomb (John 11:41). That acknowledges an unseen realm. It does not say heaven is far away.
  • Isaiah 66:1: “Heaven is my throne, and the earth is my footstool.” Matthew 6:9: “Our Father in heaven.”

The outside-of-time case

The classical side agrees with nearly all of this. It says this is a claim about space, not time, and it turns the argument around. 1 Kings 8:27 and Acts 17:24-28 say God is beyond any place and also “not far.” God is not spatial at all, so “where was God before creation?” is a creaturely question, like “when.” If location is a created category that God transcends, then duration may be one too. The title “angel of the Lord” is not always the pre-incarnate Son: in Zechariah 1:12 the angel prays to the Lord of hosts; in Zechariah 3:1-6 the angel and the Lord speak as two; in Luke 1:11, 19 the angel of the Lord is Gabriel. Some appearances do function as appearances of God (Exodus 3; Judges 13). And “God is a consuming fire” (Deuteronomy 4:24; 9:3; Hebrews 12:29) is one of many “God is” statements: spirit (John 4:24), light (1 John 1:5), faithful (1 Corinthians 1:9), holy (Leviticus 19:2), a righteous judge (Psalm 7:11). Transcendence matters. Isaiah 55:8-9 and Isaiah 40:12-26 picture him as greater than the nations.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • Nearly all of the two-realms picture is shared Christian cosmology. The inside-time side presents a common view as a correction. The one real dispute is about God, not heaven.
  • The argument undercuts itself. If the inside-time side grants that God is not confined to space, and that “where was God before creation” has no answer, the classical question follows: if God needs no “where,” why must he have a “when”?
  • The classical side has the matching question: if God is wholly present to a creation that has a before and after, in what sense is he not present to that sequence?
  • The classical side must also answer the inside-time side’s point that a wrong map of space gives a wrong map of prayer. If God is far above in a separate world, prayer shouts across a gap. The classical side’s reply is that God is “not far from each one of us.”

Part 2

Does God change? Unchanging vs. unmoved

What exactly is unchanging: God’s nature, or everything about him?

The inside-time case

Scripture says God does not change, and scripture says he does. Both are true because they speak about different things. His nature and character never change: love, holiness, wisdom, goodness, faithfulness, power. His mind, plans, methods, emotions and responses move as he deals with free people. The verses that say “does not change” each name one trait in its own setting.

  • Malachi 3:6 (ESV): “For I the LORD do not change; therefore you, O children of Jacob, are not consumed.” In context, God’s steady justice and covenant faithfulness are why Israel survives. The next verse (3:7) says “Return to me, and I will return to you.”
  • James 1:17 (ESV): “the Father of lights, with whom there is no variation or shadow due to change.” In context (1:13-16), God tempts no one and sends only good gifts. The paragraph is about God’s steady goodness.
  • Hebrews 13:8 (ESV): “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.” In context (13:5-6): he will never leave or forsake you.
  • Psalm 102:27: “but you are the same, and your years have no end.” The contrast is with a creation that wears out.
  • When free beings meet, some adjustment happens. A parent who listens to a child may change course. That does not change the parent’s character. Without this split, a personal God who loves, answers and grieves cannot exist.
  • A line through God: his nature on one side, his dealings with free people on the other.

The outside-of-time case

The “does not change” verses are stated as universal, and they ground the whole doctrine.

  • Numbers 23:19 (ESV): “God is not man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his mind.” The reason given is God’s nature as God.
  • Psalm 102:25-27 sets God’s sameness against a changing creation, and reads as an unqualified contrast.
  • Malachi 3:6 gives the reason Israel is not destroyed: God’s unchanging purpose to keep covenant. That sounds like a settled decree, not only a trait.
  • Psalm 33:11 (ESV): “The counsel of the LORD stands forever.” Proverbs 19:21 and Isaiah 14:24, 27: “who will annul it?” Isaiah 46:10: “My counsel shall stand.” Romans 11:29: the gifts and calling of God are irrevocable. 2 Timothy 2:13: he cannot deny himself.
  • Hebrews 6:17-18 speaks of “the unchangeable character of his purpose” and says it is impossible for God to lie.
  • A being who changed in himself would be gaining or losing something, which a perfect being cannot do.
  • The classical side also distinguishes nature from relation. God’s relations change (he becomes “Creator” and “Lord” of what exists) while God himself does not. It needs no “mutable will” to explain responsiveness.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The classical side can agree with almost everything the inside-time side says here. A leading statement of the classical view holds God is unchanging in his being, perfections, purposes and promises, yet does act and feel emotions, and acts and feels differently in response to different situations. So the dispute is narrower than “emotionless God versus relational God.” It comes down to one word: purposes. Are his purposes fixed, or only his character?
  • Hebrews 13:8 and Malachi 3:6 are about faithfulness in their contexts, as most readers on both sides grant. If so, the classical side cannot treat them as proof that nothing in God moves.
  • The inside-time side has to explain how a changing will stays reliable. If mind, will and plans can change, what exactly makes God dependable on his promises? Where is the line?
  • Hebrews 6:17-18 may offer a key that serves both. God swears an oath “to show... the unchangeable character of his purpose,” and Psalm 110:4 says God “will not change his mind” about the priest forever. Sworn purposes (the promise to Abraham, the Davidic throne, the priesthood, the removal of Saul) are fixed. Announced intentions (Nineveh, Hezekiah’s death, Israel after the calf) are conditional. On this reading Numbers 23:19 is a Balaam oracle about a sworn blessing, and 1 Samuel 15:29 is about a sworn removal of a king. The negations are about oaths. The affirmations of relenting are about intentions. The hard question: why swear an oath if every word is already unchangeable?
  • Both sides can be asked: if Malachi 3:6 is about character, as most agree, why can’t “I the LORD do not change” sit beside real change in plans? And if God never moves, what is the thing “relented” actually describes?

Does change imply imperfection? Impassibility, and what it really means

The inside-time case

A God who has every emotion at once, “radiating equally always,” has no real emotions. Wrath and joy would cancel. An “emotionless god” is what you get if emotions require change and change is ruled out. “Impassible” is a word from philosophy. The picture of a God who feels nothing new does not fit a Father who is angry for a moment and then moved to compassion (Isaiah 54:7-8). If God feels nothing new, grief language is empty.

The outside-of-time case

Early church writers used “impassible” as a guardrail against pagan gods with mood swings. They insisted at the same time that God loves, is angered, and suffered in Christ. God is “impassible” in the sense that no creature can overpower or surprise him, not in the sense that he is cold. The tradition affirms steady, perfect love. A perfect being lacks nothing. If something in time could add to him, he would have been incomplete. His love is not a feeling that rises and falls. It is a settled act of will toward the good of others.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The inside-time side cannot say that change in God is harmless without a reply to the perfection argument. If God really changes, does he ever become more than he was? If so, was he less than perfect before? One answer is that a person can be perfect and still be moved by a child, without gaining or losing anything in character. That is a claim about what perfection includes.
  • The classical side must say what is true about God that the tender language points to. If emotions are only figures, why would scripture choose figures that point the wrong way? If God has no real feelings, what does it mean that “God is love” (1 John 4:8) and that he “grieved” (Genesis 6:6)?
  • Both sides can accept the guardrail version of impassibility (God cannot be overpowered or manipulated). What they dispute is whether God’s emotional responses are real events in his life.

Does God have real feelings? Grief, anger and compassion

The inside-time case

The Bible shows God’s emotions changing in response to events. He grieves, is angry for a moment, then has compassion. This is real emotion, not decoration.

  • Genesis 6:5-6 (ESV): “The LORD regretted that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him to his heart.” (The wicked heart of man, v. 5, “only evil continually.”)
  • Psalm 78:40 (ESV): “How often they rebelled against him in the wilderness and grieved him in the desert!” People can give God real pain.
  • Ephesians 4:30: do not grieve the Holy Spirit. The command assumes the Spirit can be grieved.
  • Isaiah 63:9-10: in all their affliction he was afflicted; “they rebelled and grieved his Holy Spirit.” Judges 10:16: he could bear Israel’s misery no longer.
  • Isaiah 54:7-8 (paraphrase): in overflowing anger for a moment God hid his face, but with everlasting love he will have compassion.
  • Hosea 11:8 (ESV): “My heart recoils within me; my compassion grows warm and tender.” He decides against destroying Ephraim.
  • Human grief often involves a mistake. God’s regret is not a mistake. It is deep sorrow at what freedom produced. The Hebrew word nacham is closer to sorrow, being moved to pity, being comforted, than to an admission of error.

The outside-of-time case

This is the language of feeling used of a God who is not moved by anything outside him. Hosea 11:9 (ESV) follows the “heart recoils” line at once: “for I am God and not a man.” That contrast is the point. The tradition calls the tender language anthropopathism, human feeling-language for a God who is not affected as we are. God’s grief at sin is real in the sense that it expresses his settled opposition to evil and his settled love for the sinner. It does not mean something new happened to God. A person can see a death coming and still grieve. Grief at a foreseen tragedy is ordinary human experience. Isaiah 46:9-10 has God declare “the end from the beginning,” yet he is also called merciful and grieved. Revelation 13:8 and 1 Peter 1:20 say the Lamb was slain or foreknown before the foundation of the world: the grief of the cross and the foreknowledge of the cross sit side by side in the New Testament itself.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The inside-time side often says grief is only fully real if the evil was not already seen. That does not follow. A spouse watching a long illness grieves along the way and grieves again at the death. The same is said of the Father and the cross. Foreknowledge does not cancel feeling. What is harder for the classical side is surprise; what is easy for it is grief. Genesis 6:6 does not need an open future. Hosea 11 and Jeremiah 18 press harder, because they show a decision turning.
  • The classical side’s statement that both explanations are “logically possible” is fair, but it must say why scripture chooses emotional words at all. An emotion that is only a figure of speech still needs something true to point to.
  • The inside-time side has a question of its own. If God’s feelings depend on what creatures do, can creatures ever make God worse off? What guards his joy?
  • Hosea 11:8-9 is especially useful for both sides. The tenderness and the “not a man” sit in one breath. In Hosea, being God and not a man is the reason he does not destroy. In 1 Samuel 15:29, the same contrast is the reason he does not relent. Any reading must keep both.

Figure of speech, or the real thing?

The inside-time case

Some Bible language about God is clearly figurative. Isaiah 55:12: “the mountains and the hills... break forth into singing, and all the trees of the field shall clap their hands.” Psalm 98:8: the rivers clap. Luke 19:40: the stones cry out. Psalm 97:5: the mountains melt like wax. These are figures. But calling every divine change a figure of speech is forced by a prior belief that God is timeless. The test is whether the plain reading violates God’s nature or only a philosophy about his nature. Contested texts: Genesis 6:6; Exodus 32:14; Ezekiel 22:30 (God looked for someone to “stand in the breach”); Exodus 4:14 (God’s anger burned against Moses). The inside-time reading: each is a real reaction to a real choice. Either it is an anthropomorphism, or God was limited on the earth because he could not find a man. The honest limit: “I don’t know where the line is.”

The outside-of-time case

Scripture itself gives a check. The same chapter that says God “regretted” (1 Samuel 15:11, 35) also says God does not regret (15:29). The text signals that the regret language is a different kind of statement. Scripture uses body language of God everywhere: his hand, his eyes, his wings (Psalm 91:4). Nobody takes those literally. Mental and emotional language is the same kind of speech. An old image says God “lisps” to us like a nurse to a small child. Genesis 6:6 is human-shaped language for God’s settled displeasure at sin. Exodus 32:14 is a conditional threat. Ezekiel 22:30 is a vivid way of saying no one interceded. Exodus 4:14 names God’s just opposition, not a mood. Isaiah 55:12 and Luke 19:40 show that scripture speaks in figures about non-human things, and the classical side says it is the same device applied to God.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The test “does it violate his nature?” is good, but both sides pass their own reading through it. The classical reader says real regret would violate perfect knowledge. The open reader says it would not. The test moves the argument to what God’s nature includes, which is where it started.
  • A “plain” reading of Numbers 23:19 contradicts a “plain” reading of Exodus 32:14. Both sides interpret.
  • The rule “hear the heart, not just the words” works for both sides. A person asks “do I look bad in this?” and the words are about a dress while the heart is “will you reject me?” Applied to scripture, extreme language like “Esau I hated” may carry a gentler heart. The classical view uses the same tool on “the LORD regretted.” The rule needs a second rule for when the words are the heart.
  • The inside-time side has to say what rule tells it when God’s grief is literal and when the clapping trees are a figure. If the rule is “whichever fits my view,” how is that different from the classical move?
  • The classical side has to say what a text would have to say before it agreed that God really responds. If Exodus 4:14, Ezekiel 22:30 and Genesis 6:6 are all figures, what stops the whole narrative of a responsive God from being a figure?
  • Many believers hold both “God is timeless” and “God changes his mind” without noticing the conflict, a mix of ingredients that may not fit together. The classical side replies that its position already holds both on purpose: timeless being, real effects in time.

Jesus weeps at Lazarus’s tomb: real grief, or grief over a known ending?

The inside-time case

John 11:35 (ESV): “Jesus wept.” John 11:33 says he was “deeply moved in his spirit and greatly troubled.” Whoever has seen the Son has seen the Father (John 14:9). So the Father also feels loss as loss. The tears are not acting. Many readers also see Jesus grieving at the unbelief of the mourners around him (11:37-40), and that kind of grief is a response to people, as it is in Hosea 11.

The outside-of-time case

Jesus told his disciples before the tomb that Lazarus would rise (11:4, 11), and he thanked the Father at the tomb for hearing him (11:41-42). He wept anyway. Knowing the ending does not cancel feeling. John 11:33 and 11:38 show him moved by death itself, which he came to defeat. A person who knows a child will recover still weeps at the bedside. Foreknowledge and grief sit together here.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The tears show real feeling in the Son, who is God in the flesh. They do not by themselves show that the Father feels the same way without a body, and the classical side will say the incarnation is the place where God feels as a man.
  • The classical side must say what is left of “impassible” when the Son weeps. The usual answer is that he weeps in his human nature. The inside-time side asks why the Gospel shows that as the picture of the Father.
  • The inside-time side must say why Jesus wept if he was about to raise Lazarus within minutes. If the answer is “for the mourners,” it fits both sides.

God is love: does perfect love need creation, and does God need anything?

The inside-time case

Love needs an object. A being alone cannot be love. Before creation God was already love, so God is a community: Father, Son and Spirit. Creation overflows that love; it does not fill a lack. This is the lens for everything else, and it is why God relates to us in time and in real relationship.

  • 1 John 4:8, 16 (ESV): “God is love.” Stated twice as a definition.
  • John 17:24: “you loved me before the foundation of the world.” John 17:5: glory shared “before the world existed.” John 1:1-2: “the Word was with God.”
  • John 3:35, 5:20 and 14:31: Father loves Son, Son loves Father. Matthew 3:17: the voice at the baptism.
  • Philippians 2:6-11 and John 17:1: the Son glorifies the Father and the Father the Son. Glory passed around, not hoarded. That answers “isn’t a God who demands worship egotistical?”
  • Ephesians 1:4-5 (ESV): “he chose us in him before the foundation of the world... In love he predestined us for adoption as sons.” A family plan that predates creation.
  • John 15:15: “I have called you friends.” Matthew 28:10 and John 20:17: the risen Jesus calls his disciples “brothers.”
  • Genesis 1:27 and 2:18: relationship is built into the image of God.
  • A parent is in charge but does not control each breath. A good parent listens and may change course for a child.

The outside-of-time case

The doctrine of the Trinity supports love without time. The Father loves the Son eternally (John 17:24). The classical side agrees fully that God is love. It also holds aseity: God needs nothing (Acts 17:25: “nor is he served by human hands, as though he needed anything”; Job 41:11; Romans 11:35, “who has given a gift to him that he might be repaid?”). If love is complete before creation, creation adds nothing God lacked. Scripture has many “God is” statements: spirit, light, a consuming fire, faithful, holy, a righteous judge, greater than our heart. “Father” is one image among several. “King,” “Judge,” “Lord” and “Potter” appear too (Romans 9:20-21; Isaiah 64:8 uses “Father” and “potter” together). A classical theist can say God’s love is first his own infinite self-knowledge and delight. “Love needs another” is a theory of love, not a verse. Deuteronomy 6:4 and Isaiah 44:6 say God is one; the Trinity itself rests on revelation (Matthew 28:19; 2 Corinthians 13:14), not on the argument from love.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • This is the best-built argument on the inside-time side. It also quietly supports the classical doctrine that God needs nothing. If God’s love was complete before creation, what does it mean to say “God needs a man”? (See Part 5.)
  • The family picture is a temporary metaphor. Human parents are fallen and limited. Isaiah 55:8-9 and Job 38-41 insist God is not like a human parent in his knowing. The family image can be pressed too far.
  • The classical side has a question it must answer. If the three Persons exchange love and glory, is that exchange a sequence? If so, is there a before and after inside God, even without creation? And can a Father who never waits, wonders or responds be called a Father at all?
  • The inside-time side has a matching question. If a good father sometimes cannot foresee a child’s choice, should we call him wise, or only loving?

Did God change his way of dealing with people at Sinai?

The inside-time case

Before Moses, God related to his people as a Father, forgiving easily. Only the Mosaic covenant is if/then, with curses. Sinai came about because Israel was afraid and wanted a mediator, and Christ lifts the curses.

  • Genesis 15:9-18: God alone passes between the pieces while Abraham sleeps. A one-sided covenant.
  • Exodus 19:8, 24:3, 24:7: the people volunteer three times, “We will do everything the LORD has said.” Exodus 20:18-21 and Deuteronomy 5:23-29: trembling, they ask Moses to go near for them. Deuteronomy 5:29 answers with longing: “Oh that they had such a heart as this always, to fear me.” (paraphrase)
  • Exodus 32:1-8 and Acts 7:39-42: the golden calf within forty days; “in their hearts they turned to Egypt.”
  • Before Sinai, complaints are answered with provision: Exodus 14:11-12; 15:24-25 (water sweetened); 16:2-15 (bread from heaven and quail); 17:2-7 (water from the rock). After Sinai the same complaints bring fire, a plague, snakes: Numbers 11:1, 4-6, 33; 14:22-23; 21:5-6.
  • Genesis 12:10-20 and 20:1-18: Abraham lies about Sarah twice, and God rebukes the kings, not Abraham (Psalm 105:14-15). Genesis 27 and 28:13-15: after Jacob’s deceit, God’s next word to him is promises.
  • Galatians 3:10-13 and 3:19-25: the law was added because of transgressions, a guardian until Christ. 2 Corinthians 3:7-11: “the ministry of death... of condemnation.” Hebrews 7:18-19, 8:7-13 and Jeremiah 31:31-32: the first covenant was not faultless; the new is “not like” it. Romans 4:15: “the law brings wrath.”
  • Jeremiah 7:22 says God did not speak to their fathers “concerning burnt offerings and sacrifices” when he brought them out of Egypt. Ezekiel 20:25 speaks of “statutes that were not good.”
  • Matthew 19:8 and Deuteronomy 24:1: “Because of your hardness of heart Moses allowed you to divorce... but from the beginning it was not so.” Matthew 5:21-48: “You have heard that it was said... but I say to you.”

The outside-of-time case

Judgment is everywhere before Moses, and the if/then arrives before Sinai.

  • Genesis 3:14-19 (curse in Eden), 4:10-12 (Cain), 6:5-7 and 7:21-23 (the flood), 11:7-9 (Babel), 12:17, 19:24-25 (Sodom), 38:7, 10 (Er and Onan killed by the LORD, inside the family). Exodus 4:24-26: the LORD is about to kill Moses on the road.
  • Exodus 15:25-26 (“if you will diligently listen... I will put none of the diseases on you”) and 16:28.
  • Wrath is post-Mosaic too: Acts 5:5, 10 (Ananias and Sapphira); Acts 12:23; 1 Corinthians 11:30; Romans 1:18; John 3:36; Hebrews 10:31; Revelation 6:16. It is a feature of holiness meeting evil, not a feature of one covenant.
  • Abraham’s covenant has terms: Genesis 17:9-14 (“cut off”), 18:19, 22:16-18, 26:5. David’s: 2 Samuel 7:14; Psalm 132:12; 1 Kings 9:4-7. The new covenant has if/then language: John 15:6, 10; Colossians 1:23; Hebrews 3:14; 10:26-29; Romans 11:22.
  • Sinai opens with God’s initiative and tenderness: Exodus 19:4-6 (“I bore you on eagles’ wings... a kingdom of priests”). Deuteronomy 7:7-8 and 4:7-8. Deuteronomy 5:28: God says “they are right in all that they have spoken.” Exodus 20:20: Moses redirects their fear.
  • Psalm 19:7-11, Psalm 119, Romans 7:12 and 9:4, Matthew 5:17-19: the law is holy and good, a privilege; “not an iota” will pass.
  • Exodus 34:6-7, Leviticus 17:11 and Deuteronomy 30:1-10: grace is inside the if/then. Numbers 14:19-20: God pardons after Sinai. Psalm 89:30-34: the rod comes with “I will not remove from him my steadfast love.”
  • Exodus 20:24, 24:5 and 29:38-42: altars and offerings are commanded before the calf. Ezekiel 20:11 vs 20:25: the laws by which one lives came first; the “not good” statutes came after the wilderness rebellion (20:21-26). 1 Corinthians 10:5-11: Paul applies the wilderness deaths to believers as a warning.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • “Only Moses, wrath only there” fails on the Bible’s own evidence: wrath before Moses (Genesis 6), judgment on God’s own household (Genesis 38; Exodus 4), if/then terms in Abraham and the new covenant. What survives is that Sinai is uniquely two-sided in form and provisional.
  • “God did not want it” has no text. Ezekiel 20:25 refers to later statutes given in judgment, not Sinai itself. Galatians 3:24 calls the law a guardian, a good thing with an end date. That is a gentler and better-sourced picture. Paul’s explanation is accountability (the law made transgression countable, Romans 5:13, 5:20), not a change in God.
  • The inside-time side confuses the law as a covenant administration (provisional, Hebrews 8) with the law as a revelation of God’s character (permanent, Matthew 5).
  • Some early church writers read Sinai as follows: the Ten Words were God’s design, and much that came after the calf accommodated a people who wanted a master. But the text’s own order is against this: offerings are commanded before the calf.
  • The gentle-Father picture needs a gentle pre-Mosaic God, and Genesis will not cooperate. The Father in the living room also drowned the world. The inside-time side must say how Genesis 6 fits “before Moses, God barely confronted sin.”
  • The classical side has its own question. The same complaint gets bread in Exodus 16 and a plague in Numbers 11. If God’s character did not change, what did?

Same heart, different approach: does that solve the puzzle?

The inside-time case

The Father’s heart is the same in every season. His approach adapts as his children grow. This is how “God does not change” and “God changed his approach” fit together.

  • Galatians 4:1-7: the heir, as long as he is a child, is no different from a slave though owner of everything; under guardians until the date set by his father. Status fixed, treatment tied to age. Galatians 4:4: “when the fullness of time had come.”
  • Exodus 34:6 (“merciful and gracious, slow to anger”) is quoted again by Moses, the psalmists, the Levites, Joel and Jonah (Numbers 14:18; Nehemiah 9:17; Psalm 86:15; 103:8; 145:8; Joel 2:13; Jonah 4:2). The approach changed each time; the sentence did not. Lamentations 3:22-23: mercies are new every morning. Malachi 3:6.
  • Eight seasons: servant (Luke 15:17-24; Exodus 4:22-23); infant (Hosea 11:1-4; Deuteronomy 1:31; 1 Corinthians 3:1-2); child under guardians (Galatians 3:23-25; Hebrews 12:5-11); son (Jeremiah 31:31-34; Romans 8:14-17); sibling (John 17:23; Hebrews 2:11-12); friend (Genesis 18:17-33; Exodus 33:11; James 2:23); heir (Romans 8:17; Matthew 25:21; Revelation 3:21); bride (Hosea 2:16-20; Isaiah 54:5, 62:5; Revelation 19:7; 21:2).
  • Why the approach changes: where you are (Psalm 103:14; John 16:12; 1 Corinthians 3:2), where the story is (Hebrews 1:1-2; Acts 17:30), what you do (Jeremiah 18:8; Luke 15:20; James 4:8).
  • When the approach changes, it can feel like he left: Joshua 5:12 (the bread from heaven stopped); Psalm 131:2 (a weaned child). Isaiah 57:15: the God of the high place also lives with the crushed.

The outside-of-time case

The classical side agrees with the center. God “lisps” to us as a nurse to a small child. Both models accept that God’s approach adapts. They differ on whether the change reaches God’s inner life. The seasons overlap and are not a timeline. Abraham was called a friend centuries before Sinai. Paul called himself a servant (Romans 1:1); Jesus took “the form of a servant” (Philippians 2:7). Status comes all at once (John 1:12-13). 1 Corinthians 7:7-8, 32-35 and Matthew 19:12: Paul wishes all were single; Jesus honors those who forgo marriage, so a curriculum that requires spouse and children leaves out Jesus and Paul. Isaiah 56:3-5 and Psalm 68:5-6: eunuchs are promised “a name better than sons and daughters.” Ephesians 1:4-5: “us” is addressed to the saints at Ephesus (1:1); reading it as all humanity is a corporate-election view, and the verse is also the classical proof text for individual election.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • Each image is biblical and arguing from human bonds to God is scripture’s own method. But the ladder of seasons is a reading of metaphors. Hold it as illustration, not mechanism.
  • The inside-time side must say whether “same heart, different approach” is a change in God or only in the child. If only in the child, how does it differ from the classical account?
  • The classical side must say whether the Father who really “waits” for the date he set (Galatians 4:2) is waiting in a real before and after in God.
  • Two courts are not rivals in the tradition. The classical side never made judge the primary category; the Lord’s Prayer settles the address. The sharper dispute is whether the Father in the living room already knows how the evening ends. That is the time question.

Part 3

Does God change his mind?

The standing rule: “if you turn, I will relent” (Jeremiah 18)

The inside-time case

Jeremiah 18:7-10 is the clearest description of how God acts toward free people: “if you, then I.” God states a general rule. When he announces judgment or blessing for a nation, a change in that nation changes his action.

  • Jeremiah 18:8 (ESV): “if that nation... turns from its evil, I will relent of the disaster that I intended to do to it.” And the reverse in 18:10: if it does evil, “then I will relent of the good that I had intended to do to it.”
  • Related statements of the same rule: Ezekiel 33:13-16 (conditional threats and promises); Jeremiah 26:3, 13 (paraphrase: perhaps they will listen, and I will relent); Jeremiah 36:3, 7; Joel 2:13-14 (“Who knows whether he will not turn and relent?”); Jeremiah 42:10; Malachi 3:7 (“Return to me, and I will return to you”).
  • Jeremiah 18:11-12: the people answer “it is hopeless,” used as proof that the plan depended on their reply.
  • God’s will is stated. What he does depends on whether people respond. That shows God interacting with people in time rather than pre-planning every detail.

The outside-of-time case

Jeremiah 18:7-10 describes a pattern of how God deals with nations. The decrees that matter most are not conditional: Isaiah 46:10 (“my counsel shall stand, and I will accomplish all my purpose”) and Ephesians 1:11. The conditional framing is itself the means by which God accomplishes what he decreed. He ordains ends and means together. Jonah is sent so that Nineveh repents; the warning works, so it was a real warning and a real decree at once. God also announces non-relenting as policy: Jeremiah 4:28 (“I have not relented”), Ezekiel 24:14, Zechariah 8:14. The policy of mercy toward repentance is God’s constant way, not a new decision each time (Jonah 3:10; 4:2).

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The inside-time side must say whether any prophecy of God is fixed. Which ones, and who decides? If God can be talked out of a declared judgment, how can any declared judgment or promise be trusted as final?
  • The classical side has a parallel question. If the condition was always part of the plan, why does God sound surprised or newly decided in other passages? “I said... now I say” (1 Samuel 2:30) is hard to read as a static decree.
  • Both sides agree Jeremiah 18 is God’s policy. They disagree on whether the policy is something God knows will come out one way or something that is still open when he states it. That is the real fork. If God knew they would turn, is the relenting theater? The classical reply: the condition operates in time; God’s knowledge of it is not in time.

Exodus 32: “the LORD relented”

The inside-time case

God tells Moses what he will do. Moses pleads. God changes. The narrative gives Moses causal weight.

  • Exodus 32:9-10: “Now therefore let me alone, that my wrath may burn hot against them and I may consume them.” Moses appeals to God’s reputation and to his promise to Abraham, Isaac and Israel (32:11-13).
  • Exodus 32:14 (ESV): “And the LORD relented from the disaster that he had spoken of bringing on his people.”
  • Numbers 14:11-20: Moses intercedes and God says, “I have pardoned, according to your word.” Deuteronomy 9:18-20: Moses says the LORD listened to him.
  • If God already stood in the future and knew everything, he would have no need to “relent” or be told. The partnership with Moses means Moses’ opinion counts.
  • Exodus 33:11: the LORD spoke to Moses “face to face, as a man speaks to his friend.”

The outside-of-time case

The passage can be read as God testing Moses and drawing out his intercession. God has already told Moses he will keep the promise to Abraham, so Moses’ prayer is the means by which God achieves a result he already intended (compare Genesis 18:17-33). The threat was conditional from the start, so nothing in God changed. God ordained both the threat and Moses’ intercession. Hebrews 6:17-18 (an oath marks what cannot change) leaves room for ordinary announcements to be conditional.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The text really does present Moses as making a difference. Moses is not told “it was already decided.” The classical side has to say why the narrative reads as it does, and why the same God is called unchanging.
  • The inside-time side must handle a related fact: the announcement “I will consume them” had no stated condition. If a stated threat can be conditional without saying so, the classical reading of every relenting text has a foothold.
  • The hardest question for the classical side: if God eternally planned to relent when Moses pleaded, was Moses’ prayer a real cause or only an instrument of a settled result?
  • The hardest question for the inside-time side: how can promises be trusted if prayer can change God’s course?

Moses at the burning bush: was it plan A?

The inside-time case

Moses refuses God’s call five times (Exodus 3:11, 3:13, 4:1, 4:10, 4:13). God then “gives” Aaron as a mouthpiece (4:10-16). The reading: this was not plan A. Moses’ resistance changed how God proceeded.

The outside-of-time case

Exodus 4:14 says “the anger of the LORD was kindled against Moses” and also says of Aaron: “behold, he is coming out to meet you.” God tells Moses what Aaron will do before Moses’ final refusal. The passage earlier told Moses what would happen in Egypt (Exodus 3:18-22; 3:19: “I know that the king of Egypt will not let you go”). That fits a God who saw it coming better than one caught off guard. The classical side sees foreknowledge in the story.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The inside-time side has the “five refusals” but the text shows God already knowing about Aaron. Does that not already show foreknowledge of a free act?
  • The classical side has to say why God’s anger burns at a refusal he foresaw, and why the text presents Aaron as a concession.
  • Both sides read the same verse: 4:14 contains both an angry God and an advance-knowing God. A reading must keep both.

Jonah and Nineveh: a warning that was real, or a bluff?

The inside-time case

God announces a flat outcome: “Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown!” (Jonah 3:4). Nineveh repents. God relents (3:10, ESV): “God relented of the disaster that he had said he would do to them, and he did not do it.” The king bets on it: “Who knows? God may turn and relent” (3:9). Jonah himself says he knew this would happen (4:2, ESV): “I knew that you are a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love, and relenting from disaster.” The warning was a real warning, not a manipulation. If God knew all along he would relent, why tell the prophet the opposite?

The outside-of-time case

The condition was always part of the announcement, and the point of the warning was to produce the repentance God planned. Jonah is sent so that Nineveh repents. Jonah 4:2 shows that Jonah himself understood the announcement as conditional on God’s character. The warning works, so it was a real warning and a real decree at once. Some readers think the warning was a bluff meant to produce repentance; that does not fit God’s nature, and the classical side does not need it. The condition operates in time; God’s knowledge of the outcome is not in time.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • Jonah 4:2 helps both sides. Jonah knew God relents. That fits a God whose responsiveness is a known and stable part of his character, which is a point for the classical side. It also shows the relenting is real and expected, which is a point for the inside-time side.
  • An announced outcome with no stated condition (3:4) was still conditional. That is the classical key to every relenting text. If this key works here, it is hard to deny that it works at Exodus 32 as well.
  • The inside-time side must say whether a conditional warning that God knew would succeed is a real warning. The classical side must say whether it is a performance.
  • The classical side must ask, with Jonah’s story in front of it: if God always knew he would relent, in what sense did Nineveh’s turning change anything, and why does the text present it as if it did?

1 Samuel 15: “God regretted,” and “God does not regret,” in one chapter

The inside-time case

The chapter holds both statements.

  • 1 Samuel 15:11 (ESV): “I regret that I have made Saul king, for he has turned back from following me.” 15:35 (ESV): “the LORD regretted that he had made Saul king over Israel.”
  • 1 Samuel 15:29 (ESV): “the Glory of Israel will not lie or have regret, for he is not a man, that he should have regret.”
  • Background: Amalek (the command goes back to Exodus 17:14 and Deuteronomy 25:17-19); the people demanded a king (1 Samuel 8), and God gave them one; the earlier hint of a king from Judah (Genesis 49:10).
  • The reading: verse 29 is about one decision, God’s final judgment against Saul. It means “I am not a man to be talked out of this. Stop asking.” It is about God being firm, not about God never changing course. The chapter itself shows God changing his mind about Saul. Saul has already been replaced by an anointed successor.
  • God chose Saul. Why choose him if he knew Saul would fail? It fits better if God did not know how bad Saul would become. “Saul, I didn’t think you were going to be this bad.”
  • If God cannot really regret, verse 11 cannot mean what it says. If he can, verse 29 has to be limited.

The outside-of-time case

Verse 29 states a general principle (“he is not a man”) that governs how the “regret” of verses 11 and 35 is to be read. The same writer places the two sides by side on purpose, so he does not see them as clashing. Hebrew nacham has a wide range: to be sorry, to relent, to be comforted. The text may describe God’s sorrow over Saul’s choice with no change of plan. God had already announced that Saul’s kingdom would not last (1 Samuel 13:13-14), before the Amalek event. Numbers 23:19 and Hosea 11:9 also use the contrast “not a man.” Foreknowledge is not required to explain choosing Saul: God can choose a flawed king to give a people what they asked for (1 Samuel 8:7-9), fully knowing the cost. A common note says “regret” means deep sadness, not a change of mind.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The inside-time side says verse 29 is about Saul only. But verse 29 gives a reason (“for he is not a man”) that is about God’s nature. Why does a one-time verdict give a nature-reason?
  • The classical side says verse 11 is sorrow only. But then what changed about God’s response after Saul sinned? Scripture uses the same verb in verses 11 and 35 that it denies in verse 29. The chapter holds all three statements, and any reading must keep all three. Verse 29 sits eighteen verses after the first affirmation and six before the last.
  • The inside-time side owes an answer to this question: if God did not foresee how Saul would turn out, what stops that logic running onto every king, prophet and apostle he chose? Did he risk all of them?
  • Hosea 11:8-9 uses “I am God and not a man” as the reason for mercy. 1 Samuel 15:29 uses it as the reason for firmness. Both are true to the text.

Genesis 6: grief and the flood

The inside-time case

Genesis 6:5-7 (ESV): “The LORD saw that the wickedness of man was great in the earth, and that every intention of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually. And the LORD regretted that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him to his heart.” God did not expect it to get that bad. He pivoted his plan through Noah: “But Noah found favor in the eyes of the LORD” (6:8). The flood was a cleansing and a kindness, like cutting out an irreversible cancer to protect the rest. The reasoning: why experience regret if you already saw it coming? To be shocked, you have to not have known.

The outside-of-time case

Grief at a foreseen tragedy is ordinary human experience. A person watching a spouse die slowly grieves along the way and grieves more at the moment. God knew and grieved. Nacham ranges from regret to relief to comfort. The text speaks of God’s settled displeasure at sin. The flood is God’s holy wrath, as the New Testament says: 2 Peter 2:5-6 and Luke 17:26-30. Calling it “kindness” can soften what the text presents as judgment. 2 Peter 3:6-7 says the world of that time was “deluged and destroyed.”

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The inside-time side often argues that grief is only real if the evil was not seen. A person can see a spouse’s death coming and still grieve at the moment. The inside-time side can grant this: it is a coherent rival explanation. It says surprise is the harder fit and that passages showing a change of plan are not explained by it.
  • The classical side must say what “it grieved him to his heart” tells us about his choice to create, if he created with perfect foresight.
  • The inside-time side must say what else God can fail to foresee, and how a plan can be trusted to be safe if he can be surprised.
  • Genesis 6:5-6 does not need open theism. Hosea 11 and Jeremiah 18 press harder.

Hezekiah, Amos, Eli and others: announced outcomes reversed

The inside-time case

In several narratives an announced outcome is reversed after a human response.

  • 2 Kings 20:1-6 and Isaiah 38:1-5: “Set your house in order, for you shall die.” Hezekiah weeps. Isaiah 38:5 (ESV): “I have heard your prayer; I have seen your tears. Behold, I will add fifteen years to your life.”
  • Amos 7:3, 6: after Amos pleads, “The LORD relented concerning this.” Twice. The third vision, the plumb line (7:8), gets no reprieve.
  • 1 Samuel 2:30: God promised Eli’s house a lasting priesthood, then says, “Far be it from me.” A promise with “forever” in it is withdrawn.
  • 2 Samuel 24:16; Judges 2:18; Psalm 106:45; Jeremiah 42:10: God relents.

The outside-of-time case

For Hezekiah, God ordained both the prayer and the answer. Isaiah 37:35 shows God had already promised to defend the city for his own sake. For Eli, 1 Samuel 2:30 says “Far be it from me” about an earlier promise that was conditional (“I said”). The promise was always tied to faithfulness. It is not the reversal of a decree. Anthropomorphism and anthropopathism: human-shaped language for a God who does not change.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • “Hundreds of verses” overstates the count. The count is dozens, concentrated in narrative and prophecy, almost always about judgment averted. No text has God changing his mind about his character, his covenant with Abraham, or the cross.
  • Amos 7 helps both sides. Two reprieves, then none. That looks like both a responsive God and a God with settled limits.
  • The classical side still has to explain why the text says “I said... now I say.”

Do “relented” and “regretted” hide a real change of mind?

The inside-time case

The Hebrew word behind “relented” in Exodus 32, Jonah 3, Amos 7, Jeremiah 18 and “regretted” in Genesis 6 and 1 Samuel 15 is the same word that means to repent or change one’s mind. Nacham is used of God about three dozen times: Genesis 6:6-7; Exodus 32:12, 14; Judges 2:18; 1 Samuel 15:11, 35; 2 Samuel 24:16; Psalm 106:45; Jeremiah 18:8, 10; 26:3, 13, 19; 42:10; Joel 2:13-14; Amos 7:3, 6; Jonah 3:9-10; 4:2. Some older versions say “repented.” Many modern versions say “relented.” In the Greek translation of Jonah 3:10 the verb is metanoeō, so the strong sense is ancient. Accuracy should come before theology.

The outside-of-time case

Nacham has a range: to be sorry, to be comforted, to change course (Genesis 24:67: Isaac was “comforted”; Genesis 37:35: Jacob refused to be comforted). Human repentance from sin is usually shuv, “turn.” In modern English “repent” means turning from wrongdoing, which God never does, so “relent” may be the more accurate word. Nothing shows translators chose it to avoid a problem. The verb is strong either way. The Bible itself denies the sense “change his mind like a man” in 1 Samuel 15:29 and Numbers 23:19.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The word does not mean “mistake.” Context shows it can mean deep grief or moved compassion.
  • If the word means “deeply moved,” why must the outcome involve God changing a plan rather than only God feeling pain? And if the same word can mean change of mind, what in the text shows it does not here?
  • The count of how many English versions say “repented” or “relented” is not established, so arguing from a tally is weak.

Does God test in order to find out?

The inside-time case

Some texts say God tests to know.

  • Genesis 22:12 (ESV): “for now I know that you fear God, seeing you have not withheld your son, your only son, from me.”
  • Deuteronomy 8:2 (God tested you “to know what was in your heart”) and 13:3. 2 Chronicles 32:31: God left Hezekiah “to test him and to know all that was in his heart.”
  • Genesis 18:17: “Shall I hide from Abraham what I am about to do?” Genesis 18:21: “I will go down to see whether they have done altogether according to the outcry.” Then Abraham bargains from fifty down to ten (18:22-33) and God stays in the conversation.
  • The reading: these are plain statements of learning, and they fit a God who is a friend of Abraham (James 2:23).

The outside-of-time case

“Now I know” is demonstrative. James 2:21-23 says Abraham’s faith was “completed” by the act. The test reveals; it does not teach God. The same reading applies to Deuteronomy 8:2 and 2 Chronicles 32:31: “to test to reveal,” not “to test to learn.” Genesis 18:21 is a figure of a just judge examining the case, like Genesis 11:5. Abraham’s bargaining can be the means by which God does what he planned, and Genesis 18:17 shows him treating Abraham as a friend with a share in the plan.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The classical side is using the “it says that but doesn’t mean that” move that the other side objects to. That is a cost.
  • The inside-time side must handle the many places where God is said to know in advance (Part 4). If Genesis 22:12 shows that God did not know, how does Exodus 3:19 (“I know that the king of Egypt will not let you go”) show that he did?
  • “Now I know” and “tested to reveal” both fit the words. The question is which fits the whole story better. That is where the two sides disagree.

Part 4

Foreknowledge and the open future

What does “all-knowing” mean? Four ways to hold it

The inside-time case

God is perfectly knowing. He knows everything that can be known. A free choice not yet made is a possibility, not yet a fact. God knows all possibilities, every heart, his own plans and the odds. People are “pretty predictable” to him, but he can be surprised by how bad freedom turns out. The future is part settled (what God plans) and part open. This is a coherent position and a minority one with a name: the open view. It claims to take two sets of verses at face value: the “God plans” passages and the “God responds” passages. It also says “did God know or not know” is a theoretical concept that cannot be precisely proved, and it should be held loosely. Many people feel the idea that God did not know “almost makes God not God.” The reply: knowing and responding can come apart, and what matters most is that God really responds.

The outside-of-time case

God’s knowledge is complete and has no gaps.

  • Psalm 147:5 (ESV): “his understanding is beyond measure.”
  • 1 John 3:20 (ESV): “God is greater than our heart, and he knows everything.”
  • Hebrews 4:13 (ESV): “all are naked and exposed to the eyes of him to whom we must give account.”
  • Isaiah 46:10: “declaring the end from the beginning.”
  • Defining omniscience as “everything knowable” is a redefinition of the word, not a denial of it. A God who does not know the future is a God who may be wrong. That is the main reason most Christians reject the open view.

Four models for how God knows tomorrow. All four say God rules, people are responsible, and prayer matters. Take a woman who tomorrow will have the chance to help a neighbor.

  • Settled decree (compatibilist). God planned it, and she helps because she wants to. Her wanting is part of the plan.
  • Simple foreknowledge. God sees what she will choose without causing it. Psalm 139:16 is the favorite text on this side.
  • Middle knowledge. God knows what she would freely do in every possible situation and chose to make the world where she helps.
  • Open. Part of tomorrow is settled by God and part is open. Her free choice is not there to be known yet.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • “Knows everything knowable” redefines omniscience. It is coherent but it is a proposal. It does not touch the plain verses of God’s knowledge: they say “everything,” not “everything knowable.”
  • Middle knowledge holds exhaustive foreknowledge chosen before creation. It is not “working it out as it happens.” Its hard question: what makes “she would help” true before there is any woman (the grounding objection)?
  • Simple foreknowledge says knowing is not making. But if God’s knowledge cannot be wrong, can anyone choose otherwise?
  • Whether “did God know” is a non-essential is itself a judgment people dispute. For many it is central.
  • The hardest questions: for the open view, if the future is partly unknown to God, how can he promise that his purposes will be met? For the settled view, if God knew every choice and still created, is he not the author of the evil he foresaw?

Isaiah’s test: only the true God declares the end from the beginning

The inside-time case

God proves he is God by announcing his own plans and carrying them out. A person can say “at 8:45 I’m driving home” about a future that does not yet exist. An unlimited God can announce a plan thousands of years early and fulfill it. Isaiah 46:10-11 itself says “I have purposed, and I will do it.” The test is that God can say what he will do, which the idols cannot.

The outside-of-time case

This is the strongest single argument for exhaustive foreknowledge. The challenge to the idols in Isaiah is “Tell us what is to come.”

  • Isaiah 41:21-26 (paraphrase): the idols are told to declare the things to come, so that we may know they are gods.
  • Isaiah 44:6-8: God is the first and the last; who is like him, “let him declare it.” Isaiah 48:5.
  • Isaiah 42:9 (paraphrase): new things I declare; before they spring forth I tell you.
  • Isaiah 46:9-11 (ESV): “declaring the end from the beginning and from ancient times things not yet done, saying, ‘My counsel shall stand, and I will accomplish all my purpose.’”
  • Isaiah 40-48 is itself a very early Hebrew argument that the LORD stands above all gods because he declares the future. The Hebrew Bible already makes the claim that is sometimes blamed on later philosophy.
  • Knowledge of the future is the test of deity. A God who knows only probabilities fails Isaiah’s test.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • A plan can fail when the planner is limited (a car accident), but an unlimited planner’s plan does not. That makes “I announced it and then did it” a strong answer for events God himself brings about.
  • The inside-time answer does not cover prophecies that name free acts God did not cause: the actions of pagan kings, betrayals, unbelief. A plan does not explain these unless God also controls those wills, which the inside-time side denies.
  • The classical side must say what the work of planning is, and why scripture talks of God’s planning at all, if everything is already known.

Prophecy: by planning, or by seeing?

The inside-time case

Prophecy does not require a timeless God.

  • Jesus does things “that the Scripture might be fulfilled” (Matthew 26:54-56; John 19:28; Acts 1:16 on Judas). He and the Father planned them.
  • Ephesians 1:4-5 (ESV): “he chose us in him before the foundation of the world... he predestined us for adoption.” Planning before creation.
  • John 13:19 (ESV): “I am telling you this now, before it takes place, that when it does take place you may believe that I am he.” That predicts the cross, which was God’s own plan.
  • Isaiah 46:10: “My purpose will stand, and I will do all that I please.” Psalm 33:11.
  • Some predictions rest on the enemy’s plan (Luke 22:31-32: “Satan demanded to have you”) or on known hearts (Matthew 26:34; John 6:64; Psalm 139:4; Jeremiah 17:10). “I could tell you what’s going to happen without having to be in the future.”
  • God can predict the large shape of history while leaving details to free choice.

The outside-of-time case

Many prophecies depend on human free actions God does not cause.

  • Cyrus is named about 150 years ahead on the traditional dating (Isaiah 44:28-45:1; Ezra 1:1). Josiah is named about 300 years ahead, with a deed (1 Kings 13:2; 2 Kings 23:15-16).
  • Daniel 2, 7 and 11 describe successive empires in advance, with many free agents. Deuteronomy 31:16-21: “I know what they are inclined to do.” Exodus 3:19: “I know that the king of Egypt will not let you go.”
  • Psalm 41:9 and Acts 1:16: the betrayal by a friend, foretold.
  • Proverbs 21:1 (ESV): “The king’s heart is a stream of water in the hand of the LORD; he turns it wherever he will.” Acts 2:23 and 4:27-28 combine human guilt and God’s plan.
  • “I plan it” and “I know it” are not rival explanations. A God above time does both.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • “Because he plans it” covers Cyrus: God can ensure a conquest. It does not cover Peter. Can God ensure that Peter freely denies him three times before dawn? If yes, God can guarantee free acts, and “free acts are unknowable” collapses. If no, it was a lucky guess.
  • To explain named births such as Cyrus and Josiah, the open view must say God brought about those births and names. That is steering, the very thing it says God does not do to a free will.
  • The classical side has its own issue: if everything is already known, what is the work of planning?
  • How does “God plans it” account for prophecies that name free, sinful acts of strangers that God did not cause?

Peter’s denial and Judas’s betrayal

The inside-time case

Jesus knew Peter’s heart and the pressure ahead. Luke 22:31-32 (ESV): “Satan demanded to have you, that he might sift you like wheat, but I have prayed for you that your faith may not fail.” God knows the enemy’s plans and every heart, including Peter’s. “He knows the depth of me fully,” so many things about a person’s future can be named without being settled by an outside decree. On Judas: God chose someone he saw would reject him, to give everyone a chance and to include outsiders. The question is whether Jesus knew the future or saw the tendency of a heart.

The outside-of-time case

Jesus predicts specific free acts, with a count and a deadline, by agents who swore otherwise.

  • Matthew 26:34 (ESV): “Truly, I say to you, this very night, before the rooster crows, you will deny me three times.” Luke 22:34 and John 13:38 repeat it. (Matthew has the rooster crow once; Mark 14:30 has it crow twice before the third denial.)
  • John 13:19: “I am telling you before it takes place.”
  • John 6:64 (ESV): “Jesus knew from the beginning who those were who did not believe, and who would betray him.” John 6:70-71, 13:11, Acts 1:16, Psalm 41:9.
  • If God does not know whether Peter will deny him, how can Jesus say he will, and be right every time?

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The “enemy’s plan” answer explains the sifting, not the three denials. Satan’s request does not explain Peter’s free act. “Near-certain because of Peter’s character” is still not “I am telling you this so that you may believe.”
  • The classical side must answer: if Judas’s betrayal was foreseen and planned, how is Judas guilty and God clean?
  • The inside-time side must answer: how did Jesus know Peter would deny him exactly three times? And what does it say that a close friend of Jesus fails in the exact way Jesus predicts?

The cross: plan, foreknowledge and free evil

The inside-time case

“Plan” means God’s purpose for the cross, with human actors supplying their own means. God planned redemption knowing they could fail (“a plan of redemption is in place before he creates”). The cross was not a script every actor was forced to read.

The outside-of-time case

The most evil free act in history is described as foreknown and planned.

  • Acts 2:23 (ESV): “this Jesus, delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God, you crucified and killed by the hands of lawless men.”
  • Acts 4:27-28 (ESV): Herod, Pilate, the Gentiles and Israel did “whatever your hand and your plan had predestined to take place.”
  • Luke 22:22: “the Son of Man goes as it has been determined.” Romans 8:29-30.
  • Genesis 50:20 (ESV): “You meant evil against me, but God meant it for good.” Joseph’s brothers were fully responsible and God fully sovereign over the same act.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • Acts 4:28 is the hardest text for the open view. If God’s knowledge of that night was only probable, the word choice is strange. The reading that “plan” means purpose works only if you already hold the view.
  • The classical side must say whether foreknowing and planning a free evil makes God responsible for it. If foreknowing a free evil makes God complicit, the cross does too, and scripture calls it salvation. What exactly is the difference between foreknowing and causing?
  • Simple foreknowledge: if the future was seen and fixed, in what sense is anything genuinely open for God to respond to?

“The Lamb slain from the foundation of the world” and the plan before creation

The inside-time case

Revelation 13:8 (NKJV): “the Lamb slain from the foundation of the world.” The verse points to a redemption plan made before creation, “knowing they could fail.” God made man free, knowing it was risky and could really hurt. “I made a plan for Jesus before I even created you.” Ephesians 1:4-5 is read as planning before creation. A plan lets God keep the verse without a timeless God.

The outside-of-time case

1 Peter 1:20 (ESV): Christ “was foreknown before the foundation of the world.” Revelation 13:8 can be read two ways. The NKJV and KJV attach “from the foundation of the world” to the slaying. The ESV attaches it to the names “written before the foundation of the world in the book of life of the Lamb who was slain.” Compare Revelation 17:8. Both sides can cite whichever wording suits them. Both sides read it as plan, not event. If the plan of redemption was in place before creation precisely because God foresaw the Fall as likely, how is that different from knowing it?

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The inside-time side says “plan” fits an in-time God. But a plan made before creation for a sin that had not occurred is hard to tell from foreknowledge of that sin.
  • The classical side cannot use Revelation 13:8 as proof of timelessness: the Greek word order is ambiguous, and 1 Peter 1:20 is firmer for a pre-creation plan.
  • Ephesians 1:4-5: “us” is addressed to the saints at Ephesus (1:1). Reading it as all humanity is a reading. The verse is also the main proof text for individual election.

Psalm 139: “written in your book”

The inside-time case

The Hebrew of verse 16 is ambiguous. Word for word: “Your eyes saw my unformed mass; and in your book all of them were written; days were formed, and not one among them.” What does “all of them” refer to? Reading A (ESV, NIV, NASB): the days. Reading B (KJV; the Jewish JPS translation): the members of the body from verses 13-15 (“in thy book all my members were written”). Context favors B or blunts A. Verses 13-16 are one unit about formation in the womb: kidneys, knitting, frame, unformed mass. “Days were formed” uses yatzar, the potter’s verb for shaping bodies (Genesis 2:7). Even on reading A, “ordained days” in a poem of intimacy reads most naturally as God’s intention and care, not a decree of every free act. Verse 23 (“Search me, God, and know my heart”) suggests God still wants to be told. God may also choose to limit what he looks into, so that what you tell him means something. That is offered as a possibility, not a teaching.

The outside-of-time case

Psalm 139:16 (ESV, in part): “in your book were written, every one of them, the days that were formed for me, when as yet there was none of them.” This is the favorite text for a fixed future. Verse 4 is plainer still: “Even before a word is on my tongue, behold, O LORD, you know it altogether.” That is foreknowledge of a free human act, speech, stated without qualification. Jeremiah 17:10: “I the LORD search the heart.”

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • Reading A has the majority of modern versions. Reading B fits the stanza better. The psalm is a poem of being known, not a treatise on knowledge. It probably settles nothing about open or classical views.
  • Verse 4 is unqualified. The open side must say how “before a word is on my tongue” is not foreknowledge of a free act. The settled side must say that if verse 16 is about the body, what text in the psalm itself says the days of a life are fixed.
  • “God honors your privacy a little” is weak. Psalm 139:4 and Jeremiah 17:10 are plain. The version that survives: he knows, and he still wants to be told.
  • Psalm 139:7-12 is the strongest omnipresence text in scripture and serves both sides.

Surprise: “I thought she would return” and “it did not enter my mind”

The inside-time case

The Bible shows God surprised or grieved by human behavior. That fits a God who has not exhaustively scripted the future. To be shocked, you have to not have known.

  • Jeremiah 3:7, 19-20 (paraphrase of 3:7): “I thought... she would return to me, but she did not.” Isaiah 5:4 (ESV): “What more was there to do for my vineyard, that I have not done in it? When I looked for it to yield grapes, why did it yield wild grapes?”
  • Jeremiah 19:5 (ESV): “which I did not command or decree, nor did it come into my mind.” Also Jeremiah 7:31 and 32:35.
  • Ezekiel 12:3: “Perhaps they will understand.” Jeremiah 26:3 (paraphrase): “perhaps they will listen.” Jeremiah 36:3, 7: “it may be.”
  • Genesis 6:5-6 and 1 Samuel 15:11, 35.
  • God is “so wise” that most things do not surprise him (“our thoughts are pretty predictable”), but the Bible requires leaving room for real surprise.

The outside-of-time case

Surprise language sits next to flat claims of foreknowledge. Isaiah 42:9: “before they spring forth I tell you.” Isaiah 46:10. “Nor did it come into my mind” (Jeremiah 7:31; 19:5; 32:35) is a fixed idiom in Jeremiah meaning “I never commanded this.” It is about God’s will, not his foresight. Isaiah 5:4 asks a question; it does not report a surprise. Jeremiah 3:7 is pathos, the language of a heartbroken lover. Acts 15:18 (a variant reading; use with care): “known to God from eternity are all his works.”

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • Jeremiah 32:35 is weaker than it looks for the open side. It is about what God commanded.
  • The classical side must say what “I thought she would return” means if God always knew.
  • If God can be shocked by sin, how can he promise anything about the future with confidence?
  • If God always knew and only “appears” grieved, is the grief real, or is the Bible telling a kind story?

Knowing what would have happened: Keilah and Tyre

The inside-time case

The open view must read these as God’s inference from character, a very high probability, and the text does not sound like inference. How can there be a definite truth about what someone would freely choose in a situation that never occurs?

The outside-of-time case

Two texts show God knowing what free people would do in situations that never occur.

  • 1 Samuel 23:11-13: God tells David that the men of Keilah will hand him over if he stays. “Will Saul come down? He will.” David leaves, and it does not happen.
  • Matthew 11:21-23: Tyre and Sidon would have repented if they had seen Jesus’ miracles, and Sodom would have remained.

This is the best scriptural support for the middle-knowledge view, and it gives the open view something to answer.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The open side: how do you explain Keilah and Tyre without a settled “would” in God’s mind?
  • The middle-knowledge side: what grounds the truth of “she would have chosen X” if no one chose it and God did not decide it? Knowledge of what Tyre would have done is not knowledge of anything that exists.

Does knowing a choice make it unfree?

The inside-time case

If God’s knowledge cannot be wrong, can anyone choose otherwise? Knowing a choice is different from making it happen, as even the simple-foreknowledge side says. But if the future already exists, God can be “in” it. The open view doubts it: does the future exist? If not, it is not there to be known. A God who is “outside of time, in the future” already sees every choice, and then there is little left to respond to.

The outside-of-time case

Foreknowledge that does not cause the choice is a long-standing way to hold both. Knowing is not making. A God above time does not look forward at a future that “isn’t there yet.” He sees the whole at once. Ordinary experience helps: watching a recorded game, you know the score, and you do not cause it.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • If an infallible foreknowledge does not cause your act, why does it still make the act unfree? That is the inside-time side’s question to answer.
  • If the future was seen and fixed, in what sense is anything genuinely open for God to respond to? That is the simple-foreknowledge side’s.
  • This is where the logic stalls and the texts take over.

Would foreknowledge make God cruel? The marriage argument

The inside-time case

If God already knows and dictates who your spouse is, he is responsible for the divorce and the abuse. A God who said “I give you the green light” knowing they would destroy your life would be cruel. A God who knows the person’s heart, gives wisdom, and says “I’ve got you whatever happens” is a loving guide.

  • James 1:13-17: God tempts no one; every good gift is from above.
  • 1 John 1:5; Habakkuk 1:13; Psalm 5:4: God is light, with no darkness; of purer eyes than to see evil.
  • Jeremiah 29:11 (ESV): “plans for welfare and not for evil, to give you a future and a hope.”
  • Ezekiel 18:20; Deuteronomy 24:16: each soul answers for itself. The abuser, not God, is responsible.
  • Luke 13:1-5; John 9:2-3: Jesus refuses to read disasters as targeted divine judgments.
  • Ecclesiastes 9:11; Matthew 5:45; John 10:10: time and chance; rain on just and unjust; the thief comes to steal and kill.

The outside-of-time case

The argument proves too much.

  • Genesis 50:20 and 45:5-8: free wickedness and God’s intent, both affirmed.
  • Acts 2:23 and 4:27-28: if foreknowing a free evil makes God complicit, the cross does too.
  • Job 1:12, 2:6, 42:7-8: God permits Satan’s afflictions with full knowledge, is not called cruel, and the friends who explained it are rebuked.
  • Isaiah 45:7; Lamentations 3:37-38; Amos 3:6: calamity is under God’s hand, and God is still good.
  • Romans 8:28; 2 Corinthians 12:7-9; Hebrews 12:5-11: the thorn is “given”; discipline comes from a loving Father. The comfort is “God is bending it toward good,” not “God didn’t see it coming.”
  • Jeremiah 29:11 in context (verses 4, 10) is addressed to exiles after seventy years of judgment God sent. The plans are national and come after a catastrophe God authored. The verse does not say God’s plans exclude hardship; it says they end in hope.
  • Proverbs 19:14, 18:22 and Genesis 24: scripture treats God as involved in marriage.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The argument does not escape its own problem. If God knows hearts and people are “pretty predictable,” God saw a very high probability of abuse and still said go. The moral question is the same at 95 percent and at 100 percent. Lowering God’s knowledge moves the hook.
  • The comfort rests on two things the same position also teaches: human freedom and God’s presence in the harm. One reply is that God may have warned and the person did not listen. That works in the classical model too.
  • The open view has its own theodicy cost. If God could not have prevented the harm without violating freedom, he is not cruel, but he is also not able to promise much. “No matter what your spouse does to you, I’ve got you” is a promise of presence, not protection.
  • What stands on shared ground: God does not author evil (James 1:13) and abusers are responsible (Ezekiel 18). The question is not whether God wills evil but whether he knows and permits it.
  • For the classical side: what exactly is the difference between foreknowing and causing, and does that difference help a person in pain?

Part 5

Prayer: does it change what God does?

Does prayer change what God does next?

The inside-time case

Prayer is a real exchange between a Father in time and a child in time. What you did made God do something differently now. If you do not pray it makes a difference. If you do, it makes a difference. The narratives give people causal weight.

  • Exodus 32:9-14, Numbers 14:11-20 and Deuteronomy 9:18-20: Moses intercedes and God relents (see Part 3).
  • 2 Kings 20:1-6 and Isaiah 38:1-5: “you shall die”; Hezekiah weeps; “I will add fifteen years.” An announced outcome reversed in one chapter.
  • Genesis 18:22-33: Abraham bargains from fifty to ten. God stays in the conversation. Genesis 18:17: “Shall I hide from Abraham what I am about to do?”
  • Amos 7:3, 6: the LORD relents twice after Amos pleads.
  • James 5:16-18 and 4:2: Elijah’s prayer, three and a half years of drought; “You do not have, because you do not ask.”
  • Luke 11:5-13 (the friend at midnight); Luke 18:1-8 (the persistent widow); Matthew 7:7-11 (“Ask, and it will be given to you”): the grammar of petition assumes asking makes a difference.
  • Ezekiel 22:30-31 and Isaiah 59:16: judgment fell because no one stood in the gap.
  • Luke 22:31-32: Jesus prays for Peter’s faith. Jeremiah 33:3 (ESV): “Call to me and I will answer you, and will tell you great and hidden things.” Exodus 33:11: Moses spoke with God “as a man speaks to his friend.” Jeremiah 18:7-10 states responsiveness as policy.
  • Matthew 6:10: “Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven.” The will of heaven is released on earth through prayer.
  • Commands that lose force if all is settled: “pray without ceasing” (1 Thessalonians 5:17), “go and make disciples” (Matthew 28:19), “Resist the devil, and he will flee from you” (James 4:7).
  • Four models explain prayer differently. In the settled-decree model, God plans the prayer and the answer together. In simple foreknowledge, he knows your prayer and genuinely answers. In middle knowledge, free prayers are folded into his plan. In the open view, prayer shapes a still-open future. The practical difference: in a settled model God is “out there overseeing it all”; in the relational model, “me and my father are navigating this together.”
  • Three tests of any theology: does it make you want to be with him, to be like him, and to partner with him?

The outside-of-time case

Prayer is the appointed means through which God does what he eternally willed to do in response.

  • God ordains the means as well as the ends. Acts 18:9-10: Paul is told “I have many in this city,” and he keeps speaking. Acts 27:22-25 and 31: Paul says no one will be lost, then says unless the sailors stay on the ship, they cannot be saved. Promise and means go together.
  • Matthew 6:8 (ESV): “your Father knows what you need before you ask him.” Prayer is not information for God. Isaiah 65:24: “Before they call I will answer.”
  • James 5:16-18: Elijah was “a man with a nature like ours,” and his prayer stopped the rain. The classical side has no trouble with answered prayer.
  • Ezekiel 22:30 is a lament in a judgment oracle. It does not say God changed a plan. And the same God foretold the exile (Leviticus 26; Deuteronomy 28). It cuts both ways: it supports a God who sought an intercessor, and also shows that God had decided what judgment deserved and intercession was the appointed off-ramp.
  • The classical answer: God eternally wills to act in response to prayers he eternally knows.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • Both sides agree prayer matters. They differ on mechanism. The classical tradition says prayer is the appointed means. In practice the two are close. The texts (Exodus 32, 2 Kings 20) are written from the human side and sound like the inside-time version.
  • The classical side must say in what sense Hezekiah’s tears changed anything, if the answer was eternally fixed and the prayer eternally foreseen. Is “God ordained the prayer” a comfort or a loss? Is a prayer a request or a ritual?
  • The inside-time side must say how anyone can trust that a promise will hold, if prayer can change God’s course.
  • Many people hold a settled theology and a relational prayer life at once. Sermons and textbooks often miss this.

Prayers that are refused, and prayer as God’s own work

The inside-time case

Much that is unanswered is explained by freedom, by ignorance, or by the attack of an enemy. Hosea 4:6: “My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge.” 2 Peter 1:3-4: God’s promises come through knowledge of him, like an inheritance in your name that you never claim because you never learned of it. Hebrews 11:13, 39: some promises wait. Unreceived is not always unreached for. Jesus himself prays “not as I will” and so shows that a prayer can be shaped by relationship with the Father without being empty.

The outside-of-time case

Prayer does not reliably change God’s mind.

  • 2 Samuel 12:16-23: David pleads for his child; the child dies; “I shall go to him, but he will not return to me.”
  • 2 Corinthians 12:7-9: Paul asks three times; the thorn stays; “My grace is sufficient for you.”
  • Matthew 26:39, 42: “not as I will, but as you will.” 1 John 5:14: “if we ask anything according to his will he hears us.” The model prayers shape the prayer to God’s will.
  • Romans 8:26-27: the Spirit intercedes for us “according to the will of God.” Even our prayers are God’s work in us. Philippians 2:13: God “works in you, both to will and to work for his good pleasure.” Ezekiel 36:37: “I will let the house of Israel ask me to do this for them.”

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The inside-time view must handle refusals. David’s child and Paul’s thorn were not overridden by anyone. God declined. The view cannot say all refusals are caused by free agents. In your model, who said no?
  • The classical side must say why sincere prayers for healing go unanswered, and whether its picture helps. If God has already decided every outcome, what does the praying person contribute that God needed?
  • Both sides can ask: if prayer can change the outcome, why do sincere prayers for healing go unanswered, and does the open view add anything to that problem?

Does a settled future make a passive church?

The inside-time case

If the future is settled, why evangelize or pray for the sick? A settled future brings comfort but also “a certain measure of inactivity.” Fatalism is real: “whatever will be will be” does paralyze. A man in a long run of loss told himself, “God is in control, so this must be his will,” and the belief stopped him from resisting. Hosea 4:6; James 4:7 (“Resist the devil”); Ephesians 4:27; 1 Peter 5:8-9. Eighteenth-century England had a hyper-strand of the settled view that held that offering the gospel to all was improper.

The outside-of-time case

That describes fatalism and calls it the settled view. Fatalism says the outcome comes regardless of means. The settled view says God settles the means with the outcome, so prayer and preaching are how it happens.

  • The modern missions movement was launched in the late eighteenth century by a believer in a settled future who argued against the hyper-strand. Great evangelists in the same line held a settled view. A seventeenth-century confession says God’s decree takes away neither the will of creatures nor “the liberty or contingency of second causes.”
  • Acts 18:9-10: “Keep on speaking... for I have many in this city.” 2 Timothy 2:10: “I endure everything for the sake of the elect.” Romans 10:14: “How are they to hear without someone preaching?” Paul draws confidence to preach from a settled outcome.
  • Philippians 2:12-13: “work out your own salvation... for it is God who works in you.”

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The three-part test is good and both views can pass it.
  • Some people do hear “God is in control” as “nothing I do matters.” Teaching against that hearing is worth doing, but it does not defeat the view.
  • The inside-time side must say how it handles a founder of modern missions who believed in a settled future.
  • The settled side must say in what sense an unprayed prayer could have been prayed. What does “if” mean in “if my people will humble themselves” (compare Jeremiah 18:7-10)?
  • Whether a view makes people active is not a test of truth.

Does God need a man? Can you limit God on the earth?

The inside-time case

God chose to work on earth through people. He looks for a man to work through, and sometimes does less because he finds none.

  • Psalm 115:16 (ESV): “The heavens are the LORD’s heavens, but the earth he has given to the children of man.” Also Genesis 1:26-28 and Psalm 8:4-8: delegated rule is a creation ordinance. The earth is given to humanity (Genesis 1:28).
  • Ezekiel 22:30 (ESV): “I sought for a man among them who should build up the wall and stand in the breach before me for the land, that I should not destroy it, but I found none.” Isaiah 59:16 and 63:5: “he saw that there was no man... and was appalled that there was no one to intercede.”
  • 2 Chronicles 16:9: “the eyes of the LORD run to and fro throughout the whole earth, to strengthen those whose heart is blameless toward him.” Genesis 6:8: Noah found favor. Exodus 3:10: Moses sent.
  • Romans 10:14-15; Matthew 9:37-38; 2 Corinthians 5:20; 1 Corinthians 3:9: human agency is the ordinary means of God’s work.
  • Mark 6:5-6 (ESV): “he could do no mighty work there... And he marveled because of their unbelief.” Matthew 13:58.
  • He is unlimited, but in his sovereignty he has chosen to limit his activity on the earth to partnership with people. Limited by choice, not by weakness. “God allowed it” is semantics when God could stop it.

The outside-of-time case

The earth is the LORD’s (Psalm 24:1). Psalm 115:3 is thirteen verses before verse 16: “Our God is in the heavens; he does all that he pleases.” Daniel 4:35: “none can stay his hand.” God is not served by human hands as though he needed anything (Acts 17:25). Job 41:11 and Romans 11:35. Isaiah 59:16 and 63:5 continue: “then his own arm brought him salvation.” When no man is found, God acts alone. Isaiah 46:10-11, Proverbs 21:1 and Psalm 33:10-11: God’s purposes are not finally limitable by people. Romans 9:16-18 and Exodus 9:16: God works even through hostile men like Pharaoh.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • “God chooses to work through people” holds. “God needs people” does not. The first is the shape of biblical history. The second contradicts Acts 17:25, Isaiah 59:16 and the argument that God’s love was complete before creation.
  • Psalm 115:16 describes delegation, not abdication. A landlord who gives tenants the run of a house has not lost the deed.
  • Mark 6:5 is narrower than “you can limit God.” Jesus “could not” because faith is the ordinary channel, not because Nazareth had a veto.
  • “You can limit God” will be heard as “God is stuck without you.” The practical point (your yes matters) is right.
  • The inside-time side must say whether God needs a man or prefers one. The classical side must say what it means that Jesus “could not” in Nazareth, if human participation is the ordinary means and God could do it alone.

Can the church hasten the return?

The inside-time case

Prayer and maturity may speed the end. 2 Peter 3:12 (ESV): “waiting for and hastening the coming of the day of God.” Romans 8:19-22: creation groans and waits. Ephesians 4:1-16 and 4:13: the maturity of the body. Revelation 19:7: the marriage of the Lamb comes when “his Bride has made herself ready.” Romans 8:26 and 8:34: the Spirit and Christ intercede. The view leans open, though “maybe it’s both.”

The outside-of-time case

Matthew 24:36 and Mark 13:32: no one knows the day or hour, not even the Son. The date is the Father’s. “Hastening” is the church doing what it is told. The classical reading finds in Matthew 24:36 and Acts 1:7 (“times or seasons that the Father has fixed by his own authority”) a settled time. The bride getting ready is the means by which the settled day arrives.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • Whether the date is fixed or hastened by the church’s maturity is held open by thoughtful people on both sides.
  • If the date is fixed, “hasten” is hard to read plainly. If it is not, the Son’s not knowing the hour (Mark 13:32) is then about a day not yet settled, which the classical side can use against the inside-time reading of the Son’s human limits.

Part 6

Sovereignty, control and free will

Sovereign: “in charge,” or “in control”?

The inside-time case

The Bible’s meaning of “sovereign” is rule, kingship and authority. It does not mean God personally determines each event. “In charge” and “in control” are different. A parent is in charge of a house and does not control a child’s breathing, eating, sleeping or choice to jump off the stairs. If the child hurts herself, the parent did not will it. God sets the rules, the boundaries and the end. He does not decide each choice.

  • 1 Timothy 6:15 (ESV): “the blessed and only Sovereign, the King of kings and Lord of lords.” The vocabulary is royal: throne, kingdom. A king reigns over subjects who can rebel.
  • Psalm 103:19 (ESV): “The LORD has established his throne in the heavens, and his kingdom rules over all.” Psalm 103:13 puts Father and King in one psalm: “As a father shows compassion to his children, so the LORD shows compassion.”
  • Psalm 115:3 and 135:6: he does all that he pleases. If it pleases him to make free people, freedom is among the things he does. Job 42:2. Daniel 4:25-26, 32: God rules over all, gives kingdoms to whom he will.
  • Defining sovereignty as control makes God responsible for all evil.

The outside-of-time case

  • Ephesians 1:11 (ESV): God “works all things according to the counsel of his will.”
  • Proverbs 16:9 (ESV): “The heart of man plans his way, but the LORD establishes his steps.” Proverbs 16:33: “The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD.”
  • Isaiah 46:10; Daniel 4:35 (ESV): “he does according to his will among the host of heaven... and none can stay his hand or say to him, ‘What have you done?’”
  • Lamentations 3:37-38 and Isaiah 45:7: even calamity is placed under God’s command. Amos 3:6.
  • Ezra 1:1: “the LORD stirred up the spirit of Cyrus.” Acts 4:27-28: Herod, Pilate and the crowds did what God’s plan decided.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • Daniel 4:35 is a king’s confession after his restoration (4:34-37). It speaks in general terms about God’s rule, but it is Nebuchadnezzar’s own testimony after God humbled him. It should not be treated as a definition by either side.
  • The word swap is good. “Control” suggests micromanagement that few classical theologians teach. A seventeenth-century confession says the decree does not remove the liberty of second causes.
  • The hard texts show God steering particular hearts at particular moments. “In charge” survives only if it includes that. The house picture already does: a parent who is in charge still steps in sometimes.
  • Ephesians 1:11 can be read both ways: he brings every event into his purpose, or he causes each one. Serious commentators hold both.
  • For the in-charge side: if God is in charge but not in control, what is he doing in “the LORD stirred up the spirit of Cyrus”? For the in-control side: if God ordains every choice, including the evil ones, is “secondary causes” a real answer or only a name? If God does not control events, what does “he rules over all” give anyone in practice when evil is rising?

Can God’s will be resisted? The two wills

The inside-time case

Some of what God wants does not happen.

  • Luke 7:30 (ESV): “the Pharisees and the lawyers rejected the purpose of God for themselves.”
  • Matthew 23:37 (ESV): “How often would I have gathered your children together... and you were not willing!” Luke 13:34. Acts 7:51: “You always resist the Holy Spirit.”
  • 1 Timothy 2:4 (ESV): God “desires all people to be saved.” 2 Peter 3:9; Ezekiel 18:23, 32: he has no pleasure in the death of the wicked. And yet some perish.
  • Hebrews 6:4-6: even people with full knowledge can reject God, so more revelation is not guaranteed to produce obedience.
  • Psalm 91 promises protection. 2 Peter 1:3-4 says his divine power has granted us all that pertains to life and godliness “through the knowledge of him.” Promises must be accessed through knowing him, like an inheritance in your name that you never claim. Hosea 4:6.
  • A control model must split God’s will into two to explain this.

The outside-of-time case

The tradition distinguishes two wills: God’s revealed will (what he commands and desires) and his decretive will (what he ordains). Ezekiel 33:11 and 1 Timothy 2:4 speak of the first. Psalm 115:3 and Ephesians 1:11 speak of the second. Job 42:2 (ESV): “I know that you can do all things, and that no purpose of yours can be thwarted.” The Pharisee text is about what he commanded, not what he decreed. John 6:37 and Romans 8:30: everyone the Father gives to the Son comes and is glorified. “Desires” in 1 Timothy 2:4 and 2 Peter 3:9 is not “wills” in the sense of decree.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • Scripture plainly shows God’s revealed will resisted (Matthew 23:37; Acts 7:51). That is what survives for the inside-time side.
  • If God’s purposes can fail, what guarantees the final outcome he promised? The inside-time side must answer.
  • If God decreed that most people be lost, how does that fit “desires all to be saved”? The settled side must answer.
  • The “two wills” move is a distinction the text does not name. The classical side is reading it in to hold both sets of verses together.

Can God override a will? What God cannot do

The inside-time case

God’s power is unlimited, but his character limits what he will do, including overriding free will. He cannot lie, sin, be tempted or deny himself. These are not weaknesses.

  • Titus 1:2 (ESV): “God, who never lies.” James 1:13: “God cannot be tempted with evil, and he himself tempts no one.” 2 Timothy 2:13: “he cannot deny himself.”
  • Psalm 115:3 and 135:6: “He does all that he pleases.” Job 42:2.
  • In the same way he chooses not to override human free will. This is self-limitation, and he does not need to control people to get his way. God is wise enough to reach his goals without forcing any person: he does not need to create a Pharaoh or a Judas when there are enough bad actors already.
  • This is a minority view in Christianity. Most Christian theology holds that God does work on wills.

The outside-of-time case

Freedom is not named as a limit on God anywhere in the text. The limits scripture names are about his character: lie, tempt, deny himself. Free will as a limit is a deduction.

  • Proverbs 21:1: “he turns it wherever he will.”
  • Ezra 1:1; Philippians 2:13 (“it is God who works in you, both to will and to work for his good pleasure”); Acts 4:27-28; Genesis 50:20: God steers human wills toward his ends without erasing their responsibility.
  • Exodus 4:21, Romans 9:18 and John 6:44 show God acting on the will.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • Unlike the other three “cannot” items, “God will not override free will” has no verse. It is an inference.
  • The inside-time side must say where scripture says God will not override a will, and how it handles Exodus 4:21 and Romans 9:18.
  • The classical side must say: if God works all wills, how can a person be held guilty for what God made them will?

Can a decreed choice be free? Compatibilism

The inside-time case

The settled-decree view (compatibilism) says God plans every event, including human choices, and people still act “freely” because they act as they want. This is a contradiction. If God decreed it and it is irresistible, you were not free. A woman who helps her neighbor “because she wants to” has a wanting that is part of the plan, and she could not choose otherwise. Prayer becomes automatic: you pray only because God planned you would. If God determined every event, how does he stay innocent when people go to hell? Who goes to heaven or hell is fixed.

The outside-of-time case

We call an act free when it flows from the person’s own desires without outside compulsion.

  • Genesis 50:20 and Acts 4:27-28 show scripture treating one act as both fully free and fully planned.
  • Philippians 2:12-13: “work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in you.”
  • Prayer matters because God ordains prayer as the means of answers.
  • Holding “one hundred percent man and one hundred percent God” in one person is a case of two truths that look logically incompatible and that the church holds together. The same move works for sovereignty and responsibility.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The inside-time side must say: if you accept the paradox of Christ’s two natures, why not accept the paradox of sovereignty and responsibility?
  • The classical side must say: what is left of “could have done otherwise” when the choice was decreed before the foundation of the world?
  • The incarnation comparison is weak in one direction: the church has called Christ’s two natures a mystery, not a logical contradiction. Whether decreed freedom is a mystery or a contradiction is exactly the dispute.

Pharaoh: God hardened, Pharaoh hardened, “it became hard”

The inside-time case

The text says God hardened Pharaoh’s heart, that Pharaoh hardened his own heart, and that it “became hard.” The question is order. Pharaoh, who was treated as a god, started with a hard heart. God’s repeated mercies pressed on it, ten escalating signs with only a request to let the people go and worship. Each was a chance to relent. God did not need to manipulate him.

  • Exodus 7:13, 22; 8:19; 9:7: “Pharaoh’s heart was hardened” (passive). Exodus 8:15 (ESV): “he hardened his heart and would not listen to them.” Also 8:32; 9:34-35: Pharaoh hardens his own heart again after the hail.
  • Through the first five plagues the narrator never names the LORD as hardener. The LORD is first named at the sixth (Exodus 9:12). The ledger runs: Pharaoh first, then the LORD.
  • 1 Samuel 6:6: the Philistines read it as Pharaoh hardening himself: “Why do you harden your hearts as the Egyptians and Pharaoh hardened their hearts?”
  • Exodus 5: straw withdrawn, Pharaoh’s response when asked simply to let Israel go.
  • Pharaoh, a ruler worshiped as divine with a vast labor force, was not going to yield easily. God, fully knowing Pharaoh, confirmed what Pharaoh had chosen.
  • Romans 11:23 makes hardening reversible: “if they do not continue in their unbelief, will be grafted in.”

The outside-of-time case

  • Exodus 4:21 (ESV): “I will harden his heart, so that he will not let the people go.” Also 7:3. God announces the hardening before the first plague, before Pharaoh hardened anything.
  • Exodus 9:12; 10:1, 20, 27; 11:10; 14:4, 8, 17. Exodus 14:17: “I will harden the hearts of the Egyptians so that they go in after them.” The hardening has a purpose God names.
  • Exodus 9:16, quoted in Romans 9:17: God “raised you up” for this purpose. Joshua 11:20; Deuteronomy 2:30; John 12:39-40; Psalm 105:25 show the same pattern for other peoples.
  • Exodus 3:19: “I know that the king of Egypt will not let you go.”

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • No verse says Moses hardened Pharaoh. The Hebrew uses a passive: “Pharaoh’s heart was hardened” (Exodus 7:13, 22). The agents in the text are the LORD, Pharaoh, and the passive. By one count it is about ten for the LORD (counting announcements), three for Pharaoh, and six passive. God is the stated agent more often than a “two or three” count would suggest.
  • The narrative order favors the inside-time reading: first passive, then Pharaoh’s own, then God’s. The classical side must explain it. But the order of narration is not necessarily the order of cause, and 4:21 can be read as foresight of a heart already set.
  • The magicians matched two plagues, not most: blood (7:22) and frogs (8:7). At the gnats they said, “This is the finger of God” (8:18-19). “Pharaoh” means “great house,” not “Ra.”
  • The repair is forcing versus confirming. Hardening a heart already set is not reversing a free choice. That fits Pharaoh. What it cannot say is that God never acts on a will at all, because Exodus 9:12 and 14:8 will not allow it.
  • For the inside-time side: what does “I will harden” in Exodus 4:21 mean before any refusal? For the classical side: if the hardening is God’s act from the start, why does the narrator spend five plagues saying Pharaoh hardened himself, and why does the text hold Pharaoh responsible?

Jonah and Paul: persuaded without being overridden?

The inside-time case

In the stories where God seems to force someone, he is acting like a father. Jonah and Paul wanted to serve God. God helped them see what they lacked.

  • Jonah 1-2: Jonah fled because he knew God would be merciful to Nineveh (4:2). God sends the storm, the sailors and the fish to bring a prophet who “wants to obey” back. Jonah’s resistance is that he disagrees with God’s mercy. In the end he obeys and argues, a will intact. Jonah 4:11: God pities the children who do not know their right hand from their left. Matthew 12:39-40: the sign of Jonah.
  • Acts 9:3-18: light, blindness, then scales fell from Saul’s eyes. Acts 22:3 and Philippians 3:5-6 (Gamaliel’s student; a Pharisee of Pharisees, zealous in persecution); Galatians 1:14: a lifelong passion for God. John 8:44 shows that people can hold the right text with the wrong spirit.
  • “I know you will like it. Eat it.” Is that manipulation or wisdom? A light from heaven that blinded you might change your mind. That is information, not force. Paul did not need a changed destiny; he needed his eyes opened to the truth he was seeking.
  • Will everyone obey if they had more of God? No (Hebrews 6:4-6).

The outside-of-time case

  • Acts 26:14 (ESV): “It is hard for you to kick against the goads.” Saul was resisting, not simply seeking, and the encounter overrode him.
  • Galatians 1:15-16: “he who had set me apart before I was born, and who called me by his grace, was pleased to reveal his Son to me.” Paul himself credits God’s prior choice.
  • Jonah 1:4, 1:17: “The LORD hurled a great wind on the sea... the LORD appointed a great fish.” God acts on Jonah to reverse his course.
  • Jonah 2:1-2: Jonah prays from inside the fish. The “realm of the dead” in 2:2 is poetry for near death; reading Jonah as having died is the inside-time side’s own.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • Paul and Jonah were pressed hard. A blinding light, a storm and a fish are strong persuasion. “Not forced” holds. “Not overridden in any sense” is harder.
  • The sharpest objection: if God gives a big shove to a sincere seeker like Paul, why not to every sincere seeker? Scripture says those who seek will find (Matthew 7:7-8; Jeremiah 29:13). The inside-time side owes a reply for why Paul got a decisive encounter and not every seeker.
  • The classical side must say: if conversion is God’s sovereign gift, why does scripture invite and warn rather than only announce?

Romans 9: one note of the chord, or the whole chord? The potter, twice

The inside-time case

Romans 9 plays one note, “God can choose and do what he wants.” Jeremiah 18’s potter plays the rest: the clay’s response changes what the potter does. Theology should play every key.

  • Jeremiah 18:1-10: the vessel was spoiled in the potter’s hand, so he remade it. “If that nation... turns from its evil, I will relent.” Other potter texts: Isaiah 29:16; 45:9; 64:8.
  • Romans 9:22: God “bore with great patience the objects of his wrath.” Romans 9:30-32: Israel stumbled because they pursued righteousness “not by faith.” Response is in the chapter.
  • Romans 11:23, 32: “if they do not continue in their unbelief, will be grafted in”; “that he may have mercy on all.” Paul’s own argument ends where Romans 9 alone does not. Read Romans 9-11 as one letter.
  • A real potter reworks hard clay many times: adds water, waits, tries again. He discards it only if it will not yield.

The outside-of-time case

  • Romans 9:11 (ESV): “though they were not yet born and had done nothing either good or bad, in order that God’s purpose of election might continue.” Romans 9:16: “So then it depends not on human will or exertion, but on God, who has mercy.” Paul rules out the clay’s response on purpose.
  • Romans 9:18, 21: “he has mercy on whomever he wills, and he hardens whomever he wills”; the potter has the right to make some pottery for honorable use and some for dishonorable.
  • Romans 9:19-20: Paul himself raises the objection, “Why does he still find fault?” and answers by pointing to God’s right, not to human response. “Who are you, O man, to answer back to God?” The image is about right, not patience.
  • Romans 9:22-23: vessels “prepared for destruction” and “prepared beforehand for glory.” Romans 8:29-30 and Ephesians 1:4-5, 11 are the larger frame.
  • Isaiah 29:16 and 45:9 use the potter against talking back. Jeremiah 19:10-11: “Just as this potter’s jar is smashed and cannot be repaired” (paraphrase). Jeremiah’s potter also ends a pot.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • “Multiple goes” is not in the text. Jeremiah 18:4 has one marred pot and one reshaping. The patience is a fair reading of verses 7-10; the pottery detail is an illustration.
  • Whether Romans 9 is about God’s freedom to choose peoples for roles or about individual election is one of the oldest splits in Christian reading. A usual non-settled answer is roles of peoples in history. That is respected but not settled. Romans 11 changes how 9 reads; 9:11 and 16 resist the chord.
  • For the chord side: how does a potter waiting on the clay fit “before the twins had done anything good or bad”? For the one-note side: Romans 11:23 and 11:32 end the argument on mercy “on all.” How does 9:18 stand at the end of the letter?
  • Logic is secondary to the full revelation. Jesus is fully God and fully man, which the mind cannot fit.

“Jacob I loved, Esau I hated”

The inside-time case

“Hated” is a Hebrew comparison meaning “favored less,” and the choice concerns the roles of two peoples, not the souls of two men.

  • Malachi 1:2-3 (ESV): “Yet I have loved Jacob but Esau I have hated.”
  • Genesis 29:30-31: Jacob loved Rachel more than Leah; the next verse calls Leah “unloved” (Hebrew “hated”). The word is comparative. Luke 14:26 vs Matthew 10:37: “hate father and mother” is “loves more than me” in Matthew.
  • Genesis 25:23: “Two nations are in your womb.” Malachi speaks of the nation Edom centuries later. Genesis 33:9: Esau says “I have enough.” Genesis 36:7: Esau’s and Jacob’s flocks were too large to share a region; Esau became a great nation. Deuteronomy 2:5: God gave him Seir. Deuteronomy 23:7: “You shall not abhor an Edomite.”
  • Psalm 5:5 and 139:21-22 (“hate those who do wickedness”) are read as hating wickedness, not people. Romans 1:18 speaks of wrath against sin, as one wants wrath against cancer.
  • Ruth (a Moabite woman placed in the line of Jesus; compare Matthew 1:5): God takes those who want him from the wrong family.
  • Look at the fruit: God’s treatment of Esau does not look like hate. The reason for the choice could be foreknowledge of hearts, or God breaking the “firstborn” box to show his nature.

The outside-of-time case

  • Romans 9:11-13: Paul says the choice was made “before they had done anything good or bad, in order that God’s purpose of election might continue.” Foreknowledge of Esau’s heart is excluded by the text itself.
  • Genesis 25:23 was spoken to Rebekah before birth, tying the choice to a word of God before either child acted.
  • Malachi 1:3-4: “I have turned his hill country into a desolation.” For the nation, “hated” includes real judgment. Obadiah 10-14: Edom’s violence against Judah is the stated cause. Romans 9:14: Paul expects the objection “Is there injustice on God’s part?” right after quoting it. A mild comparison would not provoke that.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The idiom is real and well attested. “Love less” fits the Genesis frame and Jesus’ saying. But Malachi gives some weight back, so “favored less” understates it. Both readings can explain Paul’s “Is God unjust?”
  • If the choice was not based on works or on foreknown works (Romans 9:11), what was it based on besides God’s own will? The inside-time side must answer. If Romans 9:13 is about individuals, why does Malachi apply it to a nation centuries later? The classical side must answer.

Called before birth: Jeremiah, Paul and the “before the foundation” texts

The inside-time case

Calling before birth is real, but most callings are discovered along the way. Moses was about eighty before his assignment. Ephesians 1:4-5 is a family plan that predates creation: God planned to have sons and daughters. “Us” is addressed to the saints at Ephesus (1:1), and reading it as all humanity is a corporate-election view, not individual selection.

The outside-of-time case

God’s choice precedes the person.

  • Jeremiah 1:5 (ESV): “Before I formed you in the womb I knew you, and before you were born I consecrated you.”
  • Galatians 1:15: set apart before birth. Romans 8:29-30: those he foreknew he predestined and glorified.
  • Ephesians 1:4-5 and 1:11. Ephesians 1:4 (ESV): “he chose us in him before the foundation of the world.”

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The text of Ephesians 1 supports both a corporate and an individual reading. The same verse is the classical proof text for individual election. It is also read as a family plan.
  • Jeremiah 1:5 can mean “I knew and chose you for this role” without deciding the destiny of every person.

Does God allow evil, or do we? Job and permission

The inside-time case

“God allows on the earth what humans allow.” The language “God didn’t cause it but allowed it” is questioned: did he allow it, or did we? God gave authority and freedom to people. What happens on earth is allowed by people’s freedom. He does not approve of all he tolerates. The weight of evil rests on freedom, sin, the devil and human choices, not on God.

  • Psalm 115:16; Genesis 1:28: the earth is given to people.
  • James 4:7; Ephesians 4:27; 1 Peter 5:8-9: believers are told to resist the devil, which assumes the attack is not simply to be accepted.
  • James 1:13, 17; John 10:10: God tempts no one; only good comes from him. Acts 10:38: Jesus “went about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil.”
  • Job 3:25 (ESV): “For the thing that I fear comes upon me.” The reading: Job gave the opening for the attack, not God.
  • Matthew 19:8 and Deuteronomy 24:1: “Because of your hardness of heart Moses allowed you to divorce” shows a case where God’s heart was one thing and the practice allowed was another.
  • Luke 7:30; 1 Timothy 2:4; Hosea 4:6; 2 Peter 1:3-4: some of God’s will is not done.
  • A country that refuses to tolerate murder is not allowing it. God works through the authority people have.
  • “Permission” language puts God as a knowing bystander who could stop it.

The outside-of-time case

  • Job 1:12 (ESV): “Behold, all that he has is in your hand.” Job 2:6: “Behold, he is in your hand; only spare his life.” Twice God sets the terms. Job 2:3: “you incited me against him to destroy him without reason.”
  • Job 1:21-22 and 2:10: “The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away.” Job 42:11 speaks of “all the evil that the LORD had brought upon him.” Job 42:7-8: God rebukes the friends who explained it.
  • Mark 5:13: Jesus “gave them permission” at the pigs. Luke 22:31-32: “Satan demanded to have you... but I have prayed for you.”
  • Genesis 50:20 and Romans 8:28: God works good out of what he did not send. Acts 2:23 and 4:27-28.
  • Exodus 4:11 (ESV): “Who has made man’s mouth? Who makes him mute, or deaf, or seeing, or blind? Is it not I, the LORD?” Exodus 12:23, “the destroyer”: that reading needs permission, not its absence. Isaiah 45:7; Amos 3:6.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The Job reading of the inside-time side reverses the narrator. Job 3:25 is a lament in the poetry. Job 1 and 2 are the prose frame that tells the reader what happened, and in it God gives permission twice. “Allows” is the word to keep.
  • What survives for the inside-time side: much harm comes through human and demonic freedom, God does not author it, and believers are told to resist.
  • Both sides have comfort. “In all things God works for the good” (Romans 8:28) and “God meant it for good” (Genesis 50:20) have given many rest.
  • The classical side’s hard question: if God permits harm he could prevent, with full knowledge of the result, what does “God is not the author of evil” add to the answer a suffering person needs? And if God ordains evil acts for good ends, why is it right to say he is not the author of sin (James 1:13)?
  • The inside-time side’s hard question: how do you read Job 1:12 and 2:6 if not as God granting limited permission? And if God neither willed nor allowed a child’s abuse, what is he doing about it, and what does it mean that he could have stopped it by taking away freedom?

Judgment, the flood and the arc of history: wrath or kindness?

The inside-time case

Harsh acts like the flood and Sodom were kindness: cutting out an irreversible cancer to protect the rest. God’s wrath against sin is aimed at what is killing his children, like a father’s anger aimed at a cancer. The flood is surgery, not spite.

  • Genesis 6:1-8: the flood. Genesis 19: Sodom, where the men struck blind still groped for the door. Genesis 38: Judah’s sons struck down.
  • Wickedness grows like a seed. Eventually there are no men left, only the wicked, and then God cuts it off.
  • The old covenant was two-sided and “if-then,” so God punished under it. In the New Testament people are not struck down for sin. Acts 5 (Ananias and Sapphira): the inside-time side says it does not think God did it and defers the case.
  • Romans 5:8 and John 3:16: God’s heart toward people is the same before and after the cross. Romans 2:4: God’s kindness leads to repentance. Psalm 30:5: his anger is for a moment. Isaiah 54:7-8.
  • The kingdom grows. Isaiah 9:7: “Of the increase of his government and of peace there will be no end.” Matthew 13:24-30 (wheat and tares): let both grow until harvest. Matthew 28:19-20; Romans 10:14-15: go and make disciples. Matthew 24 and Revelation are read as mostly fulfilled.

The outside-of-time case

  • The flood and Sodom are God’s holy wrath, as the New Testament says: 2 Peter 2:5-6; Luke 17:26-30. Calling them “kindness” can soften what the text presents as judgment.
  • 2 Timothy 3:1, 13 (“evil people and impostors will go on from bad to worse”) and Matthew 24:12 (“the love of many will grow cold”) speak against steady improvement.
  • Malachi 3:6 and Hebrews 13:8: if God is the same, the covenant argument cannot explain a change in how he judges. Acts 5:5, 10 reads as God striking people under the new covenant. Acts 12:23; 1 Corinthians 11:30; Revelation 6:16.
  • Whether the world is improving is a matter of historical opinion, and serious scholars dispute it.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • The claim that the world is steadily getting better is a historical judgment, not a text. Anyone who makes it needs evidence from outside Scripture.
  • The inside-time side must say: if God is not controlling events, why trust that history is trending toward good rather than away from it?
  • The classical side must say: if God ordained and foresaw the corruption that required the flood, how do you describe the flood as grief rather than as the end of his own plan?
  • Peter calls the flood and the final fire destruction as well as cleansing (2 Peter 3:6-7, 10). So renewal comes through judgment.

What does each view say to a person in pain?

The inside-time case

The view that God is “in control of everything” can leave sufferers believing God sent or allowed their pain, and drive them from prayer. A man in ten years of constant pain stopped praying because in his view God controls everything, could heal with a snap, and has not. The relational response: this pain was not God’s will, God did not even allow it, he is grieved by it and wants to heal. Intellectual non-believers stumble on “how can a good, loving God allow so much evil?” A better theology removes that tension. Jeremiah 33:3 invites a relationship that matters. Theory and practice should match: many believers pray as if God were near while their theory says he is distant and settled.

The outside-of-time case

Romans 8:28 and Genesis 50:20: God works all things, including suffering, for good, and this has comforted sufferers across centuries. Job 42:2-6: Job is not given an explanation but a vision of God’s rule. The comfort is that God is sovereign over pain. Lamentations 3:37-38 and Isaiah 45:7 are the hard texts. Isaiah 55:8-9: God’s ways are not our ways. 2 Corinthians 12:7-9 and Hebrews 12:5-11. The tradition holds that a God who could stop evil and chooses not to, for reasons he has not told us, is more consoling than a God who could not. Philippians 4:6-7.

Where each side is weaker than it looks

  • Usefulness to a hurting person is not the same as truth, for either side.
  • The open view has its own cost. If God did not know this would happen, he cannot have a plan for it, and consolation shrinks to “God will make the best of it.” Can a person truly trust a God who may not have seen the cancer coming?
  • The classical side: how do you say “God loves you” to the man in constant pain while teaching that God controls the nerves? Can a person truly pray for a child’s healing if God already has the outcome fixed?
  • Both sentences, “God is bending this toward good” and “God is with you in this,” can be said to the same suffering person.

Part 7

Pictures each side uses

Both sides reach for pictures. Each picture helps and each one breaks somewhere. Here is each, with its limit.

Father or Judge: two rooms. Inside time: a judge sits behind a bench and rules from a file. A father sits at a table and listens. Scripture calls God Father far more than Judge, and prayer is addressed to “Our Father.” Where it breaks: a good father also decides, disciplines and keeps promises, and Scripture calls the LORD “the Judge of all the earth” (Genesis 18:25).

Same heart, different approach. Outside time: a father speaks differently to a toddler, a teenager and an adult son, and he is the same man. Sinai, the prophets and the cross are changes in approach, not in character. Where it breaks: it explains different commands, but it does not by itself explain “the LORD relented.”

Covenant stages: servant to bride. Inside time: the relationship moves from servants, to sons, to a bride, and each stage opens more closeness. Where it breaks: the God of the first stage is the same God as the last, and the picture can sound as if he were learning how to relate.

One cube, two layers. Imagine a clear cube with an outer layer where time runs and an inner layer where it does not. God, the picture says, is present in both. Where it breaks: it assumes what it needs to show, that a being can live in both layers without being divided.

A line through God. Inside time: picture time as a line and God as someone who walks along it with us, seeing each point as it comes. Outside time: picture the line drawn on a page and God as the one holding the page, seeing the whole line at once. Where it breaks: the page is also a kind of space, and “seeing at once” is itself a word borrowed from time.

The ruler. Outside time: a ruler measures length but is not itself the length of anything. Time measures change and does not contain the one who does not change. Where it breaks: a ruler is a human tool. Whether time is a mere measure or a real thing is the whole dispute.

Author and story. Outside time: an author writes one book. Every chapter exists on his desk at once. He can write himself into the story as a character without being inside the book’s clock. Where it breaks: the characters of a story do not pray, and an author is not the Father of his characters in any real sense. Inside time answers that if the author steps into the story, he is now inside it.

Temporal, not temporary. Inside time: living in time does not make God short-lived. He is the Eternal One who has no beginning and no end, and who also has a “next.” Where it breaks: “next” must be given a meaning that does not bring in aging or loss.

The middle position. Both sides: God is timeless without creation and temporal since creation. He chose to enter time when he made it. Where it breaks: it needs a “before creation” in which God was timeless, and “before” is a time word. It also leaves the classical side asking whether the timeless God changed in order to become temporal.

Four models with one woman. A woman may leave a town. Settled decree: God has planned that she will leave and plans her reasons. Simple foreknowledge: God sees that she will leave, and his seeing does not cause it. Middle knowledge: God knew what she would freely choose in any setting, and he placed her in this one. Open: God knows her heart and her likely choice, and the choice is hers to make. Where it breaks: each model describes the woman from the outside, and none of them settles whether her choice could have gone otherwise.

Oath and announcement. Outside time: when God says “I will,” that is an oath and he keeps it. Hebrews 6:17-18 speaks of “the unchangeable character of his purpose.” When he says “you shall die” to a king, that is an announcement, and announcements can be answered. Where it breaks: the Hebrew of Jeremiah 18:7-10 makes conditions part of every announcement, and then the line between oath and announcement depends on interpretation, case by case.

Forcing and confirming. Pharaoh’s case: God can set the stage for a heart that is already set without reversing a free choice. A weather forecaster who says it will rain did not make the clouds. Where it breaks: Exodus 4:21 comes before any refusal.

Hear the heart, not the words. Inside time: the prophets’ words sound like “I will destroy” and mean “I will if you do not turn.” Listen for the heart. Where it breaks: if every harsh word is only a warning, then it is hard to say what a settled word of judgment would sound like.

Pastures, not a single line. Inside time: the future is a field with many paths, and God knows each one fully. He is never lost in it. Outside time: the future is a single road that God has already walked. Where it breaks: for the inside-time side, “God knows every path” sits close to the settled view if he already knows which one will be taken.

Seed, not rescue boat. Inside time: the kingdom grows like a seed (Matthew 13:31-32), not like a boat taking people off a sinking ship. Outside time: the boat picture fits Noah, and the seed fits the harvest. Both are in the text. Where it breaks: each picture forgets the other.

Three tests of any theology. Does it make you want to be with God, to be like him, and to work with him? Where it breaks: a view can pass all three and be wrong, and a view can fail all three and be true.

In charge, not in control. Discussed in Part 6.

The soup. Outside time: a cook who lets the soup boil over is not in control of the kitchen. A cook who plans the meal in advance is. Inside time answers that a cook who plans every ingredient is also the author of what goes wrong. Where it breaks: nothing in a kitchen is free to refuse the cook.

The steak. Inside time: a loving father who orders for his child at a restaurant is not wronging the child. A father who orders for a grown daughter and says “this is what you wanted all along” is. Where it breaks: a human father does not know his daughter’s future, and God is not a human father.

The sentence. Outside time: a sentence is written all at once by the author, but a reader reads it word by word. The reader’s experience of waiting is real, and the sentence is complete. Where it breaks: the reader does not choose the next word.

The 8:45 plan. Inside time: a man plans to leave at 8:45. A plan is not a prediction. A plan can change when the kids are late. Outside time: a plan that never fails is better than a plan that can. Where it breaks: only a being who does not know the future needs a plan that can change.

The cruel guide. Outside time answers: a mountain guide who foresees an accident and lets it happen is cruel. Inside time uses this against foreknowledge. The reply: a guide who sees a fall coming and has chosen to be the one who catches it is not cruel. Where it breaks: the guide in the story did not choose the mountain.

The nap. Inside time: a father napping while his son plays is not controlling the son. A father who is awake, near, and willing to step in is not either. Where it breaks: the picture says nothing about what he does with what he knows.

The lens. A lens does not change the light, it only focuses it. The question is whether Scripture’s human-side language (relents, grieves) is a lens on a changeless God, or the thing itself. Where it breaks: a lens cannot tell you which one it is.

Cancer and wrath. Inside time: a doctor’s anger at a tumor is not anger at the patient. God’s wrath at sin is aimed at what destroys his children. Where it breaks: the flood destroyed the people, not only the sin.

Wheat and tares. Matthew 13:24-30: let both grow together until the harvest. Inside time: God lets the field grow without pulling weeds. Outside time: the harvest date is set. Where it breaks: the picture says “let them grow” and does not say whether the owner already knows the harvest.

The rock question. Can God make a rock too heavy for him to lift? Both sides use it. The answer both can accept: the question asks for something that contradicts itself, and God’s power is not a power to do nonsense. Where it breaks: whether “a free choice that God cannot override” is nonsense or a coherent thing is exactly the question.

Grieving what you see coming. Outside time: a parent whose child is diagnosed with a terminal illness grieves long before the loss, knowing what will happen. Foreknowledge does not remove feeling. Inside time: the parent grieves because she does not know how long, and hopes. Where it breaks: a human cannot see all of time.

A man in ten years of pain. Both sides: a theology is tested at a bedside. See Part 6.

The pen is not God. Outside time: a pen writes only what the writer decides, and the writer is not the pen. Where it breaks: the pen has no will, and humans do.

Manifest versus omnipresent. Outside time: God is present everywhere, but he shows his presence in some places more than others (a burning bush, a temple, a face). Inside time: this explains how God can be “far” from the wicked (Proverbs 15:29) without leaving anywhere. Where it breaks: it does not by itself tell us whether “far” involves any change in God.

Part 8

Where each idea came from

Ideas have histories, and a history does not decide whether an idea is true. Knowing where an idea came from helps each side avoid two mistakes: calling its own view “just what the Bible says,” and calling the other side’s view “just Greek philosophy.”

Plato taught that the highest realities are changeless, eternal forms, and that the world of change is lesser. Aristotle described God as an unmoved mover, a first cause that acts on everything and is not moved by anything. Philo, a Jewish thinker living in Alexandria around the time of Jesus, read Moses through Plato and described God as unchanging and beyond time. The Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible rendered the name in Exodus 3:14 as “the One who is,” which pushed the name toward being and away from “I will be.”

Later Christian writers inherited that vocabulary. Augustine taught that God made time along with the world, and he asked, “What then is time?” and admitted that he knew until someone asked him. Boethius defined eternity as “the whole, simultaneous, and perfect possession of unending life.” That definition shaped the classical view for centuries.

Three points keep the history honest. First, the genetic fallacy: an idea does not become false because it came from a pagan source, and it does not become true because it came from a respected one. Second, the same classical writers who borrowed Greek vocabulary also fought Greek systems that taught that matter was evil and that God could not touch the world. Their word “impassible” was a guardrail against a God who suffers as a pagan god does, not a claim that God is cold. Third, the foreknowledge argument is older than any Greek influence. Isaiah 40-48 already challenges the idols to declare the end from the beginning, and Hebrew prophets already speak of God as the first and the last. The inside-time side, for its part, has to admit that Scripture’s language of relenting and regretting is not a modern invention, and it has to say why the earliest readers kept those verses alongside the verses on unchanging character.

Part 9

The hardest questions for each side

For the inside-time side

  • If God lives in time and does not know the future exactly, how can he promise that his purposes will succeed (Isaiah 46:10; Romans 8:29-30)?
  • How do you read the prophecies named by name and by century (Isaiah 44:28-45:1 on Cyrus; 1 Kings 13:2 on Josiah) as anything but exact foreknowledge?
  • How do you read Psalm 139:16 and Revelation 13:8 without a planned future?
  • If God can be surprised, what does “I thought she would return” (Jeremiah 3:7) say about his knowledge, and how is it different from ignorance?
  • If prayer is a real cause, how can a person trust any promise that depends on God’s later choices?
  • How can you hold that God does not control events and still read Exodus 4:21, Proverbs 21:1 and Romans 9:18 as they are written?
  • If God does not override free will, where does Scripture say so, and what do you do with Acts 4:27-28?
  • If the future is open, what guarantees that evil will not win?
  • How do you answer Malachi 3:6, Numbers 23:19 and James 1:17 on a God who does not change?
  • If God took on a human clock at creation, did God change, and if so, what did he lose or gain?
  • When a prayer for healing goes unanswered, who said no?
  • If foreknowledge makes a God cruel, does a God who could not see a tragedy coming help the sufferer more?

For the outside-of-time side

  • If God sees all history at once, what could “relented” in Exodus 32:14 mean without being a figure of speech that empties the verse?
  • If every prayer is foreseen and fixed, in what sense does a prayer change anything?
  • If God ordained every choice, how can a person be blamed for what God made him will?
  • If God is timeless, how can he act in a particular moment, speak, or answer?
  • How does a timeless God “become” flesh, grow in wisdom (Luke 2:52) and learn obedience (Hebrews 5:8)?
  • If Jeremiah 18:7-10 states the rule of how God deals with nations, is any announcement of judgment really settled?
  • Why does the text say “it did not enter my mind” (Jeremiah 19:5) if God knew all?
  • Why does the narrator of Exodus spend five plagues saying Pharaoh hardened himself?
  • If love is complete within God alone, what does the story of creation add, and why does Scripture say he rejoices over us (Zephaniah 3:17)?
  • If God decreed that most people be lost, how does that fit “desires all people to be saved” (1 Timothy 2:4)?
  • If God ordains evil acts for good ends, in what sense is he not the author of sin?
  • If the future is fixed, how do you hear the plain meaning of “if my people will humble themselves” (2 Chronicles 7:14)?

Before the Mountains sets the two answers side by side and lets the texts argue. It does not pick a winner. A companion to A Father Inside Time and its Cross-Examination.
