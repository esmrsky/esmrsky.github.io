From Moses to the present

The centuries

The four questions that run through every section.

  • Is God inside time (temporal) or outside it (timeless)? A third option sits between: God is in time but has no beginning or end (“everlasting”).
  • Does God change? In his nature? In his knowledge? In his feelings?
  • Does God know what free creatures will do? If so, how, and does that knowledge make the act necessary?
  • Does prayer change anything, or does it only change us?

Three words to keep straight.

  • Timeless (or “eternal” in the old sense): no before and after in God’s own life. God does not wait, remember, or anticipate.
  • Everlasting (or “omnitemporal”): God has always existed and always will, moment after moment, with no start or end.
  • Immutable and impassible: God does not change, and God is not acted on or made to suffer by anything outside himself. Each word has a strong and a weak version. Many fights are really about which version is meant.

How the church has answered

1

The Hebrew Bible’s own picture of time

Who and when. The Hebrew Scriptures, written over roughly a thousand years, with most scholars dating the final forms of the major books between the 8th and 2nd centuries before Christ.

Read the whole section

The key word. The Hebrew word olam is the main word for long duration. It can mean “a very long time,” “ancient time,” “the distant future,” or “forever,” depending on the sentence. It is not an abstract word for timelessness. Hebrew has no ready word for “time” as an empty container. It has words for seasons, appointed times (moed), days, and generations.

The core claim in plain words. In the Hebrew Bible, God is the one who was there before the mountains and will be there after them. God is not described as outside time. God is described as having no beginning, no end, and no rivals in duration. God acts in history, speaks at particular moments, remembers, changes plans, and keeps promises across generations.

Key texts.

  • Psalm 90:2: “from everlasting to everlasting you are God” (Hebrew: me-olam ad-olam). The same psalm says a thousand years in God’s sight are “like yesterday when it is past” (v. 4).
  • Psalm 102:25–27: the heavens wear out like clothing, “but you are the same, and your years have no end.”
  • Isaiah 40:28: “the everlasting God” (elohe olam). Isaiah 57:15: “the high and lofty one who inhabits eternity.”
  • Isaiah 41:4 and 44:6: “I, the Lord, the first, and with the last.” Isaiah 46:9–10: God declares “the end from the beginning.”
  • Exodus 3:14, “I am who I am” (or “I will be what I will be”). The Hebrew verb can be read as present or future. Scholars are split on whether this is a claim about being or a promise of presence.
  • Genesis 1:1: “In the beginning.” Time-language is in the creation story from the first line.

How scholars read it. Three broad readings exist.

  • The “Hebrew vs. Greek” reading. In the mid-20th century, writers such as Oscar Cullmann argued that Hebrew thought saw time as a straight line of God’s saving acts, and Greek thought saw eternity as timeless. On that view the Bible’s God is plainly in time. This reading was popular, then heavily criticized. James Barr, in Biblical Words for Time (1962), argued that you cannot read a philosophy of time off Hebrew and Greek word studies. Word meaning does not carry a worldview.
  • The “both/and” reading. Many scholars say the Bible is not trying to settle the philosophical question. It speaks of God in time-words because it is telling a story. It also says God is unlike creatures in duration and in power over history. Both timeless and temporal readings can claim these texts.
  • The “anthropomorphic” reading. Classical thinkers took the vivid texts (God “repents,” “comes down,” “remembers”) as accommodation: God speaking in a human way so humans can follow. Open and process thinkers took the same texts at face value.

Texts that lean toward change.

  • Genesis 6:6: “the Lord regretted that he had made man.”
  • Exodus 32:9–14: God threatens to destroy Israel; Moses pleads; “the Lord relented.”
  • Jonah 3:10: God “relented of the disaster.”
  • Jeremiah 18:7–10: if a nation repents, God will change what he said he would do.
  • 2 Kings 20:1–6 (Isaiah 38): Isaiah tells Hezekiah he will die. Hezekiah prays. God adds fifteen years.
  • Genesis 22:12: “now I know that you fear God.” Medieval Jewish thinkers debated this line heavily.

Texts that lean toward no change.

  • Numbers 23:19: God “is not a man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should change his mind.”
  • 1 Samuel 15:29: God “will not lie or have regret, for he is not a man, that he should have regret.” Note that the same chapter says twice that God did regret making Saul king (15:11, 15:35).
  • Malachi 3:6: “I the Lord do not change.”
  • Psalm 33:11: “The counsel of the Lord stands forever.”
  • James 1:17: with God “there is no variation or shadow due to change.”

Best argument for the Bible’s own picture. Whatever the philosophy, the Bible’s God has a story. He promises, waits, warns, and delivers. A reader meets a God who is faithful across time, not a God who is exempt from it.

Main objection. The same Bible says God declares “the end from the beginning” (Isa. 46:10), knows our words before we speak them (Ps. 139:4), and chose people “before the foundation of the world” (Eph. 1:4). Those lines push toward a God whose knowledge and purpose are not tied to a moment.

The “relenting” puzzle in one sentence. Both sides agree the Bible says God relents and says God does not relent. The history below is mostly the history of how to hold both.

For a person praying. The Bible’s own picture invites bold prayer. Moses argues with God, Hezekiah weeps, and the texts count it as real. It also gives a floor under that prayer: God’s character does not wobble.

Greek philosophy’s input

Who and when. Parmenides (early 5th century BC), Plato (427–347 BC), Aristotle (384–322 BC), Plotinus (c. 204–270 AD), and, bridging into Judaism, Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BC–50 AD).

Read the whole section

Parmenides. He argued that what truly is has no coming-to-be and no passing away. It is whole, one, and unchanging. Change and time, to him, belong to appearance. This is the seed of the idea that the highest reality is changeless and timeless. One of his surviving fragments says that real being never was and never will be, because it simply is, all together, now.

Plato. In the Timaeus, the maker (the Demiurge) forms the world to resemble an eternal pattern. Plato says time came into being with the heavens, and that time is a moving image of eternity (Timaeus 37d). Eternity here is the way the changeless Forms exist. Only “is” fits them, not “was” or “will be.” Plato also says in the Republic that god is perfect and so cannot change, since any change would be a change for the worse (or a change from what is best to something less).

Aristotle. In Physics VIII and Metaphysics XII, Aristotle argues that motion needs a first mover that is itself unmoved. This “unmoved mover” is pure actuality, with no potential, thus no change. Its activity is thinking, and what it thinks is itself (noesis noeseos). In Aristotle, god does not know or care about the world’s details, and does not answer prayers. He also wrote the famous sea-battle passage in On Interpretation 9: statements about future contingents, like “there will be a sea battle tomorrow,” seem neither true nor false yet. This puzzle sits behind every later debate on foreknowledge.

Plotinus. The Neoplatonist set out “the One,” beyond being, from which comes Intellect, then Soul, then the world. For Plotinus, eternity is the life of Intellect: “all at once,” complete, without extension. Time is the life of Soul as it spreads out step by step. His Enneads III.7 (“On Eternity and Time”) is the most developed ancient treatment. Boethius and Augustine drew on it, directly or by echo.

Philo of Alexandria. A Jewish thinker in Roman Egypt, Philo read Moses through Plato. He held that God is unchanging, needs nothing, and is without passions. He read the Hebrew Bible’s human-sounding language about God as concession to weak minds. He wrote a treatise, “On the Unchangeableness of God” (Quod Deus sit immutabilis), that reads Genesis 6:6 (“God regretted”) as a figure of speech. He also taught that time began with the world, and that God’s “now” has no past or future. Philo is the hinge: the first major thinker to fuse the Hebrew God of the Bible with Greek ideas of divine perfection. The church fathers inherited his method.

The scripture they lean on (the later Christian users of this approach). Exodus 3:14 in the Greek Septuagint reads “I am the One Who Is” (ho on). Later readers heard metaphysics in it: God is being itself. Malachi 3:6 and James 1:17 were read the same way.

Best argument. “Perfect” can’t be improved on. Any change is either toward something better (so God lacked it before) or toward something worse (so God was not perfect). Therefore the perfect is changeless. If changeless, then not in time, since time is the measure of change.

Main objection. The argument makes a person hard to find. A being who never responds, never knows what is happening now, and cannot be affected by joy or grief looks more like a principle than a father. Critics say this is Greek philosophy dressed as theology. Defenders answer that the Bible itself pushes this direction and that the fathers purified the Greek ideas as much as they borrowed them (for example, they said God freely created, which Plato’s Demiurge did not).

For a person praying. In this tradition, prayer is not information sent to someone who has to be told. It is the soul lifted toward the changeless good.

The early church: Irenaeus, Origen, the Cappadocians, and impassibility

Who and when. Irenaeus (c. 130–202), Origen (c. 185–253), and the Cappadocians: Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379), Gregory of Nyssa (c. 335–395), Gregory of Nazianzus (329–390). Also Ignatius, Justin, Tertullian, and Athanasius around the edges.

Read the whole section

Core claim. The fathers described God with a set of “negative” words borrowed from philosophy: unbegotten, uncreated, without beginning (anarchos), unchangeable (atreptos), without passions (apathes). But they set these beside the story of the Gospel, where God became flesh, was born, wept, and died. That tension shaped everything.

Irenaeus. He wrote against the Gnostics, who split a remote high God from a lesser creator. Irenaeus said the one God made the world directly, “by his Word and Spirit,” and stays in relation to it. He taught that God is beyond human measure yet has made himself known step by step through history. His picture of salvation has a plan unfolding in time: humanity grows toward maturity (“recapitulation” in Christ). So Irenaeus fits neatly with an emphasis on history, while also saying God is without need, without parts, and the source of all. His God is not a being of passions like the pagan gods. Irenaeus does not set out a theory of divine time.

Origen. Origen was both bold and slippery. He taught that God is immutable and “beyond all substance.” He is also often quoted, from his Homilies on Ezekiel, as saying that God in the incarnation suffers a passion of love. He took Philo’s line on anthropomorphic texts: Scripture says God “repents” in a way fitted to the hearer’s capacity. On prayer, his treatise On Prayer argued that God foresees everything and that foreknowledge does not cause events. He compared it to a teacher who knows a student will fail without making the student fail. He also said prayer is not useless since God foresaw the prayer too, and set the answer accordingly. This line, “God foresaw your prayer,” recurs for centuries.

The Cappadocians. They helped fix the Trinity and answered Eunomius, who said God’s essence could be fully known by the word “unbegotten.” Gregory of Nyssa answered that God’s essence is infinite and can’t be bounded by any concept. For Gregory, the creature’s life is a stretched-out, step-by-step existence (diastema, “extension”), and God’s life is without such stretching. Yet his teaching on the human journey toward God is one of endless progress (epektasis, from Philippians 3:13, “straining forward”): the soul never finishes growing into the infinite God. Basil used the pattern of Moses on the mountain. Gregory of Nazianzus made a famous point about the incarnation: what the Son did not take on, he could not heal. It pointed to God entering human experience.

Impassibility, and its nuance. Impassible meant at least these things to the fathers:

  • God is not overpowered or changed against his will.
  • God does not have irrational emotions such as rage or lust, like the pagan gods.
  • God is not made worse or less by creatures.

It did not automatically mean “God feels nothing.” Many fathers spoke of God’s love, mercy, and compassion. Cyril of Alexandria (d. 444) taught, in the fight with Nestorius, that the Word suffered in his flesh while remaining beyond suffering in his divine nature. The paradox is often summed up as suffering impassibly. It was a way of saying that the one who suffers on the cross is truly God the Son, yet God’s divine nature remains beyond corruption. Some historians, such as Joseph Hallman (The Descent of God, 1991), have argued that the early church was more mixed on impassibility than later textbooks suggest. Others (Paul Gavrilyuk, The Suffering of the Impassible God, 2004) argued that the fathers held a “qualified” impassibility: God is not subject to involuntary suffering, but may freely enter suffering in love in the incarnation. Gavrilyuk is the standard modern defense of this reading.

Scripture they lean on. John 1:14 (the Word became flesh); Hebrews 13:8 (“Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever”); Philippians 2:5–8 (he emptied himself); Malachi 3:6; James 1:17; Acts 17:25 (God is not served by human hands “as though he needed anything”).

Best argument. If God could be forced to suffer or be changed by creatures, he would not be the creator but one more thing in the world. The fathers held that God’s love is real and free, not forced by the creature.

Main objection. If God is truly unmoved, how does he love? A love that costs nothing and can’t be affected looks thin. The cross, critics say, shows a God who is moved. Later theologians such as Moltmann pressed this.

For a person praying. The fathers hold that prayer is real because God has freely tied his gifts to our asking. Origen’s way of putting it: God’s answer was arranged with our prayer already in view.

Augustine

Who and when. Augustine of Hippo (354–430). Key texts: Confessions XI (written around 400), City of God XI–XII (written in the 410s and 420s), On Free Choice of the Will (De libero arbitrio, Book III, written in the late 300s), and later anti-Pelagian works.

Read the whole section

Core claim in plain words. God made time along with the world. There was no “before” the world in which God was waiting. God lives in an unchanging “today.” Yet God knows our free acts ahead of time, and that knowledge doesn’t force them.

Confessions XI. Augustine opens by asking what God was doing before heaven and earth. He first mentions the joke answer (“preparing hell for those who pry”) and rejects it as an evasion. His real answer: “before God made heaven and earth, He made not anything” (XI.12, in the Pusey translation), and “there was no then when time was not” (XI.13). Time is itself a creature. Of God’s eternity he says it stands above all past times “in the excellency of an ever-present eternity” (XI.13). God’s years, he says elsewhere in the chapter, are “one today,” neither coming nor going.

Then comes the famous puzzle about what time is. Augustine’s well-known remark is that if no one asks him, he knows what time is, but if he tries to explain it, he does not (XI.14). He argues that the past no longer exists, the future does not yet exist, and the present has no length. He concludes that time is in the mind: a “stretching” (distentio animi) of memory, attention, and expectation. This inner view of time shapes a lot of later thought, since it makes time something creatures live through, which a timeless God does not.

City of God XI–XII. Augustine asks again about creation. Against those who thought the world was eternal, or that God had cycles, he says the world was made with time, not in time. God’s will is unchanging, even though it brings about changing things. There is no new decision in God when something new happens. The change is in the creature.

Foreknowledge and free will. In On Free Choice of the Will III, Augustine’s interlocutor Evodius presses: if God foreknows I will sin, I must sin, so how is it my fault? Augustine answers in steps.

  • God’s foreknowledge of my act doesn’t cause it, any more than your memory of yesterday causes yesterday.
  • God foreknows my will as my will. If I act freely, God foreknows that I act freely. So the foreknowledge includes the freedom.
  • The necessity that comes from foreknowledge is not a force on me. I sin willingly, not under compulsion.

In City of God V.9–10, he attacks Cicero, who denied foreknowledge to protect freedom. Augustine says that denying God’s foreknowledge in order to keep free will is a foolish way to try to be free. He holds both. The order of causes is certain to God, and our wills are themselves among the causes.

Scripture. Psalm 90:4 (a thousand years as yesterday); 2 Peter 3:8 (one day as a thousand years); Psalm 102:27 (your years have no end); Hebrews 13:8; Exodus 3:14; Romans 8:29 (“those whom he foreknew he also predestined”); Jeremiah 1:5.

Best argument. If God made time, then God can’t be limited by time. The question “what came before?” misfires, like asking what is north of the North Pole. And knowing is not causing.

Main objections.

  • Is it coherent? Critics say that “an eternal Today” is a metaphor hiding a puzzle. How can a life have “length” without succession?
  • Does foreknowledge still threaten freedom? Later critics (such as Nelson Pike) said the argument shows only that foreknowledge doesn’t cause acts. It leaves open whether the act can be otherwise if God has already believed it will happen.
  • Later Augustine and the will. In his fight with Pelagius, Augustine taught that the fallen will needs grace, and that God predestines the elect. This pushed the Western church toward strong views on sovereignty, which Calvin later drew on.

For a person praying. For Augustine, prayer does not inform God or change his mind. It stretches our desire so we can receive what God already intends to give (see his Letter to Proba, Letter 130).

Boethius

Who and when. Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius (c. 480–524/525), a Roman statesman, executed under the Gothic king Theodoric. He wrote the Consolation of Philosophy in prison. Book V takes up the question of free will and God’s foreknowledge. The Consolation became one of the most read books of the Middle Ages.

Read the whole section

Core claim. God is not “before” our future. God lives an eternal now. His knowledge of what we will do is more like seeing than like predicting. He sees our free acts as they happen.

The famous definition. In Book V, prose 6, Boethius defines eternity as the whole, simultaneous, and perfect possession of unending life. The Latin is interminabilis vitae tota simul et perfecta possessio. Notice the words:

  • interminabilis, without an end (and, he adds, a beginning).
  • tota simul, the whole at once.
  • perfecta possessio, a full, complete having, not a piece at a time.

Boethius says eternity differs from the everlasting universe. Plato’s world, if it never ended, would still live one moment after another. God has no such lack. God does not move from past to future. He has “the present” of everything in one comprehensive grasp.

The two kinds of necessity. Boethius distinguishes simple necessity (the sun rises; all humans are mortal) from conditional necessity (if you see someone walking, they are walking). Seeing a free act doesn’t make the act necessary in the first, strong way. It’s only “necessary” in the conditional sense: while it’s happening, it’s happening. God sees it all in the present, so the necessity is the weak, conditional kind.

The images. The hilltop and observer picture is Boethius’s own in spirit. God sees from above: he is like a person on a height who can see the whole road with every traveler on it. Aquinas takes up the road-and-height picture explicitly (ST I q.14 a.13). A picture of a circle is also used, in which the center relates equally to all points on the circumference.

Scripture. Psalm 139 (God sees all my days); Isaiah 46:10; 2 Peter 3:8; Psalm 90:4. Boethius, as a philosopher, leans less on proof texts than later theologians.

Best argument. If God knows by seeing, then knowing a free act is like watching someone do it. Watching doesn’t force them. The “fore-“ in foreknowledge is only our way of talking. For God, there’s no “fore.”

Main objections.

  • Does the dilemma just move? Critics say that, even if God is outside time, God’s knowing is “from eternity.” So there’s still a fact about what God knows that does not depend on us. Philosophers sometimes call this the worry about the necessity of the timeless realm.
  • Prophecy. Timeless knowledge can’t be told to a prophet before the event. But the Bible has prophets who tell the future. The temporal revelation seems to bring the problem back (this is the “prophecy problem,” pressed by philosophers such as David Widerker and Edward Wierenga).
  • The “now” problem. A timeless God seems unable to know what time it is now. Defenders reply in several ways.

For a person praying. If God sees my future prayer and its answer together, then my prayer is part of the picture he sees. In Boethius’s view, nothing I ask surprises him, yet it is really mine. Boethius ends Book V with a call to pray and to live uprightly, since we live before a judge who sees all things.

Anselm and Thomas Aquinas

Who and when. Archbishop of Canterbury. Key texts: Monologion (1076), Proslogion (1077–78), De Concordia (c. 1107–08, on foreknowledge, predestination, and free choice).

Read the whole section

Anselm (c. 1033–1109)

Core claim. God is that than which nothing greater can be conceived. A greater being would not be limited by parts or by time. So God exists wholly, everywhere and always, as a single present. Anselm argues that if God existed only part by part at each moment, his essence would be divided along the lines of time. So God’s eternity is identical with God’s being.

Argument. In Proslogion 19–22, Anselm asks how God can be everywhere and always without being spread out. His answer is that God is not “in” place or time as a container. Place and time are contained in him. In Monologion 21–24, he says God’s life is “supreme being,” with no before and after.

Anselm on compassion. In Proslogion 8, Anselm asks the famous question: how can God be both compassionate and impassible? His answer: God is compassionate in terms of our experience, since we receive the effect. But God does not feel the passion. In effect, Anselm tells God that he is compassionate from our side and not from his own. This is a famous and often-criticized line. It is a clear case of the strong form of impassibility.

Anselm on foreknowledge. In De Concordia Book I, Anselm separates two kinds of necessity: an antecedent necessity that causes, and a subsequent necessity that only follows. God’s foreknowledge, he says, is of the second kind: it follows from the fact that the act will happen. It doesn’t cause it. He also suggests that the “future” is not future to God, who sees all things in his eternal present.

Objection. Same as above. The remark that God is not compassionate from his own side sounds, to many readers, like a love with no one inside.

Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274)

Who and when. Dominican friar and teacher in Paris and Italy. Summa Theologiae (begun 1265–66, unfinished). The relevant parts of the Prima Pars (First Part) are q.9–10 (immutability and eternity), q.14 (God’s knowledge), q.19 (God’s will), q.13 (names of God), q.25 (power), and q.22–23 (providence and predestination).

Core claim. God is pure act (actus purus), with nothing in him still to be realized. So he cannot change (q.9). Because he cannot change, he is eternal (q.10). Eternity is “the simultaneously-whole and perfect possession of interminable life” (q.10 a.1, quoting Boethius). God is not merely in eternity; he is his own eternity (a.2).

What the text says. In q.10, Article 1 defends Boethius’s definition and says we grasp eternity by starting from time. Time measures motion’s before and after, and eternity is the opposite: no succession. Article 4 says the essential difference between eternity and time is that eternity is “simultaneously whole” and time is not. Eternity measures permanent being; time measures motion.

Divine simplicity. For Aquinas, God has no parts. God’s goodness, wisdom, power, and existence are one. So we cannot truly separate God’s knowledge from his will or his being.

Analogy. Aquinas holds that when we say God is “wise” or “loving,” we use the words neither in exactly our sense (univocally) nor in an entirely different sense (equivocally), but analogically (q.13 a.5). There is a real likeness, but God exceeds it. This lets him hold that Scripture’s language about God is true yet limited. For Aquinas, “God repents” is true by figure: it describes the effect (the world looks as if God changed his mind) and not a change in God (q.19 a.7 treats the question of whether God’s will can change). He cites Numbers 23:19 and 1 Samuel 15:29.

Future contingents (q.14 a.13). The objections hold that if God’s knowledge is necessary and causes what he knows, then everything is necessary. Aquinas replies that a contingent thing is known one way from its causes (as future and not yet settled) and another way as it actually is (as present). God knows contingents “as they are actually present to Him,” because his knowledge is measured by eternity, which is “simultaneously whole.” He gives the road-and-height image: a traveler on the road sees only those near him, but a person on a height sees “all travelling by the way” at once. The reply to objection 2 handles the conditional “if God knew it, it will be.” Aquinas says to read it as the thing seen in its presentness, and so it is necessary only in the weak sense.

God’s will (q.19). God wills his own goodness necessarily. He wills creatures freely (a.3). God’s will is the cause of things (a.4) and cannot fail (a.6). Yet he moves some secondary causes to act necessarily and others to act contingently (a.8). So free creatures act freely because God wills that they do (q.22 a.4, q.83 a.1 ad 3). He holds God’s causality and human freedom are on different levels: God is not another cause alongside our will but the cause of our will’s being a will.

Providence and prayer (II-II q.83 a.2). Aquinas asks why we pray if God’s plan is fixed. His answer: we do not pray to change the plan but to obtain what God has arranged to give through prayers. Prayer is one of the causes God uses. Because God’s plan includes secondary causes, our prayer is a real cause of the outcome. This is the classical answer.

Impassibility. God is not acted on. But Aquinas does not say God is cold. God’s love is the act of willing the good of another. In q.20 he says love in God does not involve passion. Mercy is a perfection of God “in its effect,” not as a feeling (q.21 a.3).

Scripture. Exodus 3:14; Malachi 3:6; James 1:17; Psalm 32:15 in the Vulgate numbering (Psalm 33:15 in modern numbering, “he understands all their works”); Hebrews 4:13; Psalm 102:27; John 8:58 (“before Abraham was, I am”).

Best argument. The “five ways” and the argument from act and potency: anything that changes is moved by something else. A chain of that sort needs a first, unmoved source. That source is not in time, since time measures change.

Main objections.

  • Coherence. Can one hold that God knows “what happens now” while he does not have a now? Can “simple” eternity be a living will that responds? (Stump and Kretzmann tried to answer.)
  • Is Aquinas’s God personal enough? The Reformation, process theology, and Moltmann say no.
  • Divine determinism. Critics say that by making God the cause of everything, including free acts, Aquinas leaves no true freedom. Defenders (Thomists) say that is a confusion of levels.

For a person praying. Aquinas: pray boldly, since your prayer is one of the real causes God has chosen to use. But don’t expect to alter God himself.

Late medieval: Scotus and Ockham on future contingents

Who and when. John Duns Scotus (c. 1266–1308) and William of Ockham (c. 1287–1347). Both Franciscans, both writing at Oxford and Paris.

Read the whole section

The problem they inherit. Aristotle’s sea-battle puzzle (On Interpretation 9) says future contingent statements may lack truth value. Aquinas answered with God’s eternal present. By 1300, other thinkers, such as Peter Auriol, tried a different route, saying God’s knowledge of future contingents is not “determinate” in the ordinary way. Scotus and Ockham each take the stronger line: God knows them, and they are true.

Scotus. Scotus is best known for defending real contingency in God’s own will and in the world. For him, God’s will is free in a deep sense, which includes being able to will otherwise at the very moment of choosing (“synchronic contingency”). This was new. Earlier thinkers had said freedom meant being able to choose otherwise later. Scotus said that a free choice is one which, at the very moment it is made, could have been otherwise. He applied this to God and to creatures. For God’s knowledge of future contingents, Scotus said God knows them by knowing his own will and the free wills of creatures in eternity. He rejected the view that God’s knowledge makes them necessary. He tied the claim to his idea that God’s essence is infinite and has no “before and after.”

Ockham. Ockham’s Treatise on Predestination and God’s Foreknowledge with Respect to Future Contingents (c. 1321–24) is the key work. He faced the standard worry: if God believed yesterday that I will sit tomorrow, then yesterday’s belief is now fixed and past, so I cannot now do otherwise. Ockham answered by splitting facts about the past in two.

  • Hard facts are strictly about the past: “Caesar crossed the Rubicon.”
  • Soft facts look like facts about the past but depend partly on the future: “God believed yesterday that I will sit tomorrow.”

Soft facts are not fixed in the dangerous way. If I had stood tomorrow, God would have believed otherwise yesterday. This is “Ockhamism.” Ockham also said it is not quite correct to say all “past” truths are necessary. He admitted that how God knows future contingents is a mystery. He did not claim a full account.

On this account, Ockham denied that the necessity of the past covers the whole past. The main criticism is that the Ockhamist distinction between hard and soft facts is hard to draw without ad hoc moves.

Scripture. Psalm 33:15; Psalm 139; Isaiah 46:10; Matthew 26:34 (Jesus foretells Peter’s denial). The standard cases for “God knows free acts.”

Best argument. The Ockhamist move is to say a fact about a past belief gets its truth from a future free act. It is part of an ordinary pattern. “Yesterday it was true that I would write this sentence today” is not a deep constraint on me.

Main objection. Critics say Ockhamism makes God’s past belief depend backward on my act, which looks like a kind of backward causation, or treats God as a being with no real “past.” Others say a true “soft fact” must have some ground. What grounds it?

Why it matters for later debate. Ockham’s solution works for a God in time, with exhaustive foreknowledge. This is the “everlasting God with simple foreknowledge” option on the menu below. It does not need timelessness.

For a person praying. If the future is already settled in God’s knowledge but I still make it, then my prayer is also part of what God knew. This is close to Origen’s view, now with logic behind it.

The Reformation: Luther, Erasmus, Calvin, Arminius, Dort

Who and when. Desiderius Erasmus published On Free Will (De libero arbitrio) in 1524. Martin Luther replied with On the Bondage of the Will (De servo arbitrio) in 1525.

Read the whole section

Luther versus Erasmus (1524–25)

Erasmus’s claim. Humans have some power of will to turn toward or away from grace. He didn’t want a hard debate. He thought many commands in Scripture (“choose this day,” “return to me”) would make no sense without some real power to respond. He urged a moderate, practical stance.

Luther’s claim. The will after the fall is bound. It is not coerced, but it is a slave to sin. Luther went further than Erasmus expected: he tied it to God’s foreknowledge. If God foreknows and wills, then, Luther argued, everything takes place by necessity. He said the Christian should accept this as comfort: if our salvation rested on our own will, we could never be sure. Luther distinguished the revealed God (preached in Christ) from the hidden God (whose will is beyond our reading) and warned people not to pry into the second.

Scripture. Romans 9 (Jacob and Esau; “it depends not on human will or exertion, but on God, who has mercy”); Ephesians 1:4–5; John 6:44; Exodus 4:21 (God hardens Pharaoh’s heart); Proverbs 21:1; Isaiah 45:7.

Best argument. Luther’s strongest point is the pastoral one: if God’s promise depends on my power to hold on, it isn’t a promise. Only a bound will under a sovereign God gives a sure comfort.

Main objection. Erasmus: the command-and-warning texts presuppose real ability. And if God wills all things by necessity, God becomes the author of sin. Luther’s answer: God’s ways are hidden and we are not to judge him.

For a person praying. Luther: pray as a beggar. The only reason we can pray at all is that the Spirit has been given.

John Calvin (1509–1564)

Who and when. Calvin’s Institutes of the Christian Religion, final Latin edition 1559. Key sections: Book I chapters 16–18 (providence), Book III chapters 21–24 (predestination), and Book III chapter 20 (prayer).

Core claim in plain words. God governs everything that happens. Not only does he know it; he wills it, in some way. He chooses some people for salvation before the world was made, not because of anything in them. Calvin attacked the idea that foreknowledge is mere seeing with no will behind it. In his view, God foresees what will happen because he ordained that it should.

Providence (I.16–18). I.16 says God is not an idle spectator but a ruler. Calvin rejects “fortune” and “chance.” I.17 shows how to apply providence for comfort in suffering. I.18 treats God’s use of evil acts to do good. God uses the wicked’s evil without being evil himself. He appeals to the case of Job, Joseph (Genesis 50:20, “you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good”), and Acts 2:23 (Jesus delivered up by the “definite plan and foreknowledge of God” and by “lawless men”).

”Repentance” of God. In I.17.12–13, Calvin takes up the Bible’s passages that say God repents. He says Scripture “accommodates” itself to us as a nurse speaks to a child. God “does not repent,” but the Bible speaks as though he does so we can grasp his dealings. Calvin rejects the idea that God changes his mind, citing Numbers 23:19 and 1 Samuel 15:29. This is classic accommodation.

Predestination (III.21–24). Election is unconditional. God’s decree is the cause. Calvin admits that this is a hard and frightening teaching. He holds that God both elects some and passes over others (reprobation). He grounds this in Romans 9–11, Ephesians 1, and John 6 and 17. He also warns people not to speculate but to look at Christ, in whom election is seen.

Prayer (III.20). Calvin asks why pray at all if God already knows. His reply: prayer is not to tell God something but to train us. It builds our faith, our dependence, and our habit of seeking God. He says God has chosen to give many things only in answer to prayer, as one of the means he uses. This is close to Aquinas.

Time and eternity. Calvin does not write a treatise on divine time. He mostly follows Augustine on God’s eternity. He says foreknowledge and predestination are both in God, and he is often read as saying that for God all things are present, with no past or future.

Best argument. If God is God, nothing happens outside his control. The Bible’s comfort for the suffering is that evil is in the hand of a sovereign and good God (Romans 8:28).

Main objections.

  • Author of evil. Does God cause sin? Calvin denies it but admits he cannot fully explain it.
  • Sincerity of the offer. How can the gospel be offered to all if God has chosen only some?
  • The relenting texts. Open theists and Arminians say “accommodation” explains away what the text says plainly.

For a person praying. Calvin: pray with confidence and humility. Confidence because God’s will is good. Humility because the prayer is a means in his plan, not a lever on his mind.

Jacob Arminius (1559–1609)

Who and when. Dutch theologian at Leiden. He died in 1609. His followers, the Remonstrants, submitted five articles in 1610 (“the Remonstrance”).

Core claim. God’s election is based on his foreknowledge of who will believe. God offers grace to all. Grace is necessary for faith, but it can be resisted. Arminius kept a very high view of God’s sovereignty and foreknowledge. He did not say foreknowledge is limited.

Foreknowledge. Arminius taught that God knows all things, including free acts, by a knowledge he sometimes called “free” and “natural.” He engaged with Molina’s idea of middle knowledge and was sympathetic to it. He also said God’s foreknowledge doesn’t cause.

Scripture. Romans 8:29 and 1 Peter 1:2 (“according to the foreknowledge of God the Father”); 1 Timothy 2:4 (God “desires all people to be saved”); 2 Peter 3:9; Ezekiel 18:23, 32; John 3:16; Hebrews 6 and 10 (warnings against falling away).

Best argument. Arminius’s best argument is moral: if God decrees the fall and then condemns sinners for it, he is the author of sin. Better to say God foreknows who will believe and elects them in Christ.

Main objection. Calvinists say conditional election makes salvation depend on human choice, which makes faith a “work.” Also, if God foreknows who will believe, then who believes is already settled, so the problem seems to return.

Synod of Dort (1618–1619)

What. An international church assembly in the Dutch Republic. It condemned the Remonstrant articles and issued the “Canons of Dort,” often summed up as five points: total depravity, unconditional election, limited atonement, irresistible grace, perseverance of the saints (the “TULIP” acronym is a later invention). The Remonstrants, after Dort, were barred from the Dutch Reformed church for a time.

Best argument at Dort. Salvation is all of grace. Faith itself is a gift.

Main objection. The Remonstrants said this made God’s offer insincere and his judgment unjust.

For a person praying. Dort and Remonstrants differ on whether my prayer for another’s salvation can change that person’s response. Both agree prayer is commanded.

Luis de Molina and middle knowledge

Who and when. Luis de Molina (1535–1600), a Spanish Jesuit. His Concordia (1588) tried to reconcile grace and free will in the face of Dominican (Bañezian) critics, who held that God predetermines every act by his “physical premotion.”

Read the whole section

Core claim in plain words. God has three kinds of knowledge.

  • Natural knowledge: all necessary truths and all possibilities. It comes before God’s decision to create.
  • Free knowledge: what will actually happen. It comes after God decides what to create.
  • Middle knowledge (scientia media): what any free creature would freely do in any situation. These are “counterfactuals of creaturely freedom.” Middle knowledge comes before God’s decision to create, but it isn’t up to God.

With middle knowledge, God can pick a world in which free creatures freely do what he wants, without causing them. This gives God complete control and gives humans real libertarian freedom at once.

Scripture.

  • 1 Samuel 23:6–13. David is at Keilah. He asks if Saul will come down, and if the men of Keilah will surrender him. God says yes to both. David leaves. Saul does not come. Molinists say God knew what would have happened if David stayed, and that did not happen. That is knowledge of a counterfactual, neither necessary nor future.
  • Matthew 11:21–23. Jesus says if the miracles done in Chorazin and Bethsaida had been done in Tyre and Sidon, they would have repented. Again: knowledge of what free people would do under conditions that never came about.
  • Others cited: 1 Corinthians 2:8; Ezekiel 3:6; Jeremiah 38:17–18; Matthew 12:7 (if you had known, you would not have condemned the guiltless).

Modern Molinists. Alvin Plantinga used a Molinist idea in his free-will defense of God against the problem of evil (The Nature of Necessity, 1974). Thomas Flint’s Divine Providence: The Molinist Account (1998) is the standard book-length defense. William Lane Craig, in The Only Wise God (1987) and later writings, defends it as the best account of providence and foreknowledge. Craig takes the further view that God is timeless without creation and temporal since.

Best argument. Molinism offers the strongest “have it both ways” claim: complete providence and genuine freedom. God can plan a world with certainty, down to details, and yet no one is forced. It also fits the “would have” texts.

Main objections. Philosophers raise these standard ones.

  • The grounding objection. What makes a counterfactual of freedom true? If the person is truly free (could do otherwise), there seems to be nothing to make “she would choose X” true before she exists. Robert Adams pressed this. The worry is that these counterfactuals would be explanatorily prior to our free acts, which conflicts with freedom.
  • Replaces one mystery with another. How God knows the future becomes how God knows counterfactuals.
  • Luck. God can’t choose which counterfactuals are true. So he may be stuck with worlds in which people freely choose badly. Critics say this limits his control.
  • Calvinist objection. Molinism makes God’s choices depend on creatures’ free decisions that he did not decide.

For a person praying. In Molinism, God knows my prayer and every possible prayer. He arranged the world so that prayers would be answered in just the way that fits his plan. The Molinist can say, “Your prayer matters. God planned the whole world with your freely chosen prayer in view.”

The Socinians

Who and when. Followers of Laelius and Faustus Socinus, active in Poland and Transylvania in the 16th–17th centuries. They issued the Racovian Catechism (1605). They denied the Trinity and held a rationalist reading of Scripture. Some Socinians, in the 17th century, held that God does not know future free acts.

Read the whole section

Core claim. Future free acts are not yet “there” to be known. If they were certain, they wouldn’t be free. God’s knowledge is perfect, but “knowing what is not yet determinate” is not something to know. God knows all that can be known. That excludes free future acts.

Who exactly. The leading Socinian figure on this is Faustus Socinus himself, and some of his followers are said to have held the same line. Histories of open theism list Socinus among its earlier precursors. Calcidius, the late-antique translator of the Timaeus, is also sometimes listed as a precursor.

Scripture. Genesis 22:12 (“now I know”); Exodus 4:1–9; Deuteronomy 8:2 (God tested Israel “to know what was in your heart”); Jeremiah 3:7 and 19:5 (God says “I did not command or decree, nor did it come into my mind”); 1 Samuel 15:11; Jonah 3.

Best argument. Socinus argued that if God foreknows all free acts, then God’s warnings, tests, and offers are for show. God’s own words in the Bible treat the future as open.

Main objections. Calvinists and Arminians alike said this reduces God and ignores the Bible’s prophecy. The church response to Socinians was strong, and the label “Socinian” became a charge to be avoided.

Why it matters. Socinus’s view is the oldest Christian ancestor of the view that today is called open theism. Today’s open theists differ from the Socinians in two key ways: they affirm the Trinity and the full deity of Christ, and they say God has chosen to leave the future open, rather than saying it is by nature unknowable.

For a person praying. In Socinian terms, prayers are real inputs. God does not know ahead of time what you will say. Your prayer is truly new to him.

Jonathan Edwards, John Wesley, and Adam Clarke

Who and when. New England pastor and philosopher. A Careful and Strict Enquiry into the Modern Prevailing Notions of that Freedom of the Will (1754), usually Freedom of the Will.

Read the whole section

Jonathan Edwards (1703–1758)

Core claim. The will always follows the strongest motive. A person is free when they do what they want. They are not free to want otherwise by sheer will. This is “compatibilism”: freedom and determinism go together. Edwards then used it against the Arminian view that real freedom needs a “self-determining power” of the will. If the will chooses without any cause, it is random, and if the will chooses by a prior choice, we regress without end.

Foreknowledge. Edwards argued, in Part II, that if God foreknows all choices infallibly, then they are certain. This certain connection, he said, does not offend true liberty. He claimed that the Arminian who accepts foreknowledge is stuck with necessity anyway, so Arminians should either deny foreknowledge (as Socinians did) or accept compatibilism. Edwards attacked the Socinian and Arminian views both.

Scripture. Isaiah 46:10; Acts 2:23; Acts 4:27–28; Proverbs 16:9; Romans 9.

Best argument. “Foreknowledge is infallible and free acts are not necessary” is unstable. Edwards pushed a dilemma: either deny God’s foreknowledge or deny libertarian freedom.

Main objection. Critics say compatibilism is “freedom” in name only. If a person’s choice is fixed by prior causes, back to God, then God is the author of sin. Edwards’ Concerning the End for Which God Created the World and Original Sin are tied to this; he denied God is the author of sin by a distinction between positive cause and permission.

For a person praying. Edwards: God’s decrees are fixed, but prayer is one of the means. God has decreed the prayer along with the answer.

John Wesley (1703–1791)

Who and when. Anglican priest, founder of Methodism. Relevant: the sermons “Free Grace” (1739) and “On Predestination” (1773); also “On Divine Providence.”

Core claim. God’s grace is free to all, not only a few. Predestination in the strong Calvinist sense makes preaching vain, destroys holiness, and slanders God’s character. Wesley held that God foreknows all things, including free acts, but that “foreknowledge is not causing.”

The Wesleyan solution on eternity. In “On Predestination,” Wesley takes up the old answer. He argues that God is outside our time, in an eternal present. He says in effect that God does not foresee, since foresee implies “before.” God “knows” and “sees” all things. This is Boethius. Wesley says that foreknowledge, being a way of speaking for us, doesn’t cause anything. Knowing a thing is not the cause of the thing known.

Scripture. Ezekiel 18:23; 1 Timothy 2:4; John 3:16; 2 Peter 3:9; Titus 2:11; Hebrews 2:9 (Christ tasted death for everyone); 1 John 2:2.

Best argument. Wesley’s best argument is moral and scriptural. A God who chooses some and passes over others, and still commands all to repent, is hard to square with the God who “so loved the world.”

Main objection. Calvinists said Wesley’s view makes man’s will the final cause of salvation. Open theists later said Wesley’s use of Boethius gave away too much: it still bound the future.

For a person praying. Wesley: prayer is a real means of grace. Go to God for yourself and for the lost, because he truly offers grace to all.

Adam Clarke (c. 1760–1832)

Who and when. A Methodist scholar of wide learning, best known for his Bible commentary, published in the early 1800s. Some later writers name him among the 19th-century forerunners of the open view. His commentary shows tension. On passages like Genesis 22:12 and Exodus 32, some readers find that he writes of God’s knowledge in ways that sound open. The safe thing to say is that later writers have cited him as a forerunner.

Nineteenth-century open-view forerunners

Who and when. In the 1800s, scattered writers argued, mostly inside Methodist, Holiness, and Free Will Baptist circles, that God does not foreknow all free acts. This is the same place later open theists draw on.

Read the whole section

Names often mentioned. Isaak August Dorner (German Lutheran, 1809–1884), Gustav Fechner (1801–1887), Otto Pfleiderer (1839–1908), Adam Clarke, and Lorenzo D. McCabe. Contributions grew as the century closed.

Lorenzo D. McCabe. A Methodist minister and professor in the 1800s. He wrote books in the late 1800s arguing that God’s not knowing the future free acts of his creatures is a necessity, or that such acts by nature can’t be known. His reasoning was largely Arminian: if God foreknows free acts, they cannot be free. Other Methodists criticized him, and older histories say he kept his post.

Billy Hibbard. Billy Hibbard was an early American Methodist preacher. He is sometimes cited in the open-theism literature as an early American advocate of the view that God doesn’t foreknow free acts. Little is firmly known about the details of his teaching.

Others sometimes mentioned in the literature: Samuel Fancourt (18th-century English Dissenter), and, in Britain, writers in the Unitarian and Methodist traditions. Thomas Jay Oord and others have traced further names.

Core claim. The future includes free choices that do not yet exist to be known. God’s omniscience is real and perfect. It means knowing all that can be known.

Scripture. The same texts as the Socinians, plus Jeremiah 32:35 (God did not command it, nor did it enter his mind), and the “if/perhaps” in Exodus 3:18–4:9 and Ezekiel 12:3 (“perhaps they will understand”).

Best argument. If foreknowledge fixes the future, the Arminian system’s central claim (real, resistible grace) falls. So the consistent Arminian drops exhaustive foreknowledge. McCabe pressed this.

Main objection. The 19th-century church was not ready to give up foreknowledge. Opponents said it was a rationalist move, plain Socinianism with a Methodist coat on.

For a person praying. The open-view forerunners emphasize that prayer can make a difference in what God does, because the future is not fixed.

Process theology

Who and when. Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947), Process and Reality (1929). Charles Hartshorne (1897–2000), The Divine Relativity (1948), Omnipotence and Other Theological Mistakes (1984). Later: John Cobb, David Ray Griffin, Marjorie Suchocki, Norman Pittenger.

Read the whole section

Core claim in plain words. Reality is made of events in the process of becoming, not of fixed things. God is not above the process but the supreme participant in it. God is affected by the world, takes in everything that happens, and responds with new offers to each creature. God does not control the world by force. God persuades.

What the standard accounts say.

  • Whitehead’s God has two natures. The primordial nature holds all possibilities. The consequent nature takes in the world’s achieved experience and keeps it.
  • God also gives each creature an “aim” suited to its situation.
  • Whitehead has a striking line on this: it is as true to say that God creates the world as that the world creates God.
  • Hartshorne separates God’s abstract character, which never changes, from God’s concrete states, which do. God is “surpassable” only by God’s own later states, and unsurpassable by anyone else. Hartshorne called this “dual transcendence.” He also developed panentheism: the world is in God, somewhat as a body is in a person, but God is more than the world.
  • Process theism affirms real relations in God: God is genuinely affected by creatures. Classical theism denies this.
  • God’s knowledge changes as the world changes.

How it differs from open theism. Open theists, mostly evangelicals, hold that God voluntarily limits himself to open up to the world. Process theists hold that the give-and-take is essential to God’s nature. It is not a choice. Other differences:

  • Open theists hold creation out of nothing; most process thinkers do not. For process thought, some kind of creaturely stuff always existed.
  • Open theists hold that God can act unilaterally (miracles, resurrection), and that he chooses restraint. Process thinkers say God cannot override freedom at all.
  • Open theists keep the traditional Christian story (incarnation, bodily resurrection, a coming kingdom). Process thinkers reinterpret much of it.

Scripture. Process theologians usually argue from the overall biblical witness rather than proof texts: Exodus 3 (God hears and comes down), Hosea 11:8 (“How can I give you up?”), the prophets’ language of divine pathos, and John 1 (the Word becoming flesh). Hartshorne and others also lean on the claim that “God is love” (1 John 4:8) is the central text and that love means being affected.

Best argument. Love requires being moved by the beloved. A God who cannot be affected cannot love in a way that means anything. And the problem of evil is lighter: God did not choose the suffering and cannot stop it by force. He suffers with the world.

Main objections.

  • Not enough power. If God cannot override anything, can he promise a final victory? Can there be a resurrection or a new creation?
  • Not the God of Scripture. Critics say the God who parts the sea, raises the dead, and promises a coming kingdom has been replaced.
  • Creation out of nothing. If God did not create from nothing, then something exists that God did not make.

For a person praying. In process thought, prayer is real and effective but not coercive. God takes it in and feeds it back as a new possibility for the people involved. It changes the world and God’s experience.

The twentieth century: Barth, Cullmann, Heschel, Moltmann, Kitamori

Who and when. Swiss Reformed theologian. Church Dogmatics, especially II/1 (1940) on the perfections of God, including “God’s eternity” (§31.3).

Read the whole section

Karl Barth (1886–1968)

Core claim. Barth rejected both pure timelessness and the idea that God is just a long stretch of time. He argued that eternity is not the absence of time. It is God’s own true time, the source of past, present, and future. God has “time for us” since he made time. God’s eternity is “pure duration,” in which beginning, middle, and end are not separate but fully together, yet are not collapsed into a point. In eternity, past, present, and future are distinct but not apart.

Scripture. Revelation 1:8 (“who is and who was and who is to come”); John 1:1; Galatians 4:4 (“when the fullness of time had come”); Hebrews 13:8; Psalm 90.

Best argument. In Christ, God truly enters time and takes it up. If God were only timeless, the incarnation would be a strange foreign act. Barth’s God is Lord of time and so is able to have time.

Main objection. Is “pure duration” a clear idea or a fancy way of splitting the difference? (This is the concern that Alan Padgett and others share.)

For a person praying. In Barth, prayer is a creature’s answer to the God who has time for us. God “hears” in a real sense because he has freely made himself present in Christ.

Oscar Cullmann (1902–1999)

Who and when. Lutheran New Testament scholar. Christ and Time (1946, English 1950).

Core claim. The New Testament sees time as a line with Christ at the center. History before Christ points toward him. History after Christ points toward the end. Salvation happens in time. “The” Greek view (the circle, or timelessness) is quite different. He wrote that primitive Christianity’s idea of eternity is an unending line of time, not timelessness.

Scripture. Mark 1:15 (“the time is fulfilled”); Galatians 4:4; Ephesians 1:10; Hebrews 1:1–2; Revelation 10:6 (“there should be no more delay”; some translations: “time no longer”).

Best argument. The central events of Christianity are historical, one-time events. A faith built on “once for all” (Hebrews 9:26–28) is bound up with a time line.

Main objection. James Barr (1962) and others said Cullmann read philosophy into word studies. The Greek word aion has no single meaning. Also, the claim that the NT excludes timelessness goes beyond the text.

For a person praying. Cullmann’s view stresses that prayer takes place between the “already” and the “not yet.”

Abraham Joshua Heschel (1907–1972)

Who and when. Jewish scholar and teacher, Poland, Germany, then the United States. The Prophets (1962), God in Search of Man (1955).

Core claim. The prophets did not describe a God of pure thought. They described a God of pathos: a God who cares, is moved, grieves, and rages. Pathos is not weakness or irrational emotion. It is God’s chosen involvement with creatures. Heschel called his view “the theology of pathos” or “divine pathos” and argued the Greek idea of an unmoved mover was at odds with prophetic experience.

Scripture. Hosea 11:8–9; Jeremiah 31:20 (“my heart yearns for him”); Jeremiah 8:18–9:1; Isaiah 63:9 (“in all their affliction he was afflicted”); Genesis 6:6.

Best argument. The prophets saw God’s anger and grief as the center of their message. To say these are only figures of speech is to ignore how the prophets experienced them. A God of pure self-sufficiency would not need to send prophets.

Main objection. Maimonides and the long Jewish tradition of negative theology held that God’s perfection means no emotion. Defenders of that tradition say Heschel is reading anthropomorphisms literally. Heschel replied that the prophets’ language is not about God’s inner states but about God’s relation to people. He insisted the pathos is not a loss of power.

For a person praying. In Heschel, prayer is joining God in what he cares about. He famously described prayer as meeting a God who is already looking for us (“God in search of man”).

Jürgen Moltmann (b. 1926)

Who and when. German Reformed theologian. Theology of Hope (1964), The Crucified God (1972, English 1974), The Trinity and the Kingdom (1980).

Core claim. The cross is not an event that God watches from far away. It happens within God. The Father gives up the Son and the Son is abandoned, and the Spirit holds them together. God is not “above” suffering. He takes suffering into his own life. Moltmann thought the old doctrine of impassibility was the product of Greek thought, and that it is not compatible with the God who is love. He also argued that the future is God’s mode of coming: God’s being is “coming” (Hebrews, Revelation 1:4), not only “past” or “timeless.”

Scripture. Mark 15:34 (“My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?”); Romans 8:32; 1 John 4:8; 2 Corinthians 5:19; Revelation 1:8.

Best argument. Only a God who suffers can be with the suffering. The problem of Auschwitz and the cross together made suffering a theological problem for the whole 20th century. Moltmann argued that classical theology had no answer for it.

Main objection. Does God become less than God if he suffers? Does he need the world to be complete? Critics (Thomists, Reformed, Orthodox) said Moltmann blurred the Creator and the creature, and that a God who cannot overcome suffering cannot save us from it.

For a person praying. In Moltmann, I pray to a God who has already been where I am.

Kazoh Kitamori (1916–1998)

Who and when. Japanese Lutheran theologian. Theology of the Pain of God (1946, English 1965).

Core claim. The core of the gospel is that God’s love is love rooted in pain. God’s pain arises because he loves those who deserve wrath. The “pain of God” is the pain of loving sinners. Kitamori drew on Jeremiah 31:20 and Isaiah 63:9. He wrote in Japan just after World War II, and the book is often described as one of the first major non-Western theological works to be widely heard in the West.

Scripture. Jeremiah 31:20; Isaiah 63:9; Romans 8:32; Hosea 11.

Best argument. If God’s love for sinners cost him nothing, it wouldn’t be much of a love. The cross is the place where God’s wrath and love meet, and the result is pain in God.

Main objection. Critics said Kitamori risks making God’s pain a necessity within God, and so blurs the freedom of God’s love. Others said the analogy to human pain is too direct.

For a person praying. Kitamori: prayer is not only asking. It is taking part in the pain with which God holds the world.

Philosophers of religion in the analytic tradition

Who and when. Reformed philosopher at Calvin College, then Yale. Essay “God Everlasting” (1975).

Read the whole section

Nicholas Wolterstorff

Core claim. God is everlasting, not timeless. The Bible’s God acts, speaks, remembers, and responds in sequence. A timeless being could not do these things. Wolterstorff also argued that a timeless God cannot know what is happening now, since tensed facts change, and God, being omniscient, must know them. If God knows tensed facts, he must be in time. He is a leading defender of divine temporality, and of the argument from omniscience and tensed facts.

Scripture. The whole narrative of God’s dealings with Israel; Exodus 3 and 19; Hosea 11.

Best argument. “God remembers,” “God rescues,” and “God will come” would be false or mere metaphor if God were timeless. A timeless God cannot be a living God with a history.

Main objection. Timelessness defenders say tensed knowledge isn’t needed. A timeless God knows each tensed fact in a tenseless way, or by direct awareness (William Alston offered one version).

Nelson Pike

Who and when. American philosopher, Cornell and elsewhere. God and Timelessness (1970), and the 1965 article “Divine Omniscience and Voluntary Action.”

Core claim. In the 1965 paper, Pike set out the modern form of the foreknowledge dilemma. If God believed yesterday that I would do X, I cannot now do otherwise, since that would mean either changing the past or making God wrong. Pike is widely credited with presenting the dilemma clearly and forcefully. In God and Timelessness, he argued that timelessness raises troubles of its own, mostly that a timeless God could not be personal in the Bible’s sense.

Best argument. The dilemma is the sharpest modern form of the old worry. It exposes how Ockhamists, Boethians, and others each have to give something up.

Main objection. Many replies: the Ockhamist reply (soft facts), the Boethian reply (timelessness), and the Molinist reply. Philosophers list these along with the prophecy problem.

Eleonore Stump and Norman Kretzmann

Who and when. Stump (St. Louis University) and Kretzmann (Cornell). Their paper “Eternity” (1981) is the key text.

Core claim. Defend Boethius. They pulled four marks from his definition: an eternal being is alive, its life is unlimited, it has a special kind of timeless “duration,” and it possesses its whole life at once. To explain how a timeless God deals with a temporal world, they introduced ET-simultaneity: a relation between one eternal item and one temporal item. ET-simultaneity is not transitive, which prevents all times from collapsing into one. Stump also defended timelessness in a Thomistic context in her later book Aquinas (2003).

Best argument. Eternity can be treated as a mode of existence. From God’s standpoint, every moment of time is present, like an event seen from the top of a tower.

Main objections.

  • Is “atemporal duration” coherent? Fitzgerald said it is either nonsense or just duration in time.
  • A philosopher named Lewis argued that observation cannot cross the timeless/temporal divide.
  • The analogy to relativity is murky. Padgett argued the analogy is weak.

Alan Padgett

Who and when. Alan G. Padgett, God, Eternity and the Nature of Time (1992).

Core claim. Relative timelessness. God is timeless relative to our measured time, but not in an absolute sense. God has his own “metaphysical” time. Padgett distinguishes “Measured Time” (the time of physics and history, bound up with the universe) from God’s own time. God is in the second, not measured by the first. He also holds that God changes in some ways, and so is temporal in the broad sense.

Scripture. Psalm 90; Revelation 1:8; Isaiah 57:15; John 1.

Best argument. Relativity theory suggests that there is no single universal “now” in physics, so a timeless God is hard to link to it. But a God who is not tied to physical time can still be temporal in the other sense.

Main objection. Critics ask how God’s time differs from ordinary time, and whether the distinction is just a verbal fix.

William Lane Craig

Who and when. William Lane Craig. Time and Eternity (2001); God, Time, and Eternity (2001); earlier The Only Wise God (1987).

Core claim. God is timeless without creation and temporal with creation. God enters time at the moment of creation. The objection that “before” creation implies time is answered by saying there was no “before.” God’s timelessness was not a state that came earlier. Craig holds the A-theory of time (the view that real tense exists: some events are present, and the present moves), and supports it with a neo-Lorentzian reading of relativity. He argues that if the A-theory is true, God’s omniscience of tensed facts requires him to be temporal. He distinguishes “Measured Time” from “Ontological Time”.

Scripture. Titus 1:2 and 2 Timothy 1:9 (God’s promises or grace “before the ages began” or “before the beginning of time”); John 17:24 (“before the foundation of the world”); Ephesians 1:4; and the whole account of God’s acts in history.

Best argument. It joins the creation argument (time began, so God existed without time at least “then”) to the argument from divine action and knowledge (God acts and knows the present now, so he is in time now).

Main objection. The change from timeless to temporal seems to be a change in God. If God changes from timeless to temporal, he isn’t immutable. Craig accepts that, and says this is a change of relation and not in nature.

Paul Helm

Who and when. Paul Helm, Eternal God (1988; 2nd ed. 2010); The Providence of God (1993).

Core claim. Strong timelessness, in the Augustinian-Calvinist line. God has “no succession,” and so his knowledge, will, and act are one. Helm defends divine timelessness as the best way to say that God is Lord of time, and says it fits meticulous providence.

Best argument. Timelessness secures the Creator-creature distinction. A God in time is a very big creature.

Main objection. Does it make God’s relation to the world too abstract?

Brian Leftow

Who and when. Brian Leftow, Time and Eternity (1991).

Core claim. All temporal things exist together with God in timeless eternity. His “Zero Thesis” is that God’s distance from spatial things is zero, so they share one frame in which all events are simultaneous. He later developed “Quasi-Temporal Eternality,” in which God’s life has earlier and later points without real succession.

Objection. The step to zero distance is called unsound, and the view sits uneasily with the B-theory.

Richard Swinburne

Who and when. Richard Swinburne, The Coherence of Theism (1977; revised 1993), The Christian God (1994), Providence and the Problem of Evil (1998).

Core claim. God is everlasting, not timeless. Swinburne has long argued that the idea of timeless eternity is incoherent. He has also argued that God’s knowledge of the future is shaped by the nature of free acts: God knows all that is logically possible to know, and truly free acts are not yet there to be known. On this point his views have been described as close to open theism, though his position has shifted and is best stated loosely.

Best argument. A person has thoughts and acts one after another. A being with no succession cannot have thoughts or act at all.

Main objection. The classical response is that this treats God as a bigger person, not as the source of persons.

For a person praying (all of the philosophers above). Timelessness: God hears every prayer in his single eternal act. Temporalism: God hears your prayer after you pray it, and then acts.

Open theism

Who and when. The term goes back to Richard Rice’s 1980 book The Openness of God. The movement became public with The Openness of God: A Biblical Challenge to the Traditional Understanding of God (1994), by Clark Pinnock, Richard Rice, John Sanders, William Hasker, and David Basinger. Greg Boyd wrote God of the Possible (2000); John Sanders wrote The God Who Risks (1998; 2nd ed. 2007). Rice is usually credited with introducing the term, and the 1994 book set off a firestorm of controversy.

Read the whole section

Core claim in plain words. God is a loving person who made a world with real freedom in it. Because the freedom is real, some of the future is not yet settled, and not even God knows it ahead of time. God knows everything that can be known: all of the past and present, all possibilities, and all the settled parts of the future. He is not caught off guard because he is smart enough to deal with whatever happens. God is in time (everlasting). God takes risks. God can be moved. God can change his mind about what to do, though not about who he is.

Their scripture.

  • God’s regrets: Genesis 6:6; 1 Samuel 15:11, 35.
  • Tests and discovery: Genesis 22:12; Deuteronomy 8:2; 2 Chronicles 32:31.
  • “Perhaps” and “if”: Exodus 3:18, 4:8–9; Jeremiah 26:3; Ezekiel 12:3.
  • God changing the plan: Exodus 32:9–14; Jonah 3:10; Isaiah 38:1–5; Jeremiah 18:7–10.
  • God saying “it did not enter my mind”: Jeremiah 32:35; 19:5.
  • Relationship: Hosea 11:8; John 15:15 (friends); James 4:8.
  • The incarnation and God’s real answer in Christ.

Their best argument. Take the Bible’s picture of a God in a back-and-forth relationship at face value. If everything is settled from eternity, then prayer, warnings, and conditional promises (“if my people will humble themselves,” 2 Chronicles 7:14) lose their force. And God’s own word that he “regretted” is hard to square with a God who knew exactly how it would turn out.

The reply to the prophecy texts. Open theists say God can predict the future in three ways: (1) he knows what he himself plans to do; (2) he can see where present causes are heading; (3) he can foretell, with certainty, things he has decided to bring about no matter what. Peter’s denial (Matthew 26:34) is a prediction based on knowing Peter’s character, with a risk of being wrong. Some, like Boyd, say God can also speak of what will probably happen.

Main objections (the critics).

  • Bruce Ware, God’s Lesser Glory (2000): the view diminishes God’s glory, and open theism is a departure from evangelical doctrine. (It is often called the most influential critique of open theism.)
  • John Frame, No Other God: A Response to Open Theism (2001): the Bible’s God is Lord, not a risk-taker. He challenges the open theist reading of the relenting texts.
  • John Piper and others (Beyond the Bounds, 2003): open theism cuts at the comfort of God’s sovereignty in suffering.
  • Norman Geisler (with others, The Battle for God, 2001) called it “neotheism,” arguing it is not orthodox. Millard Erickson (What Does God Know and When Does He Know It?, 2003) accused open theists of selective reading and of caricaturing the classical view.
  • The core objections: (a) the Bible says God declares the end from the beginning (Isaiah 41–48); (b) if God doesn’t know the future, how can he give reliable promises or guide history? (c) a God who learns is a God who changes in his knowledge, which looks like a loss of perfection; (d) the “immutability” texts (Numbers 23:19; Malachi 3:6).
  • Open-theist replies. The immutability texts are about God’s character and faithfulness, not about his every thought or plan. This is a common open-theist reply. The “end from the beginning” texts speak of what God resolves to do.

Evangelical Theological Society debates in the early 2000s. The Evangelical Theological Society is a large American society of evangelical scholars. Its doctrinal basis is a short statement of biblical inerrancy and the Trinity. In the early 2000s the society debated whether open theism fit that basis. Members pressed charges against leading open theists such as Clark Pinnock and John Sanders. In the end the society did not expel them.

Open and relational theology. Thomas Jay Oord is the best-known recent figure. His The Nature of Love: A Theology (2010) argues that God’s nature is uncontrolling love, and that God cannot override creatures. This is closer to process thought than to the evangelical open theists, who say God chooses not to override. Oord lists four paths to open and relational theology: the biblical witness, themes in Christian tradition, the philosophy of free will, and reconciling faith and science. Oord’s “essential kenosis” is his own term for the idea that God’s self-giving love is necessary to God.

For a person praying. Open theism says prayer matters in the strongest sense: God really takes your request into account, and the outcome may be different because you asked.

Eastern Orthodox perspective

Who and when. The Greek-speaking East from the 4th century on. Key figures: the Cappadocians (4th c.), Pseudo-Dionysius (c. 500), Maximus the Confessor (c. 580–662), John of Damascus (c. 675–749), and Gregory Palamas (1296–1359). Modern voices include Vladimir Lossky (The Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church, 1944), John Meyendorff, and Christos Yannaras.

Read the whole section

Core claim in plain words. God’s essence is beyond all knowing and beyond all time-talk. But God has energies, which are God himself acting and giving himself to the world. We meet God in the energies, not in the essence. This “essence and energies” distinction was defended by Palamas in the 14th century against critics who said it broke up God’s unity. A church council in Constantinople in the mid-1300s endorsed it.

Time and eternity. The East has no single doctrine of timelessness. Gregory of Nyssa spoke of the creature’s life as diastema (a spread-out existence) and of God’s life as beyond it. Maximus the Confessor wrote that created things have a beginning and move toward an end, and that God, who has neither, is “beyond” this motion. Maximus describes creation as a movement (kinesis) from its beginning (genesis) to a rest (stasis) in God, and humans as the ones who can take that movement up consciously. The East also speaks of aion, the age, as a created mode between time and eternity, in which the saints and angels live. In the East, eternity is not mostly a logic puzzle about simultaneity. It is the fullness of life that humans are called to share.

The logoi. Maximus taught that every creature has a logos, a divine intention or word, in the Logos (Christ). All the logoi are gathered in him. God’s purposes for each creature are “in” him before time, and yet creatures live them out in time.

Divine-human synergy. The East speaks of synergy: God and humans work together in salvation. Grace is not a force that overrides the will. Human freedom is real, and grace heals it. The East has not followed Augustine’s later line on predestination. It did not hold a Calvinist-style decree. Orthodox writers often say Western debates over foreknowledge and free will are framed by Western assumptions (such as the idea that God’s essence is known and that divine simplicity means no real distinction). Orthodox thinkers hold that God is Lord of all, has complete knowledge, and does not need to be “informed” by prayer. They also hold that God truly responds. The tension is held in prayer, not solved by theory.

Impassibility in the East. Orthodox thinkers hold that God’s nature is impassible, and they also say that one of the Trinity suffered in the flesh, a phrase connected with the Fifth Ecumenical Council in the sixth century and the “theopaschite” formula. Cyril’s idea of suffering impassibly is basic. Orthodox writers such as David Bentley Hart (The Beauty of the Infinite, 2003) have defended a strong form of impassibility, and others (Bulgakov) went toward a suffering God. This is a live debate.

Scripture. Exodus 33:18–23 (Moses sees only God’s back); 2 Peter 1:4 (“partakers of the divine nature”); John 17:21–23; Isaiah 55:8–9; Romans 11:33–36; Psalm 90.

Best argument. The essence-energies distinction lets the East say both that God is wholly beyond (apophatic) and wholly present (in the energies). It explains how one can truly know God without comprehending him.

Main objection. Western critics (and some Orthodox) ask whether the distinction splits God. If the energies are God, how do they differ from the essence? If they are not, what do we meet?

For a person praying. In the East, prayer is a path to union. The Jesus Prayer (“Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me”) is the best-known form. It is not mainly a request that God change things. It is standing before God in a way that transforms the one praying.

Pentecostal and charismatic reception

This section paints in broad strokes. The specifics vary a great deal from church to church.

Read the whole section

Who and when. The Pentecostal movement began around 1900–1906 (Topeka, Azusa Street). The charismatic renewal in the older churches began in the 1960s. Today these groups number in the hundreds of millions worldwide. Unlike the movements above, they did not start with a doctrine of time. They started with experience: healing, tongues, prophecy, answered prayer.

Core claim in plain words. These churches tend to assume that God is personal, near, and active now. They expect prayer to change things: bodies, finances, relationships, nations. Many of them preach that God responds to faith and persistence in prayer. That way of speaking fits a relational reading of God. It sits less easily with strict timelessness or meticulous determinism, though the churches seldom say so in these terms.

Different positions inside the family.

  • Many Pentecostal churches have an Arminian or Wesleyan background (the Holiness roots), so they stress human response and the sincerity of the gospel offer.
  • A significant Reformed-charismatic stream (sometimes called “Calvinistic charismatics”) holds both God’s sovereignty and the gifts of the Spirit.
  • Some Pentecostal scholars have written on open theism with interest. Clark Pinnock was himself a leading voice on the Spirit and wrote on Pentecostal-charismatic themes, and Amos Yong and others have engaged both.
  • Yet many Pentecostal leaders reject open theism on the question of foreknowledge. They affirm that God knows all things and that prophecy is real. Prophecy, as these churches practice it, assumes God knows the future.

Scripture they lean on. James 5:14–16 (the prayer of faith will save the sick; the prayer of a righteous person has great power); Mark 11:22–24; Luke 18:1–8 (the persistent widow); Genesis 18:22–33 (Abraham bargains for Sodom); Exodus 32:11–14; Matthew 7:7–11; Acts 12:5–12 (the church prays Peter out of prison); Acts 2:17–18 (the Spirit poured out).

Best argument. The testimony of ongoing experience: when people pray, things happen. A theology that makes prayer decorative cannot explain what the church sees and hears. They also point to the New Testament, where prayer is plainly meant to do something, and to Jesus’ own repeated “ask, seek, knock.”

Main objection. Critics say that this view of prayer can make God seem like a vending machine, and can leave people crushed when healing does not come. It can also treat unanswered prayer as a failure of faith. Theologically, a prayer-heavy view may drift toward a limited God who needs our prayer to act. Pentecostal writers answer that God’s sovereignty and human prayer are held together by faith and not explained.

Why relational or open readings appeal. Three reasons are commonly given.

  • The language of prayer is the language of relationship: asking, waiting, persisting, being heard.
  • Open readings give answers to the question “why did God not act?” that do not blame the sufferer: freedom, an open future, and a God who grieves.
  • The reading matches what believers say they feel in prayer, namely that God responds.

For a person praying. In this setting, prayer is a conversation with a living God who listens and acts. The theology behind it can vary, but the practice is the same.

  • c. 500–300 BC, Greek philosophy. Parmenides’ changeless being; Plato’s “moving image of eternity”; Aristotle’s unmoved mover and the sea-battle puzzle.
  • c. 20 BC–50 AD, Philo. Moses meets Plato. God is unchangeable; the human-sounding passages are figures of speech.
  • 2nd–4th centuries, early church. Irenaeus’ unfolding plan; Origen on foreknowledge and prayer; the Cappadocians on the infinite God; impassibility defined with care (“suffers impassibly”).
  • 354–430, Augustine. Time is a creature. God’s years are “one today.” Foreknowledge does not force.
  • c. 524, Boethius. Eternity as the whole, simultaneous, and perfect possession of unending life. The hilltop image.
  • 1033–1109, Anselm. God as greater than anything conceivable; eternity as identity with being; the line that God is compassionate from our side but not from his own.
  • 1265–74, Aquinas. Pure act, simplicity, analogy, future contingents seen in eternity, prayer as a cause.
  • c. 1300–1350, Scotus and Ockham. Real contingency; the hard/soft fact answer to the past-is-fixed argument.
  • 16th century, Luther, Calvin, Socinus, Molina. The bound will; unconditional election and providence; future free acts called unknowable; middle knowledge (1588).
  • 1609–1619, Arminius and Dort. Conditional election versus the five Canons.
  • 1739–1754, Wesley and Edwards. Free grace to all with eternal-present foreknowledge; compatibilist freedom.
  • 19th century, forerunners. Clarke, McCabe, Hibbard, Dorner and others float limits on foreknowledge.
  • 1929–1948, process theology. Whitehead and Hartshorne’s dipolar God.
  • 1940s–1970s, Barth, Cullmann, Heschel, Kitamori, Moltmann. God’s own time; the line of salvation history; divine pathos; the pain of God; the crucified God.
  • 1965–2001, analytic philosophy of religion. Pike’s dilemma; Wolterstorff’s everlasting God; Stump and Kretzmann’s ET-simultaneity; Padgett; Craig; Helm; Leftow; Swinburne.
  • 1994–2003, open theism. The Openness of God, Sanders, Boyd; critics Ware, Frame, Piper, Geisler, Erickson; the Evangelical Theological Society disputes.
  • 2000s, open and relational theology. Oord’s uncontrolling love. Growing conversation with science and with Pentecostal scholars.

The menu of positions today

1. Strong timelessness. Who: Augustine, Boethius, Anselm, Aquinas in their strong form; Paul Helm; the Reformed scholastics. The view: God has no succession at all. He is simple, immutable, and impassible. All times are present to him. Best text: Psalm 90:2, “from everlasting to everlasting,” with Malachi 3:6 and James 1:17. Hardest question: How can a God who has no “now” act in this moment, answer a prayer after it is prayed, or know what time it is? The view answers with analogy and timeless willing, but many find the answer thin.

Read the whole section

2. Timeless, with analogical language. Who: Aquinas and Thomists, Eleonore Stump and Norman Kretzmann in their modern form, and much of Orthodoxy in its own vocabulary. The view: God is timeless, but all our words about him (including “know,” “act,” and “love”) are stretched, not literal. The Bible’s time-language is true as analogy. Best text: Isaiah 55:8–9 (“my thoughts are not your thoughts”) and Exodus 3:14. Hardest question: If the words are stretched so far, what is left that we can say is true? Stump and Kretzmann’s ET-simultaneity tries to give content but is accused of obscurity.

3. Relative timelessness. Who: Alan Padgett, Gregory Ganssle, and others. The view: God is timeless relative to physical, measured time (which began with the universe) but has his own kind of order, so he can change and act. Best text: Psalm 90 and Revelation 1:8 (“who is and who was and who is to come”). Hardest question: Is “God’s time” a real thing different from ordinary time? Or just temporality under another name?

4. Timeless then temporal. Who: William Lane Craig. The view: Without creation, God is timeless. By creating, God enters time and stays in it. Best text: Titus 1:2 (“before the ages began”) with the Bible’s story of God’s dealings in history. Hardest question: Is the change from timeless to temporal a real change in God? If so, how can a changeless God make it? Craig answers with “there was no before,” but critics say the story still sounds like a sequence.

5. Everlasting and temporal, with exhaustive foreknowledge. Who: Wolterstorff, Swinburne in part, many Ockhamists, many Arminians, and others. The view: God is in time, has no beginning or end, and knows the future completely (perhaps by Ockhamist soft facts). Best text: Isaiah 46:9–10, “declaring the end from the beginning,” read with the Bible’s narrative God. Hardest question: Pike’s dilemma. If God believed yesterday what I will do tomorrow, can I still do otherwise?

6. Simple foreknowledge. Who: Many Arminians and Wesleyan-minded evangelicals. The view: God foresees all that will happen, including free acts. Foreseeing does not cause. God’s election is based on what he foresees. Best text: Romans 8:29 and 1 Peter 1:2. Hardest question: If God foresees what I will freely do, how is it open to me? And if my act is the ground of his plan, did God learn it from me, or did it come before me?

7. Molinism. Who: Luis de Molina, Thomas Flint, William Lane Craig, and Alvin Plantinga in his use of the idea. The view: God knows what every possible free creature would do in every situation, and designs the world with that in view. This secures both providence and freedom. Best text: 1 Samuel 23:6–13 (what Saul and the men of Keilah would have done) and Matthew 11:21. Hardest question: The grounding objection. What makes “she would freely do X” true before she exists and without God deciding it?

8. Open theism. Who: Pinnock, Rice, Sanders, Hasker, Basinger, Boyd. The view: God is in time, has a real relationship with creatures, and knows all that can be known. Free future acts are not yet there to be known. God takes risks and responds. Best text: Genesis 6:6, Exodus 32:14, Jonah 3:10, and Jeremiah 18:7–10. Hardest question: Isaiah 46:10 and the great prophecies. How can God promise a final outcome (and not only his intent) if he does not know what free creatures will choose? The answer is that God can bring about what he has decided, but critics ask whether that is enough comfort in suffering.

9. Process theism. Who: Whitehead, Hartshorne, Cobb, Griffin; and, in a related line, Oord. The view: God is dipolar: changeless in character, changing in experience. God is affected by the world, cannot coerce, and persuades. Creation is ongoing, with no first beginning from nothing in most versions. Best text: 1 John 4:8 (“God is love”) and Hosea 11:8 (“How can I give you up?”). Hardest question: If God cannot override anything, what grounds the hope that evil will not have the last word, and in what sense is this still the God of the Bible who raises the dead?

Harold R. Eberle

The framework behind the relational Father

Eberle’s Father-Son Theology is the framework behind the relational-Father reading laid out elsewhere in this material. God is a Father who planned a family before creation (Ephesians 1:4-5). He lives and acts in time with his children. He matures them step by step from servant to child to heir. And he partners with them in prayer.

Core claims, in plain words

The publisher’s description of the second edition is the best open summary. It says:

  • The aim is to help readers see God as Father and themselves as his sons and daughters.
  • Centuries of Western-culture Christianity have hidden this.
  • Christianity must be separated from the dualism at the base of Western civilization.
  • Plato’s influence came into the church mainly through Augustine and Calvin, and Eberle rejects it. He wants the Hebrew picture of God instead.
  • The idea that God’s holiness requires separation from sinners is a false doctrine, which he dates to the fourth century.

Its description says Western philosophy has its roots in the ancient Greek worldview, that Christianity has become tangled with it, and that this produced great errors. God and time

Eberle’s own page or chapter on time is not freely available online. What is available is secondhand.

Reader reviews of Who Is God? say that Eberle holds that God is not timeless. They say he teaches that God can change his mind but his nature does not change, that God is sovereign in power but leaves room for human input, and that the book treats the open view of God as the biblical view. So the best-supported summary is this. Eberle places God inside time, in a real back-and-forth with people, and he blames the opposite view on Greek philosophy passed down through Augustine. This fits how he is generally described. His own words on the subject are not freely available.

Free will, sovereignty, changing his mind

According to reviewers, God is sovereign in power, but he has chosen to leave room for human input, and some outcomes depend on what people do. God can change his mind in response, while his nature stays the same. This is the standard open-view shape: a stable character and flexible plans. The match to open theism’s usual line is the reviewer’s own framing.

Eberle’s public statement of faith is far more traditional and does not mention free will. It says the Father loves the world, is involved with humanity, and is working all things after the counsel of his will. That wording comes from Ephesians 1:11, a favorite Reformed text. So the short statement and the book as reviewers describe it are in some tension, or at least the statement is silent where the book is bold. A good debate point: the short creed is conventional, the longer teaching is not.

Prayer and partnering with God

No open Eberle page on prayer is available. His publisher’s blurb for Who Is God? says that your view of God shapes every decision you make and every prayer you pray. From his open-view position, prayer would be a real partnership. If God leaves room for human input, prayer is one of the inputs. That is a reasonable reading of his position, not something found in his own words.

Human nature, covenant stages, maturing sons

His book Precious in His Sight is a fresh look at the nature of man. The publisher’s description of the theology book says no gap opened at the fall. That fits the common description of him as rejecting inherited guilt and Reformed total depravity. His own text on this is not freely available. The “covenant stages and maturing sons” idea is likewise not something an open source confirms. It belongs to the framework as it is taught, as laid out above.

God as Father

This is the center of the whole system. The name “Father-Son Theology” tells you. The Bible’s basic frame is a Father raising children toward maturity, not a judge and a defendant. Holiness is love that reaches out, not distance. The cross is not the Father turning away from the Son.

Critiques and reviews

Public reviews are mostly short reader comments. They praise the book as very challenging and life changing. Few formal scholarly or evangelical reviews of Father-Son Theology are publicly available. A Reformed critic would likely raise these points. They are the kinds of points critics would make:

  • His account of Augustine and Calvin as Platonists is a very large claim. Scholars note that Augustine criticized parts of Platonism, and that the early church’s talk of an unchanging God also leans on Psalm 102:27, Malachi 3:6, and James 1:17, not only on Greek thought.
  • A Greek-or-Hebrew split is too neat. Jewish thinkers in Alexandria, such as Philo, mixed the two before Christ.
  • The charge that a view is “Greek” does not show it is false. It needs an argument from Scripture.

Strongest critique

He asks the right question: did Greek philosophy bend the doctrine of God? But in the open record the claim is stated more than argued. Classical theists answer that unchangeableness is taught in Scripture itself.

Place on the time question

Inside time. This rests on reviewers’ reports and on his open-view alignment, so it is a fairly but not completely firm placement.

2

Paul Ellis (Escape to Reality)

Paul Ellis is a New Zealander who grew up in Western Australia, pastored a church in Hong Kong, and now lives in Auckland. He has a doctorate and teaches at a university. He says he burned out working six days a week, was forced to rest, and then began learning grace. He runs Escape to Reality (E2R), launched in February 2010, with about a thousand articles indexed by scripture and subject.

Read the whole section

Who he is

His about page names these books: The Gospel in Ten Words, The Gospel in Twenty Questions, The Hyper-Grace Gospel, Stuff Jesus Never Said, AD70 and the End of the World, Letters from Jesus, The Chocolate Gospel, The Silent Queen, and The Grace Bible with a Grace Commentary in progress.

Core message

His own summary is that grace is not just a doctrine but a Person. There is no bad news in the good news. Life is learning that you are a dearly loved child of God. Faith rests in what Jesus did, not in rule-keeping. Repentance means a change of mind and turning to Jesus, and he is skeptical of making “turning from sin” the main thing.

His theology of God’s goodness

This is where he touches the debate’s questions, though never as a time question. The thread across his articles is this: Jesus is the clearest picture of the Father, so any text that makes God the author of sickness, death, or loss gets read through Jesus.

Job 1:21. His article “Does God Give and Take Away?” says the verse is one of the most misused in the Bible. God is a giver. The thief in Job is the devil. Job had an incomplete view of God and spoke foolishly in grief. Ellis tells readers to resist loss rather than accept it as God’s will. In his words, in substance, God is not behind your loss. He cites Job 42:3, where Job says he spoke of things he did not understand.

Isaiah 45:7 (“I create disaster”). Ellis says the verse was spoken to Cyrus, a Persian from a two-powers religion (light versus dark). God was saying he alone is behind all outcomes in history. It does not mean God authors evil. He ends by saying God is good all the time, and he cites Isaiah 5:20 against calling evil good.

Ananias and Sapphira (Acts 5). He argues God did not kill them. Christ’s death removed the condemnation, and the couple likely died from the shock of exposure, a natural result of sin. His point is that God does not kill sinners but saves them, and that if you must blame someone, you should blame sin. In a later comment he reportedly said he does not believe God killed anyone after the cross.

Old Testament wrath. In “The Wrath of God in the New Covenant” he argues that Old Testament links between God’s anger and sin belong to the time before the cross. After Christ’s once-for-all sacrifice, God’s wrath does not answer misdeeds. “Disobedience” in the New Testament means unbelief, so wrath falls on those who refuse to trust his goodness. He says Jesus changed the game, and that his sacrifice was the once-and-final solution for sin.

Sickness and discipline. Articles titled “Does God Scourge Us? 7 Reasons to Say ‘No!’”, “Does God Kill Babies?”, and “Did God Hand Job Over to Satan? (Job 2:6)” are on the site. Summaries of his work say he explains Miriam’s leprosy as a king punishing a rebellious subject under the old frame, not a Father disciplining a child.

Jesus as revelation. He leans on John 14:9 (“whoever has seen me has seen the Father”) and Hebrews 1:3, and says Jesus healed leprosy rather than giving it.

Sovereignty: “Is God in control?”

No E2R article with the title “Is God in control?” seems to exist. The nearest are the Job, Isaiah 45:7, and Christchurch earthquake articles (“The Christchurch Earthquake: 4 Questions Christians Can Answer” appears in the index). From these, a clear reading is that Ellis denies that God sends disasters and sickness, and he says God’s gifts are never withdrawn. That is a denial of meticulous sovereignty over evil. It is not a denial that God is ultimately in charge of history. His Isaiah 45 article says God is behind all outcomes in history in the sense of working through events.

Foreknowledge and predestination

Ellis says on E2R, mostly in comments, that predestination means “predetermined” in the sense of God having a plan, not God choosing some and rejecting others. He reads Ephesians 1 as God’s plan to bring in the Gentiles, and says God knew ahead that he would invite them. He also writes that God knows everything you have ever done and everything you will ever do, and that this does not disappoint him.

This is notable for the debate. Ellis seems to assume God knows the future in full. That sits with the classical view, not the open view, even though his picture of God’s character is warm and relational. He uses foreknowledge to comfort. God knew all about you and still chose you, the way Reformed preachers say it.

Prayer

No E2R article on how prayer changes what God does is publicly known. His emphasis on faith as rest, and on God already having given everything in Christ, suggests prayer as receiving what is already given, not persuading a reluctant God. He does not say this himself. It is a reasonable reading.

Does God change his mind?

No E2R article on this is publicly known. His one relevant move is about repentance (metanoia), which means a change of mind. He applies this to human beings, not to God. For God he emphasizes that the Father has not changed. Jesus is the unchanging picture of the Father, and the Old Testament frame was “before the cross.” That is a historical change in how God dealt with people under a covenant, not a change of God.

What it implies for time

Ellis does not address time. A careful reading goes like this. His view requires a God who responds to the cross as a turning point, which sounds like God acting in history. But he says God’s nature is not what changed. He therefore keeps both a historic turning point and a stable character. He assumes full foreknowledge. A listener could place him as in time in action, and settled in character and knowledge. That is a reading, not his claim.

The critics: Michael L. Brown

Michael L. Brown’s Hyper-Grace: Exposing the Dangers of the Modern Grace Message (Charisma House, 2014) calls the modern grace message a mixture. Brown sees real Jesus-lifting revelation, but also misreading of Scripture, bad theology, and divisive rhetoric. He allows that many are truly blessed, and objects to the imbalance. One reviewer says Brown presents hyper-grace teachers as opposed to repentance and confession of sins.

Ellis answered in two ways. One is the book The Hyper-Grace Gospel: A Response to Michael Brown and Those Opposed to the Modern Grace Message, which he says won a silver medal at the Illumination Book Awards. The other is a blog post, “Some Honest Answers for Michael Brown.”

In that post he answers eleven questions from Brown to Joseph Prince and others. His answers, in summary:

  • Blessings come through Christ, not as a reward for obedience.
  • Believers can grieve the Holy Spirit, but only because he cares for their good. Do not confuse behavior with identity.
  • Sin is destructive to the believer. It will not undo the work of the cross, but it could undo you.
  • God does not forgive because you ask. Asking is allowed.
  • Fear of the Lord means worship, not terror.
  • He sees no danger in assuring believers that Jesus keeps his promises.

Other critics of the movement include R. C. Sproul and Ligonier, Grace to You, Daniel K. Norris, and Paul Barker of The Gospel Coalition Australia. Brown’s ministry, Line of Fire, posted a reply titled “Dr. Paul Ellis Underscores the Errors of Hyper-Grace.”

Strongest critique

There are two parts. First, the hard texts. Ananias and Sapphira (Acts 5), Herod (Acts 12:23), the Corinthians who got sick and died after misusing the Lord’s Supper (1 Corinthians 11:30), and Hebrews 12:5-11 (the Lord disciplines those he loves) all describe God acting on his people after the cross. Ellis’s reading that God never does this has to explain these away. Second, sovereignty. If God never sends hard things, what does Romans 8:28 or Genesis 50:20 (“you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good”) mean? Ellis reads Romans 8:28 as God working within circumstances, without causing sickness. So he has an answer, though critics find it thin.

Place on the time question

Unclear, since he makes no direct statement. He leans toward inside time in how God acts and relates, and he assumes complete foreknowledge. This is a loose placement.

The Shack (William Paul Young)

The Shack is a 2007 novel by William Paul Young, later a 2017 film. It has sold well over 20 million copies. A grieving father, Mack, whose daughter was murdered, is invited to a shack where he meets God as three people. Papa (the Father) is an African-American woman. Jesus is a Middle Eastern carpenter. Sarayu (the Spirit) is a small Asian woman.

Read the whole section

Who and what

Young has described a childhood of abuse and later addiction. He gave interviews about the book in 2008, including one on Sheridan Voysey’s radio program Open House. He wrote a nonfiction follow-up, Lies We Believe About God, which takes on 28 “lies.”

The picture of the Father

The picture is relational and wounded. The Father is not the distant judge Mack fears. She cooks, jokes, and has scars on her wrists. Critics describe the book’s claim as this: when Jesus became human, the whole Trinity entered the experience of suffering. The Trinity is shown as mutual relationship with no chain of command. Critics also point to an idea of partnership without any overlay of power.

Time, evil, free will

The book does not argue about time in an analysis that is publicly quoted. As the novel is generally remembered, God is not controlled by a clock and speaks about being present with Mack in his pain. This is a general impression of the novel, not a quotation.

On evil and free will, the story’s answer to suffering is relationship, not explanation. God does not cause evil, God grieves in it, and God works good out of it. Human freedom is real and God does not override it. One reviewer calls the novel part theodicy and part spiritual autobiography, and notes that Young never mentions Satan’s role.

Critiques

  • Tim Challies says Young has an inadequate and often unbiblical understanding of the Trinity. He blurs the persons and their roles. He also objects that God is shown submitting to humans, a point he credits to Albert Mohler. He adds that the cross appears sparingly, so a newcomer would not learn what Jesus’ death did. And he notes a thread that people of many religions are loved by God and brought into his family.
  • Albert Mohler (2010) says the dialogues show a theology that is unconventional at best and in places heretical. He charges universal reconciliation.
  • The patripassianism charge. Critics say the scars on Papa’s wrists suggest the Father suffered on the cross, a heresy the early church rejected.
  • Young has described himself as a hopeful universalist but declines to teach it as doctrine.
  • Young’s defense is that the book is a story, not systematic theology.

Place on the time question

Unclear, leaning inside time. Relational presence, a suffering Father, and no hierarchy all fit a God who shares the human story. But the book is fiction, and no source states a position on time.

Shorter sections

Who. Pastor of Woodland Hills Church in Minnesota and a theologian. He wrote God of the Possible (2000), the best-known popular case for open theism, and the two-volume The Crucifixion of the Warrior God (2017; the short version is Cross Vision).

Read the whole section

Greg Boyd

Core claims. Scripture shows two themes about the future: some things are settled and some are open. The future is partly a realm of possibility, so God learns as events unfold. God is still omniscient, since the open parts are not there to know as fact. God adjusts plans to human choices. One reviewer says Boyd also holds that God knows all possible futures and each person’s character perfectly, which brings him near Molinism. A blurb by the scholar Roger Olson describes Boyd’s God as all-powerful and in charge, and yet vulnerable.

Scripture. Verses where God relents or is sorry (Genesis 6:6; Exodus 32:14; 1 Samuel 15:11; Jonah 3:10), God testing people to know what is in their heart (Genesis 22:12), and Jeremiah 18:7-10 on God changing course by whether a nation repents.

Hard texts on the other side. Numbers 23:19 and 1 Samuel 15:29 (“God is not a man that he should repent”), and prophecy that foretells the future (Isaiah 46:10). His reply, as usual among open theists, is that God’s character does not change, and that God can foretell what he intends to bring about or what is nearly certain.

Time. Boyd holds that God experiences time as a sequence. Open theism needs this. If God stood outside time and saw all moments, the future would already be there to see. This is the standard open-theism argument.

Cruciform reading. In Crucifixion of the Warrior God the cross is the lens for all Scripture. Violent Old Testament portraits of God hide a character that the cross reveals. Critics charge Marcionism, the old heresy that rejected the Old Testament God.

Strongest critique. A Catholic critic and many Reformed writers say that if the future is open, then prophecy and promises depend on odds, and Romans 8:29-30 loses its footing. John Piper’s article “Answering Greg Boyd’s Openness of God Texts” is the Reformed reply.

Place. Inside time.

Brad Jersak

Who. Canadian theologian and editor, associated with Christian Orthodox and Anabaptist voices. He wrote A More Christlike God: A More Beautiful Gospel (2015).

Core claims. God is exactly like Jesus, because Jesus is God the Son and the image of the invisible God. Love is the starting point; it is not balanced against other traits. Wrath is not God’s violent payback but the built-in result of sin. The cross reconciles us to God and does not appease an angry deity. The book has three parts: “What is God?”, “The Cruciform God,” and “‘Unwrathing’ God.”

Scripture. Colossians 1:15, John 14:9, Hebrews 1:3, and Romans 1 on wrath as God handing people over.

Hard texts. Reviewers say he leans on the New Testament and quotes the Old when it fits. How he handles Old Testament violence is worked out in the book itself.

Time. Not addressed in anything publicly available. Unclear.

Critique. Randal Rauser, who is mostly persuaded by the book, says Jersak is harsh toward penal substitution, reads opponents uncharitably, uses mystery for himself and not for them, and offers a false choice of theodicies. Rauser also says Jersak’s claim that God bound himself to natural law sounds deistic. Rauser compares the book to Yancey’s book on grace as a paradigm-shifting work.

Place. Unclear. Cruciform love implies God enters the human story, but no text speaks to time.

C. Baxter Kruger

Who. American Reformed-trained theologian, founder of Perichoresis, a ministry in Jackson, Mississippi. His books include Jesus and the Undoing of Adam (2001) and The Shack Revisited (2012).

Core claims. The Triune God eternally purposed our adoption in Jesus Christ. The book is shaped by Athanasius and T. F. Torrance. The fall is described less as a legal offense and more as a distortion of how people see God and themselves. Summaries say he argues against penal substitution and legal framings. Resurrection and ascension lift humanity into the life of the Trinity.

Scripture. Ephesians 1:3-5 (chosen “before the foundation of the world” for adoption), John 17 (the oneness of Father and Son shared with us), and Galatians 4:4-7.

Time. Ephesians 1:4 makes the plan older than creation, which sounds timeless. But he stresses that the Son entered history, and Torrance’s tradition holds that God relates to time without being its prisoner. That makes his position hard to pin down.

Critique. Reformed critics say that “included in Christ” slides toward universalism and weakens the need to believe.

Place. Unclear, leaning outside or both, since the purpose is eternal and the Son entered time.

Jack Frost and Shiloh Place

Who. Jack Frost died of cancer in 2007. He founded Shiloh Place Ministries, and his wife Trish leads it. He wrote Experiencing Father’s Embrace (Destiny Image, 2002).

Core claims. Healing through agape love, sonship in Christ, and knowing the Father’s love. The “orphan heart” idea means living as if you have no father, and it is set against the “sonship” heart. The book retells the prodigal son of Luke 15. A South African reviewer praised the fresh take on that parable.

Scripture. Luke 15, Romans 8:15, and Galatians 4:6-7.

Time. Not addressed. This is a pastoral book, not a doctrine of God.

Critique. No substantial open critique is known. Likely points are that it can make personal woundedness the key to everything, and that it lacks a doctrine of God’s rule.

Place. Not applicable. The book does not take up the question.

Andrew Murray and E. M. Bounds

Murray. With Christ in the School of Prayer is a classic. Its message is that intercession is the church’s greatest source of influence, prayer is the root and strength of all other work, and the deepest prayer-power comes with abiding in Christ. God is eager to answer prayer in Jesus’ name, and people limit his promises with caveats Scripture does not contain. Christ intercedes and prays through believers.

Bounds. Power Through Prayer. He was a lawyer, then a Civil War chaplain, and prayed three hours each morning. He treats prayer as active and as effective in changing the world through the power and grace of God.

What they say on the debate. Both treat prayer as truly moving God to act, in plain language, but neither argues about time. Murray holds that God hears, and that prayer shares in Christ’s work. That is a classical-leaning frame, with Christ as the mediator, in relational language.

Critique. Reformed readers say these men overstate “ask whatever you will” and can make God’s gifts look like prizes for those who pray.

Place. Unclear. They do not speak of time. They would say God hears and answers.

The classical answer

The classical position is held by Augustine, Aquinas, the medieval church, and most Reformation confessions. It says God is eternal and unchanging, knows all things at once, rules everything, and is not moved by creatures. The writers below are modern defenders.

Read the whole section

R. C. Sproul on prayer

Summaries of his teaching say that prayer does not change God’s mind, but it changes things. Nobody can advise God or improve on his wisdom. God’s immutability covers his thoughts and his being. Passages where God seems to repent, like 1 Samuel 15:29 (“not a man that he should relent”), are read alongside narratives where God threatens judgment. Threats are conditional. Prayer is a means God uses to reach his ends. It changes outcomes and changes us. This comes from his Ligonier teaching on whether prayer changes God’s mind.

Sproul is also named as a critic of hyper-grace.

Scripture to use: Numbers 23:19, 1 Samuel 15:29, Malachi 3:6, James 1:17, Isaiah 46:10, Matthew 6:8 (“your Father knows what you need before you ask”), and James 4:2 (“you do not have because you do not ask”).

John Piper

Piper opposes open theism strongly. He endorsed Bruce Ware’s God’s Lesser Glory. He has called open theism toxic, saying in effect that it dishonors God, distorts Scripture, and damages faith. He wrote the foreword to Beyond the Bounds (Crossway, 2003), whose blurb asks how to read passages where God changes his mind, repents, or expresses surprise.

His article “God Does Not Repent Like a Man” comes from a sermon he preached in 1998. It is about 1 Samuel 15:11 and 15:29. In summary, God’s repentance happens in spite of perfect foreknowledge, whereas human repentance usually happens because we lack foreknowledge. God can feel sorrow over an act in view of foreknown evil and still choose to do it for wise reasons.

This is useful for the debate. Piper does not say God has no feelings or responses. He says God’s feelings are real, and compatible with knowing the end. He describes two parallel wills in God.

Scripture: Isaiah 46:10 (declaring the end from the beginning), Ephesians 1:11 (works all things according to his will), Romans 8:28-30, Acts 2:23 and 4:27-28 (the cross was by God’s plan yet the killers were guilty), Genesis 50:20, and Proverbs 16:9.

On free will. Classical Reformed writers say humans choose freely in the sense that they act on their own desires, while God’s decree is behind it all. This is called compatibilism. Piper has taught this at length.

James Dolezal

All That Is in God (2017) argues that God is simple (not made of parts), immutable, impassible, and timeless. Dolezal names his rivals “theistic mutualists.” These are people who say God and creation really affect one another. He says that if God can be moved, there would have to be unrealized potential in him, which would make God changeable and dependent.

R. T. Mullins reviewed the book in a journal. He criticizes it on several points. Terms like absoluteness, fullness of being, and infinity are not defined clearly. Dolezal leans on Thomistic metaphysics without much argument. His claim that divine infinity is the classical consensus is questioned. And the charge of idolatry against rivals is not helpful. Mullins also says the method is more contemplative than biblical-theological, that it treats God as ahistorical, and that Dolezal explains away biblical passages that suggest God is temporal.

Dolezal is the one voice here who answers the time question flatly: outside time. Everything in Scripture that sounds like God changing is figurative speech for God’s unchanging act toward a changing creation.

Kevin DeYoung

In a talk at the Together for the Gospel conference, “‘Tis Mystery All, The Immortal Dies,” DeYoung argues that impassibility, properly defined, is both biblical and a buttress to the gospel. He says God’s majesty is weakened if God can suffer and face pain. He frames the incarnation as the immortal one dying, which makes possible what was otherwise impossible. He is also a council member of The Gospel Coalition, and he wrote Impossible Christianity (Crossway, 2023). No DeYoung piece on hyper-grace is known.

The Westminster Confession, chapter 2, section 1, says God has no body, parts, or passions.

The Reformed answer to the “relational Father” in short

  • Scripture speaks of God in human terms because we are human. God “repents,” “comes down” (Genesis 11:5), and “remembers.” This is accommodation. The same book says in plain terms that he does not change (Malachi 3:6; James 1:17).
  • Real relationship does not need a changing God. We relate to God as Father. He does not need to learn or be surprised for that to be true. A Father who knows the end and loves you in it is more comfort, not less.
  • Prayer is a means. God rules the ends and the means together. We pray because he commands it and works through it.
  • Grace theology and sovereignty. The Reformed can agree with Ellis that Christ is the center and that the believer is secure. They split on whether God ever sends hard things to his children. They point to Hebrews 12:5-11, 1 Corinthians 11:30, Job 42:11 (his friends “comforted him for all the evil that the Lord had brought upon him”), and Lamentations 3:38.
  • The cross. Acts 2:23 says he was delivered up “according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God.” God’s own plan included the worst evil. That is why they resist any picture where God is only reacting.

Strongest critique of the classical side (for balance)

Mullins’s review of Dolezal is a good example. Much of the classical case rests on a particular metaphysics (Thomism) and on a reading of “simple” and “pure act” that many evangelicals did not hold before the 1900s. Relational writers say a God who cannot be affected cannot truly love, since love involves being moved by the beloved. The reply of DeYoung and others is that the cross is God’s love in action without God being diminished.

Three lines that could drive the debate

  • Foreknowledge splits the grace camp. Ellis, a warm relational voice, says God knows everything you will ever do. Boyd and Eberle say the future is not all settled. So “relational” does not equal “inside time.”
  • ”Greek philosophy” versus “Scripture.” Eberle’s complaint is that the timeless God came from Plato by way of Augustine. The classical reply is that Malachi 3:6 and James 1:17 say it plainly, and that the Hebrew-versus-Greek split is overdrawn.
  • The Father’s suffering. The Shack (Papa with scars) and Boyd’s vulnerable God say a loving Father shares pain. DeYoung says God’s majesty and the gospel depend on his not suffering, and the Son suffers as a man. This is where the time question and the Father question meet.
