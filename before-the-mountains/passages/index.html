NIV

Which side it helps. Both. “Everlasting to everlasting” reads most naturally as endless time (inside). “In your sight” is a comparison of viewpoint, which outside-time readers use. NLT’s “beginning to end” tilts toward God spanning all of time.

NIV

Which side it helps. “Lives forever” helps inside time (endless duration). “Inhabits eternity” sounds like a realm and helps outside time. The same Hebrew supports both, so the English word chosen carries much of the argument.

Compare the translations
How the versions differ
NIV and NASB: “lives forever.” NLT: “lives in eternity.” ESV, NKJV, KJV, AMP: “inhabits eternity.” The verb shokhen is “dwells,” the same verb as “I dwell in the high and holy place” in the same verse. A formal translation keeps the parallel: God dwells in eternity and dwells in the high place.
The original word
Hebrew shokhen ad. Ad means perpetuity or continuing duration. Many Hebrew scholars take it as “dwells forever.” The older English tradition (KJV) made it a place, “eternity,” and later Christian thought read “eternity” as timelessness. Scholars disagree.

NIV

Which side it helps. Both. Equal weight for a day and a thousand years can mean God’s experience of time is unlike ours (outside-leaning). But the point of the verse is God’s patience over a long wait (inside).

NIV

Which side it helps. Both. “I am” (absolute, present) is the outside-time reading. “I will be what I will be” / “I will be with you” is relational and inside time.

Compare the translations
How the versions differ
All seven give the same main text. NIV/NLT/ESV/AMP use small or varied capitals. NASB and NKJV use full capitals “I AM WHO I AM” for the divine name. Several study Bibles note an alternative “I will be what I will be” in a footnote.
The original word

What the translations do, across all of them

  • One Hebrew root, many English words. Nacham is “repent” (KJV), “relent” (NIV, ESV, NKJV), “change his mind” (NASB, NLT, AMP), “regret” (ESV, NIV), “be sorry” (NLT), and “revoke” (AMP in Amos). A reader of one version sees one picture; a reader of three sees a range. The KJV’s “repent” is the most literal to the root, but in 1611 it did not imply sin.
  • 1 Samuel 15 is the clearest test. Verses 11, 29 and 35 use the same Hebrew root. The ESV and KJV keep one English word and let the tension show. NIV, NASB, NLT and AMP vary the word (“regret” in 11 and 35, “change his mind” in 29), which makes the chapter sound less contradictory than the Hebrew does.
  • Formal versus dynamic on “relent.” The formal versions (KJV, NKJV, NASB, ESV) mostly keep a “relent/repent/change mind” word. NLT, the most dynamic, drops it in Jeremiah 18:8 and 18:10 (“I will not destroy it as I had planned”), so the open-view proof text barely appears there. NLT then adds “eager” in Jonah 4:2 and Joel 2:13, which is not in the Hebrew and makes relenting sound like God’s preference.
  • AMP adds conditions in brackets. Joel 2:13 (“when His people genuinely repent”), Jonah 4:2 (“when sinners turn to You”), Romans 8:29 (“loved and chose beforehand”), Acts 4:28 (“before the creation of the world”). These are not in the Hebrew or Greek, and each one picks a side.
  • ”Eternity” versus “forever.” Olam and ad mean a long or unbounded duration. “Inhabits eternity” (Isaiah 57:15, ESV/NKJV/KJV/AMP) reads as a place beyond time, while “lives forever” (NIV, NASB) reads as endless time. NASB/AMP/NLT in Isaiah 43:13 add “eternity” where the Hebrew says “from the day.”
  • ”Before time began” assumes more than the Greek says. Pro chronōn aiōniōn (Titus 1:2, 2 Timothy 1:9) is “before times of the ages.” NIV and NKJV say “before the beginning of time / before time began,” a claim that time itself had a start. NASB says “long ages ago” in Titus and “from all eternity” in 2 Timothy for the same Greek phrase. The ESV’s “before the ages began” is the most neutral.
  • KJV “time no longer” in Revelation 10:6 supported an end-of-time reading for centuries. Modern versions all say “delay.” This one change removes a proof text for time ending.
  • Foreknowledge nouns become verbs. NLT turns prognōsis (“foreknowledge”) into “God knew ... in advance / knew you long ago” (Acts 2:23, Romans 8:29, 1 Peter 1:2). The ESV and NASB keep the noun. 1 Peter 1:20 shows the same Greek verb as “foreknown” (ESV, NASB), “foreordained” (KJV, NKJV), and “chosen” (NIV, NLT): knowing, deciding, and electing are different doctrines.
  • ”Know” versus “choose.” Hebrew yada in Genesis 18:19 is “chosen” in NIV, ESV, NASB and NLT, but “known” in KJV and NKJV. Because “know” can mean relationship in Hebrew, the choice shapes what “foreknow” means in Romans 8:29.
  • ”I thought” versus “I said.” In Jeremiah 3:7 and 3:19, the NIV, ESV, NLT, NASB (v. 7) and AMP say “I thought she would return,” which implies God’s expectation failed. The KJV and NKJV say “I said,” a command. The Hebrew allows both.
  • Missing conditionals. In 1 Samuel 23:10-13 all seven versions say “he will come down” and “they will surrender you,” though the Hebrew imperfect also allows “would.” No translation chose “would,” so the passage reads as a prediction that did not come true. That favors the open view; the “would” reading is open only to readers who go to the Hebrew.
  • Permission versus decree. NLT’s Lamentations 3:37 (“without the Lord’s permission”) replaces “commanded/decreed” in the other six. That moves the verse from decree to allowance. In Proverbs 16:9, NLT’s “determines” is stronger than “establishes.”
  • Translators add the explanation. Examples: NLT’s “dead father” in Galatians 4:1, NLT’s “forever” in Hebrews 12:9, NIV’s “in history” in Acts 17:26, NIV’s “have to destroy” in Ezekiel 22:30, NLT’s “I want to know” in Genesis 18:21. Each nudges the reader on God and time, knowledge, or fatherhood.
  • ”Before” in KJV versus modern in Psalm 139:4. KJV and NKJV leave “before” out; the other five put it in. Whether God knows a word in advance or only completely is partly a translation choice.
  • The Passion Translation (TPT) is quoted in full because its added words are the thing to compare. TPT is largely one translator’s work, and scholars criticize it for adding phrases that have no basis in the Hebrew or Greek. So a TPT phrase shows how one modern reader hears the verse; it is not evidence of what the original says. TPT has not yet translated Exodus, Numbers, Deuteronomy, 1 Samuel, 2 Kings or Ecclesiastes, and it sometimes joins verses together (for example Psalm 102:26-27).
  • The Russian Synodal Bible (1876) is the standard Russian Protestant and Orthodox text. It numbers most Psalms one lower than English Bibles (Psalm 90 is its Psalm 89) and often counts a Psalm’s title as verse 1, and its Daniel 4 runs three verses behind, so each Synodal line gives its own reference.
  • TPT pulls both ways, harder than any other version. Its added words often settle a question the original leaves open, and they do not all favor one side.
    • Toward God outside time and in full control. Jude 25 says “from before he created time,” the only version here that says God made time. Titus 1:2 and 2 Timothy 1:9 say “before time began.” Isaiah 57:15 has God filling “the eternal realm,” a place more than a span. Psalm 139:16 adds that the days were “planned for me” as well as written. Romans 8:29 turns “foreknew” into “knew all about us before we were born,” knowledge of facts rather than relationship. Ephesians 1:11 adds “Before we were even born, he gave us our destiny.” Proverbs 16:9 says the Lord “chooses the steps,” stronger than “establishes.”
    • Toward God inside time and moved by people. Amos 7:3 says “My prayer moved God’s heart to compassion,” which states as fact what the Hebrew only implies, that the prayer changed the outcome. Jonah 3:10 says God “decided not to punish.” Genesis 6:6 says “his heart was filled with pain.” Joel 2:13 says God “is always ready to forgive and cancel judgment.” Hosea 11:8 says “My heart beats for you.” Genesis 22:12 keeps “now, I know,” and Genesis 18:21 keeps “then I will know.” Malachi 3:6 adds “And you, children of Jacob, you haven’t changed either,” which reads “I do not change” as covenant faithfulness, not timeless sameness.
    • Foreknowledge sometimes disappears. 1 Peter 1:2 drops “foreknowledge” and says only “chosen and destined by Father God.” Lamentations 3:37 says “unless the Lord is willing,” permission language like the NLT’s.

Before the Mountains sets the two answers side by side and lets the texts argue. It does not pick a winner. A companion to A Father Inside Time and its Cross-Examination.
