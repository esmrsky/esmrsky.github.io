A study on the pain of a God who loves us

Hurt for you, more than hurt by you.

As Jesus rode down into Jerusalem, the crowd was shouting his praise. He looked at the city and wept. He wasn’t crying because they had turned him down. They were cheering. He was crying over what was about to happen to them.

This study pulls together three earlier ones, Firsthand, Pursue or Wait and The Thread, around one idea: everything God does is love, and even his pain is love’s pain. It adds what brain research says about different kinds of hurt, and a grace reading from the writer Paul Ellis. Switch translations at the top; tap any underlined reference to read it in place.

1 A correction

God’s tears are mostly not about being snubbed.

Pursue or Wait was built on the line “They have not rejected you, they have rejected me” (1 Samuel 8:7), and one of its headings says simply, “He is hurt.” That is true as far as it goes. But it can leave the wrong picture: a God whose feelings are bruised, who weeps because he was left out.

Read the scenes where God or Jesus is actually in pain, and a different picture comes up again and again. It is a parent watching a child walk toward a cliff. The pain is for the person, not about the parent.

The Bible is honest that he feels the break too. In Ezekiel 6:9 he says their unfaithful hearts have broken him, and Jeremiah 3:20 uses the picture of a wife who walked out. So this study won’t claim God never feels rejected. It claims something smaller and truer: when God hurts, the hurt is aimed at what is happening to us, and it always moves him toward us.

Hurt by you

  • It’s about what you did to me.
  • It wants an apology, or to be chosen again.
  • It pulls back to protect itself.
  • It keeps the score.

Hurt for you

  • It’s about what is happening to you.
  • It wants you safe and whole, even far away.
  • It moves toward you, even when it costs.
  • It keeps the door open.

2 Four feelings

Four kinds of pain that feel alike from the inside.

When someone you love keeps doing what hurts them, you can feel several things at once. They are worth pulling apart, because they pull you in different directions. Researchers who study empathy draw almost the same lines.

Most real moments mix these. A parent can feel all four in one phone call. The point isn’t to feel only the right one. It’s to know which one is driving.

Finish the sentence: “This would stop hurting if…”

Pick the ending that feels most true right now, not the one you think is right.

3 Which one God shows

In Scripture, God’s pain is almost always the third kind.

Go through the passages where God grieves, and compassion is the one that keeps showing up. The other three appear too, but not the way they do in us.

Compassion

Everywhere.

The Gospels keep using one Greek word for what Jesus feels when he sees people, splagchnizomai. It is a gut word, “moved in the inner parts.” It is what he feels for a crowd with no shepherd, for a grieving widow at Nain (Luke 7:13), and what the father feels when he sees his son far off (Luke 15:20).

The Old Testament says the same in its own words. His heart is changed within him; all his compassion is aroused. And then the line that sets him apart from us:

Rejection

Present, but never the last word.

Ezekiel 6:9 and Jeremiah 3:20 speak of a broken bond, and Hosea lives it out in his own marriage. But each time, the next move is pursuit. In Hosea he will “allure her” and “speak tenderly to her” (Hosea 2:14). In Jeremiah it is “Return, faithless people; I will cure you” (Jeremiah 3:22).

Frustration

Rare, and it sounds like a rescuer’s weariness.

“How long?” he asks in Numbers 14:11, and in Jeremiah 15:6 he says he is tired of holding back. Even there, the harm to the people is named close by. His own name for himself is “slow to anger” (Exodus 34:6). Annoyance is quick. This is patient.

Overwhelm

He feels it all, and never turns away.

In Gethsemane Jesus felt the full weight of sorrow. Then he walked toward the cross instead of away. That is the line between empathic distress and compassion, lived out: the pain was real, and it didn’t make him flee.

An honest debateDoes God really feel pain, or is that a way of speaking?

Serious believers disagree. The classical view, held by the early church fathers, Augustine and Thomas Aquinas, says God is not overcome by passions the way we are. The emotion words are true, but they describe his steady love as we experience it. Thomas Weinandy defends this in Does God Suffer? (2000). He adds that the Son truly suffers, as a man.

The other view takes the words more at face value. Abraham Joshua Heschel called it the “divine pathos” in The Prophets (1962): God is not indifferent, and he is moved by what we do. Terence Fretheim’s The Suffering of God (1984) argues that the Old Testament shows God suffering because of his people, for them and with them.

Common groundBoth sides agree Jesus truly wept, and his tears were not an act. Whichever view you hold, the tears show which way his heart faces.

4 How to tell

Seven signs in the text that it’s love’s pain.

  1. He says what he wants for them.

    Life, not payback. Wounded pride wants to be repaid. This wants them to live.

  2. “If only you had known.”

    Jesus says it over Jerusalem (Luke 19:42). God says it through Moses (Deuteronomy 5:29) and Isaiah. It is the wish of someone who can see a danger the other person can’t.

  3. He takes no pleasure in their pain.

    Even when he lets consequences come, it is not what he wants. Ezekiel says it twice (18:23 and 18:32). Lamentations says it from the middle of the ruins.

  4. The anger is aimed at the harm.

    In the synagogue Jesus is angry and grieved at the same moment, at hearts so hard they would leave a man unhealed to win an argument. Then he heals the man.

  5. The pain moves him toward them, at a cost.

    He sees, and he comes down. The father sees, and he runs. Hosea pays to bring his wife home (Hosea 3:2). Jesus goes to the tomb. Hurt pride pulls back. Love goes.

  6. The grief comes before any insult.

    Jesus weeps over Jerusalem during the welcome, not after the rejection. He weeps at Lazarus’s tomb with his grieving friends, when no one has wronged him at all, minutes before he raises Lazarus. Jeremiah 48 and Isaiah 16 even weep over Moab, a foreign people. (Readers differ on whether God or the prophet is speaking there.)

  7. He waits.

    Frustration is quick. This is slow, and the delay is for them.

5 The hard verses

The verses that seem to say the opposite.

A study like this is only honest if it faces the places where God does sound hurt by people, not just for them. Here they are, with what each one says.

Genesis 6:5-6He regretted making people.

The reason is given a few verses later: the earth was “full of violence” (Genesis 6:11). People were hurting each other. There is also a maker’s sorrow here over a creation gone wrong. And verse 8 turns straight to rescue: Noah found favor.

VerdictMostly grief over harm, with a real note of disappointment. The story is about rescue as much as judgment.

Ezekiel 6:9“I was broken.”

This is the strongest “hurt by” verse in the Bible. The Hebrew verb means “I was broken,” and many translations soften it. It is about the relationship, and the text doesn’t explain it away. But notice what broke the bond: idols that, a few verses earlier, are bringing ruin on the land and the people (Ezekiel 6:3-7).

VerdictReal hurt about the relationship. The study shouldn’t pretend otherwise. Even here, what he grieves is also what is destroying them.

Jeremiah 3:19-20Like an unfaithful wife.

Betrayal language, but look at what he had hoped for them in the verse just before: a father’s inheritance, “the most beautiful inheritance of any nation.” And the next word is an invitation: “Return, faithless people; I will cure you” (Jeremiah 3:22).

VerdictBoth. The hope was for their good, and the hurt turns into a call home.

Psalm 78:38-41They grieved him in the wilderness.

The verbs of rebellion and provoking sound like an affront. But the psalm wraps them in mercy: he forgave, he held back his anger, he remembered they were only flesh.

VerdictBoth, with compassion on either side.

Ephesians 4:30-32Do not grieve the Holy Spirit.

Read the next verse. Bitterness, rage, brawling, slander, malice: the things that grieve the Spirit are ways we hurt each other. The verses before it are about lying, stealing and words that tear down.

VerdictGrief over what we do to one another. It is the clearest New Testament case for this study.

Isaiah 48:9-11 and Ezekiel 36:22-23“For my name’s sake.”

Some passages say God acts for the sake of his own name, and Ezekiel 36:22 even says “not for your sake.” Scripture doesn’t hide that God cares how he is known. What it argues is that people knowing him as he really is serves them. For him, his honor and their good are not opposed.

VerdictReal, and not ego. Don’t claim the Bible never speaks of his name.

Exodus 34:14 and 2 Corinthians 11:2A jealous God.

Covenant jealousy isn’t envy. Envy begrudges someone else’s good. This jealousy won’t share the beloved with what is ruining them, and the prophets keep saying the idols are ruining them (Jeremiah 2:13). Paul shows which way it faces: jealous for them, not of them.

VerdictA demand for loyalty that protects the one who is loved. The text doesn’t always spell that out, so hold it as a reading, not a proof.

Exodus 34:6-7And his anger?

When God describes himself to Moses, the mercy words come first and take up most of the line. Anger is described by its delay. The Bible quotes this description again and again (Numbers 14:18, Psalm 103:8, Joel 2:13, Jonah 4:2). Psalm 30:5 adds that his anger lasts only a moment, but his favor lasts a lifetime. The end of verse 7 matters too: he doesn’t pretend harm is fine. Love that shrugged at what is destroying you wouldn’t be love.

VerdictAnger is real, slow, and aimed at what destroys people.

Scripture rarely shows God in pain because he was snubbed. When it does show hurt over the relationship, it also shows what he does next, and it is nearly always to go toward the people at a cost to himself. The pain and the pursuit travel together.The summary of this section

6 A grace reading

How Paul Ellis reads these passages.

Paul Ellis is a New Zealand writer and former pastor who teaches what is often called the grace message. His website is called Escape to Reality, and his best-known books are The Gospel in Ten Words (2012) and The Hyper-Grace Gospel (2014).

His method is to read every hard verse through two lenses. The cross finished the old “do bad, get bad” arrangement, and Jesus shows exactly what the Father is like (John 14:9). That makes him a strong voice for this study’s main idea, and it is also where his critics push back.

Readings that fit this study

  • Ephesians 4:30. God really is grieved by sin, Ellis says, but not because we let him down or broke a rule. In his words, Our sins grieve him because they hurt his kids. That is this study in one sentence. Read the article.
  • Why behavior matters. Your behavior matters to God because you matter to God. He cares about the outcome because of who you are to him, not because he is keeping score.
  • Jerusalem. Jesus wept because they would not listen, and Matthew 23:37 shows him longing to gather them. Ellis calls him a gatherer, not an executioner. Read the article.
  • Wrath. He reads God’s anger as aimed at what keeps people from grace, not at the people. God is for sinners.
  • Correction, not condemnation. The Spirit steers us away from harm because God doesn’t want us to wreck our lives, not to punish us.
  • Romans 11:1. “Did God reject his people? By no means!” People walk away; he doesn’t shut the door.

Where to hold him loosely

  • “God is not jealous.” Ellis rejects the word. Most scholars read the Bible’s jealousy as covenant love that won’t share the beloved. His real point, that God is not insecure or controlling, stands without denying the word.
  • Hebrews 12:6. He argues the word “scourges” doesn’t belong there. But the verse quotes Proverbs 3:12, and the Greek Old Testament the writer quoted already has that word. Most readers say God does train his children through hardship, as a father, not a punisher.
  • 1 John 1:9. He reads it as written to unbelievers. Most commentators read it as written to believers.
  • Repentance. He defines it as a change of mind about God. That is the word’s core meaning; most scholars add that the change shows in how you live (Matthew 3:8).
  • The critics. Michael Brown’s Hyper-Grace (2014) argues this message plays down repentance and holiness, and Ellis answered him in The Hyper-Grace Gospel. Whether a believer can be lost is also debated between Christian traditions. Ellis treats it as settled; many readers won’t.

8 For us

When you love someone who keeps hurting themselves.

Everything above is also a map for us. God gives his people his own heart, and that heart will hurt (Romans 12:15). The question is which hurt is driving.

Name what you feel.

Use the four above. Rejection and frustration aren’t sins to hide; they are signs of what you were hoping for. Overwhelm isn’t a failure either. It is often a sign you are carrying something alone.

Move from overwhelm toward compassion.

In small studies, people trained in compassion, meaning warmth and good wishes toward the one suffering, felt less distress and more warmth than people trained in empathy alone (Klimecki and colleagues, 2014). The research is early, but it matches Gethsemane: Jesus felt it all and kept moving toward people.

Name the grief that can’t finish.

The family therapist Pauline Boss calls it ambiguous loss: someone is here, but not really here. You can’t grieve them like a death, and you can’t stop grieving either. Just having a name for it helps many families (Boss, Family Process, 2002).

If it’s addiction, there is help with evidence behind it.

The best-studied help for families is CRAFT, Community Reinforcement and Family Training. A review of four randomized trials with 264 family members found it got about three times as many resistant loved ones into treatment as Al-Anon-style support (Roozen, de Waart and van der Kroft, Addiction, 2010). You are far from alone: researchers estimate around 100 million family members worldwide live with a relative’s addiction (Orford and colleagues, 2013, an estimate).

Carry what is yours, and give him the rest.

Galatians 6 says “carry each other’s burdens” (6:2) and three verses later, “each one should carry their own load” (6:5). Pursue, but never force. What you can’t carry, hand to the one who already hurts for them more than you do.

Remember who is doing the pursuing.

You are not their savior. You are someone loved by theirs. His heart for them is not tired, and it is not offended. It is still on the road.

This is not a call to stay where you are being hurt. If someone is harming you, getting safe comes first, and loving them from a distance is still love. If someone you love may be in danger of suicide, in the US you can call or text 988, any time.

The last word

He is not nursing a wound. He is singing over you.

The God of the Bible is not a ruler with a bruised ego. He is a shepherd in the hills, a father on the road, a Savior weeping over a city that cheered him and didn’t understand. His pain is real, and it is for you. It has never once made him turn away.
