Salvation

What actually happens when you believe.

Salvation is a rescue, and it was finished before you got there. Every sin was paid for. The door is open. The only thing that keeps anyone out is not walking through. Walking through is called believing. Not agreeing. Trusting. When you believe, God doesn’t improve you. He gives you a new birth, a new standing and a new name: child. From that day he sees you in his Son. That isn’t a feeling. It’s where you stand. You’ll still stumble. The standing doesn’t move. That’s what grace is.

This page shows the verses, says what we take them to mean, and marks every place where Christians read them differently. You’ll be able to tell the three apart the whole way down.

01

What is salvation?

The Greek word is sōzō: to rescue, to make safe, to heal. It’s the word for pulling someone out of the sea.

The name Jesus is the Hebrew Yeshua, a short form of Yehoshua: “the LORD saves.” The angel said what from: She will give birth to a son, and you are to give him the name Jesus, because he will save his people from their sins.” Matthew 1:21 Salvation isn’t a status upgrade, or a ticket. It’s being pulled out of something you couldn’t get out of, by someone who could.

Everyone needs it: for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God, Romans 3:23

From the beginningTextReadingBefore anything went wrong, and the first thing he did after.
The text

When people argued with Jesus about the law, he took them back to the start: it was not this way from the beginning (Matthew 19:8). So start there.

The first thing God does to the people he has made is bless them (Genesis 1:28), and he calls all of it very good (Genesis 1:31). His one command is a warning for their sake: eat from that tree and you will die (Genesis 2:17).

When they fall, he comes looking. But the Lord God called to the man, “Where are you?” Genesis 3:9 They hide because they are afraid (Genesis 3:10). The fear is new. It came in with the fall. It didn’t come from him.

He curses the serpent, and he curses the ground (Genesis 3:14, 17). He never says “cursed are you” to Adam or to Eve. Then he makes them clothes out of skins and dresses them himself (Genesis 3:21). He guards the way to the tree of life, so that they won’t eat from it and live forever (Genesis 3:22-24).

With Cain he talks first, warning him before the murder (Genesis 4:6-7). Afterward he puts a mark on him so that no one will kill him (Genesis 4:15).

And the rescue was never a reaction. The Lamb was chosen before the world was made (1 Peter 1:20; Revelation 13:8).

Our reading

The beginning shows the heart. God blesses first. He goes looking for the one who is hiding. He asks questions where he could have made accusations. He covers shame, and he protects even the guilty. Even shutting the garden protects them: no one should live forever inside the fall. Fear of punishment came in with sin, and love is what drives it out (1 John 4:18).

Jesus is that heart with a face: Jesus answered: “Don’t you know me, Philip, even after I have been among you such a long time? Anyone who has seen me has seen the Father. How can you say, ‘Show us the Father’? John 14:9 He is the exact image of the Father’s being (Hebrews 1:3), and he did only what he saw the Father doing (John 5:19).

So this page reads every hard verse the way Jesus read the law: from the beginning, and through him. When a verse seems to show a different God from the one Jesus showed, we assume we are reading it wrong. The Father hasn’t changed (James 1:17; Hebrews 13:8).

Three tenses of one rescueTextReadingSaved, being saved, will be saved. Most of the worry lives in mixing them up.
The text

You have been saved: For it is by grace you have been saved, through faith—and this is not from yourselves, it is the gift of God— Ephesians 2:8 In Greek that’s a perfect tense: done, and still standing.

You are being saved: For the message of the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God. 1 Corinthians 1:18 Where Paul says “saved” there, the Greek is a present participle: “us who are being saved.”

Our reading

The past tense is the one that answers “am I saved?” The present tense is you being renewed, which is unfinished in everyone. The future tense is the day he finishes it, body and all. Most people who doubt their salvation are looking at the middle tense and asking it a question only the first one can answer.

One way to picture the three tenses is spirit, soul and body. That’s a reading, set out in question 07.

The text

Paul’s summary of what he preached first: For what I received I passed on to you as of first importance: that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures, 1 Corinthians 15:3-4

Without the second half, he says, faith is futile and you are still in your sins (1 Corinthians 15:17). Jesus was raised so that we could be declared right with God (Romans 4:25). So “believe what?” has a short answer: that he died for your sins and was raised. It’s a person to trust, not a theory.

Was it instant?TextDisputedReadingA thief with no time, no baptism, and one sentence.
The text

A dying thief asked for one thing: Then he said, “Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom.” Luke 23:42 The answer came the same day: Jesus answered him, “Truly I tell you, today you will be with me in paradise.” Luke 23:43 No baptism, no works, no time to ask twice.

At Cornelius’s house the Spirit arrived before the water did: While Peter was still speaking these words, the Holy Spirit came on all who heard the message. Acts 10:44 The baptizing is four verses later (Acts 10:47-48).

Where Christians differ
  • Baptism is where the new birth happens. Jesus ties new birth to water and the Spirit (John 3:5), and puts believing and being baptized side by side (Mark 16:16). Peter tells the crowd to repent and be baptized for the forgiveness of their sins (Acts 2:38). Paul is told to be baptized and have his sins washed away (Acts 22:16). Paul says believers were buried with Christ in baptism (Romans 6:3-4), clothed with him (Galatians 3:27) and saved through the washing of rebirth (Titus 3:5), and Peter says baptism now saves (1 Peter 3:21). Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran and many Anglican churches hold this, and so do the Churches of Christ: most Christians in history. The thief, they note, died before Christian baptism was given (Matthew 28:19).
  • Faith is where it happens, and baptism is its public sign. Saved through faith, not by works (Ephesians 2:8-9), and the thief went to paradise that day. Paul says Christ didn’t send him to baptize but to preach (1 Corinthians 1:17), which would be strange if baptism were the moment of rebirth. Peter explains his own verse: baptism saves not by washing the body but as a clear conscience pledged to God (1 Peter 3:21). And Mark 16:16 condemns only for not believing.
Our reading

We hold the second. The new birth happens when you believe, and baptism is its commanded, public witness. Both sides agree baptism isn’t optional for a believer. The question is only whether it saves, or shows.

Water is where faith goes public, not a second thing to trust. Acts keeps the two so close that it knows nothing of a believer who simply declines.

03

Is it really not by works?

Yes. And the reason matters: if you could earn it, it would be wages, and wages can be docked.

Paul puts it bluntly: However, to the one who does not work but trusts God who justifies the ungodly, their faith is credited as righteousness. Romans 4:5

But “believe” is where people slip. The word is pisteuō: to trust, to rely on, to lean your weight on. You believe that there is one God. Good! Even the demons believe that—and shudder. James 2:19 Agreeing that God exists isn’t the door. Trusting him is.

So how do I walk through?You tell him. There are no set words. The thief managed one sentence, and so did the tax collector. Tell him you’re done trusting yourself, and you’re trusting him.for, “Everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved.” Romans 10:13If you just did, the list in question 02 is about you.

What trust looks likeTextReadingTrust is when you stop checking.
The text

A Roman officer sent word rather than come himself: The centurion replied, “Lord, I do not deserve to have you come under my roof. But just say the word, and my servant will be healed. Matthew 8:8 Then he went home, and the servant was well (Matthew 8:13).

If you declare with your mouth, “Jesus is Lord,” and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved. Romans 10:9

Our reading

Believing, in the New Testament’s sense, is resting your whole weight on something that’s already true. You sit in a chair without testing it first. That’s the posture.

It doesn’t need to be loud, and it doesn’t need to feel strong. A weak grip on a strong rope still holds you up.

It doesn’t need many words either. The prophets of Baal shouted from morning until evening (1 Kings 18:26-29). Elijah prayed two sentences (18:36-37). Jesus says the pagans think they’ll be heard for their many words, and your Father knows what you need before you ask (Matthew 6:7-8).

Believing, in John, is always a verbTextReadingHe never uses the noun “faith.” He uses “believe” about a hundred times.
The text

John wrote his Gospel so that people would believe (John 20:31). Yet the noun pistis, faith, never appears in it. The verb pisteuō appears about a hundred times, and over thirty of those are “believe into him,” pisteuō eis: the way you move your weight onto something.

He also shows believing that isn’t trust yet. In Jerusalem, many believed in his name when they saw his signs (John 2:23). Then: But Jesus would not entrust himself to them, for he knew all people. John 2:24 In Greek it’s the same verb both times. They believed in him, and he didn’t entrust himself to them.

It happens again in John 8. A crowd that believed him at verse 30 is picking up stones by verse 59.

Luke has Jesus tell it as a parable. Some seed lands on rock: Those on the rocky ground are the ones who receive the word with joy when they hear it, but they have no root. They believe for a while, but in the time of testing they fall away. Luke 8:13

Our reading

John isn’t describing two strengths of faith. He’s describing two objects. The crowd in Jerusalem trusted what he could do. The people he calls his own trust who he is. Jesus’ picture for the difference is a root, not a quantity.

Being impressed by Jesus isn’t the same as leaning on him. You can admire a bridge from the bank. Believing is walking out onto it.

That should settle a fear, not start one. The question was never whether your belief is strong enough. It’s whether it’s resting on him.

Trying to be a better personTextReadingNicodemus was told he needed to be born. Works are made. Fruit grows.
The text

Nicodemus was a Pharisee, a member of the ruling council, and in Jesus’ own words Israel’s teacher (John 3:10). If anyone could have improved his way in, he could. Jesus didn’t offer him a better record: Jesus replied, “Very truly I tell you, no one can see the kingdom of God unless they are born again.” John 3:3

Paul had the best religious record of his generation, and he lists it (Philippians 3:4-6). Then he writes it off: But whatever were gains to me I now consider loss for the sake of Christ. Philippians 3:7 Rules, he says, can’t even hold back what they’re aimed at (Colossians 2:23).

Galatians 5 sets two lists side by side and names them differently. The first is the erga of the flesh, its works (Galatians 5:19). The second is the karpos of the Spirit, its fruit (Galatians 5:22). Works is plural. Fruit is singular. One list is produced. The other grows, and Jesus said from where: “I am the vine; you are the branches. If you remain in me and I in you, you will bear much fruit; apart from me you can do nothing. John 15:5

Our reading

Wanting to be a better person isn’t wrong. It’s the wrong door. Self-improvement renovates a house. The gospel is a birth, and you can’t renovate your way into being born.

Works are what you produce to become something. Fruit is what shows up because you already are something. An apple tree doesn’t strain to be an apple tree. You could staple apples to a fence post, and it would still be a fence post.

So the order flips. You’re made new first, and the better person grows out of that. The fruit is real, and people can see it. But it’s the Spirit’s, it takes seasons (Mark 4:28), and it grows from staying attached, not from effort aimed at the fruit. It’s the same arrow as become, belong, behave, pointed the right way. “Fallen from grace”TextDisputedReadingPaul said it about the people doing more, not less. And he kept having to say it.
The text

In English, someone “falls from grace” when they’re caught in a scandal. Paul used the phrase once, and he meant nearly the opposite: You who are trying to be justified by the law have been alienated from Christ; you have fallen away from grace. Galatians 5:4

The Galatians hadn’t drifted into immorality. They were adding circumcision to faith, to be sure (Galatians 5:2-3). Paul’s alarm was about the ones being most careful. Just before, he told them: It is for freedom that Christ has set us free. Stand firm, then, and do not let yourselves be burdened again by a yoke of slavery. Galatians 5:1

And he had already asked the question the whole letter turns on: I would like to learn just one thing from you: Did you receive the Spirit by the works of the law, or by believing what you heard? Galatians 3:2

It wasn’t only Galatia. Almost every drift in the letters went the same way:

  • Galatia. Circumcision added to faith. Paul calls them foolish, as if someone had put them under a spell (Galatians 3:1).
  • Antioch. Peter himself pulled back from eating with Gentile believers, and Paul opposed him to his face (Galatians 2:11-14).
  • Colossae. Food laws, festivals, Sabbaths and a list of don’ts (Colossians 2:16-23).
  • Philippi. Confidence in circumcision, and Paul’s own record written off as loss (Philippians 3:2-9).
  • Rome. A whole letter built on the answer: justified freely, apart from the law (Romans 3:24-28; 4:4-5).
  • Ephesus. Teachers who forbade marriage and ordered people off certain foods (1 Timothy 4:1-3).
  • Corinth drifted the other way, using freedom as cover for sleeping with prostitutes. Paul answered with who they were, not with a rule (1 Corinthians 6:15-20).
Where Christians differ
  • Lost. “Alienated from Christ” means what it says: a believer who turns to the law for their standing can be cut off. Notice that even on this reading, what cuts them off is adding works, not sinning.
  • Never had it. Anyone who finally trusts the law instead of Christ shows they were never trusting Christ.
  • Off the ground, not out of the family. They stepped off grace as the ground they stood on, onto law. They lost the freedom, not the sonship. Paul still calls them brothers and sisters, and says he’s confident they’ll come around (Galatians 5:10). Paul Ellis on Galatians 5:4
Our reading

We hold the third. But all three readings agree on which way the fall goes. The danger Galatians names isn’t sinning too much. It’s trusting something added to Christ.

That’s why working for it and believing are opposites, not partners. Paul puts them on two sides of one sentence: Now to the one who works, wages are not credited as a gift but as an obligation. Romans 4:4 Israel, he says, stumbled because it pursued righteousness by works instead of by faith (Romans 9:32).

So if you’re working to be accepted, you aren’t trusting yet. You’re trusting yourself, and the work is the tell. But the same good deed done because you’re already accepted is something else entirely. Paul follows “not by works” with this, in the next breath: For we are God’s handiwork, created in Christ Jesus to do good works, which God prepared in advance for us to do. Ephesians 2:10 Not by works. For works. The deed can look identical. What it’s for is the whole difference. One Shape calls it the same sweat with an opposite engine.

Paul treated the drift toward rules as the emergency. He called it foolish and bewitched, and said it would mean Christ died for nothing (Galatians 2:21). The unbelief he fought hardest didn’t look like atheism. It looked like religion that wouldn’t stop working.

The fear that grace makes people careless is real, and Paul answers it (question 08). But look at how he answered the one church that did get careless. Not with more law. With identity: you were bought, you’re his, you’re one spirit with him.

How the Father disciplinesTextDisputedReadingTraining, not payment. His eye first, and his word.
The text

The Hebrew word for the LORD’s discipline, musar, is the same word Proverbs uses for a father’s instruction (Proverbs 1:8; 3:11). One psalm puts them in a single line: the one you discipline is the one you teach from your law (Psalm 94:12).

The Greek word, paideia, comes from pais, a child. It means raising a child. Scripture is given for it (2 Timothy 3:16), and fathers bring up their children in it (Ephesians 6:4). Paul even says who does the training. Where grace “teaches” us to say no to ungodliness, the verb is paideuō: grace disciplines (Titus 2:11-12).

God describes his own way: I will instruct you and teach you in the way you should go; I will counsel you with my loving eye on you. Psalm 32:8 In the next line he says not to be like the mule, which has to be led with bit and bridle (Psalm 32:9).

In the wilderness he let Israel go hungry, then fed them manna, to teach them that people live on every word that comes from his mouth. Moses calls that discipline, the kind a father gives a son (Deuteronomy 8:2-5). Jesus answered the devil with that same verse (Matthew 4:4).

Hosea shows a Father teaching a toddler to walk, holding him by the arms, lifting him to his cheek, bending down to feed him (Hosea 11:3-4). Hebrews says what discipline is for: our good, so that we share his holiness (Hebrews 12:10). And in John 15 the Father prunes the fruitful branch, and the next verse says you are already clean because of his word. The Greek words for prune and clean come from the same root (John 15:2-3).

When Elijah wanted to die, God’s first answer was bread and sleep, twice. Only then came a question, “What are you doing here, Elijah?”, and a gentle whisper (1 Kings 19:4-13). Spirit · Soul · Body and The Thread both tell this story.

How Jesus corrected his own:

  • Peter, after the third denial: a look, and a remembered word. The Lord turned and looked straight at Peter. Then Peter remembered the word the Lord had spoken to him: “Before the rooster crows today, you will disown me three times.” Luke 22:61
  • Peter, after the resurrection: three questions over breakfast (John 21:15-17).
  • Peter, speaking for Satan: a sharp rebuke (Matthew 16:23).
  • James and John, who wanted fire on a village: a rebuke, and they walked on to the next village (Luke 9:54-56).
  • Martha, anxious and upset: a gentle word, her name said twice (Luke 10:41-42).
  • Thomas, doubting: an invitation to touch his wounds (John 20:27).
  • The twelve, arguing about who was greatest (Luke 22:24): he served them, and washed their feet (John 13:4-15).
Where Christians differ
  • Discipline includes hardship God sends. Hebrews calls discipline painful and says he chastens every son he receives (Hebrews 12:6, 11). A psalm says the LORD chastened me severely (Psalm 118:18), and another thanks God for affliction that taught his decrees (Psalm 119:67, 71). Jesus says he rebukes and disciplines those he loves (Revelation 3:19), and Paul calls sickness at Corinth discipline (1 Corinthians 11:30-32). The Greek verb stretches that far: Pilate uses it for having Jesus flogged (Luke 23:16). Most traditions read these as God bringing hard things on his children to correct them.
  • Discipline is training, and his tools are his word, his presence and his Spirit. Hardship comes from a fallen world, from what we sow and from the enemy. God works in it for good (Romans 8:28) without sending it. What hurts is the correction itself: hearing the truth about yourself from someone who loves you. Paul Ellis on Hebrews 12:6
Our reading

We hold the second. Discipline is training, not payment. The payment was made at the cross (Isaiah 53:5), and a Father doesn’t collect twice.

His first way is the one he names: counsel, with his eye on you. That is how Jesus corrected Peter at his lowest: one look, and Peter remembered the word. The bit and bridle is for the mule that won’t come near, and even then it is to bring it close.

In the one place in his letters to the churches where Jesus names his discipline, it comes as counsel and a knock. Those I love I rebuke and discipline, he tells Laodicea. The verse before is: I counsel you (Revelation 3:18). The verse after is: I stand at the door and knock (3:20).

Sin still costs. You reap what you sow (Galatians 6:7). That is the harvest of the seed, not the Father’s hand. And even while you grieve the Spirit, you are sealed (Ephesians 4:30).

A child who breaks a window isn’t un-adopted. His father sits him down and teaches him. Whether God ever uses sickness is the next drawer. The difference between judging and correcting is what Judgment is about.

The text

Peter summed up Jesus’ ministry for a Roman officer: how God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and power, and how he went around doing good and healing all who were under the power of the devil, because God was with him. Acts 10:38

In the Gospels he heals all who come to him (Matthew 8:16; 12:15; Luke 6:19). He names what had kept one woman bound for eighteen years, and it is Satan (Luke 13:16). He never makes anyone sick. The only thing he ever withered was a fig tree (Mark 11:13-14, 20-21).

When James and John wanted to call fire down on a village, the way Elijah once did (2 Kings 1:10-12), he turned and rebuked them (Luke 9:54-55). Some manuscripts add his reason: the Son of Man did not come to destroy people’s lives, but to save them (Luke 9:56).

He said where his works came from: Jesus gave them this answer: “Very truly I tell you, the Son can do nothing by himself; he can do only what he sees his Father doing, because whatever the Father does the Son also does. John 5:19

Right after the Red Sea, God gives Israel a name for himself: the LORD who heals you. The same sentence calls the diseases of Egypt his doing (Exodus 15:26).

Sickness is on the law’s list of curses (Deuteronomy 28:21-22, 27-28, 59-61). Christ redeemed us from that curse by becoming a curse for us (Galatians 3:13). Matthew says his healings fulfilled Isaiah’s word that he would carry our sicknesses (Matthew 8:17; Isaiah 53:4-5).

And the other texts, in full. Miriam turned leprous when the LORD’s anger burned against her (Numbers 12:9-10). The LORD struck David’s child (2 Samuel 12:15). King Uzziah was struck with leprosy (2 Chronicles 26:19-20). In Acts an angel of the Lord struck Herod down (Acts 12:23), and Paul told a sorcerer that the hand of the Lord was against him (Acts 13:11). In Revelation, Jesus himself says he gave a false teacher time to repent, and because she would not, he will put her on a bed of suffering and strike her children dead (Revelation 2:21-23).

Where Christians differ
  • God sends sickness to correct and to judge. The texts above say so plainly, and God calls himself the one who wounds and heals (Deuteronomy 32:39). Jesus speaks the hardest one himself (Revelation 2:22-23). On this reading God rules over sickness as over everything, and sometimes uses it on his own children (1 Corinthians 11:30-32). This has been the majority view through most of church history.
  • God allows; the destroyer destroys. Scripture sometimes names God and another agent for the same act. The LORD incited David to count the people (2 Samuel 24:1); the retelling in Chronicles says Satan did (1 Chronicles 21:1). Satan strikes Job with sores (Job 2:7), and the book still calls it trouble the LORD brought on him (Job 42:11). At the Passover the LORD sees the blood and won’t let the destroyer in (Exodus 12:23), and Paul says the grumblers in the wilderness were killed by the destroying angel (1 Corinthians 10:10). On this reading, “the LORD struck” often means he stepped back from people who had pushed him away, and let the destroyer in.
  • Christ carried it. Sickness belongs to the curse Jesus became, and God does not lay back on his children what his Son carried. The Old Testament is read through Jesus, the exact image of the Father (Hebrews 1:1-3), who refused Elijah’s fire. Paul Ellis argues this through the hard cases, Job included. Paul Ellis on Job 2:6
Our reading

We hold the third, and read the hard texts with the second. God does not put sickness on his children. Jesus is what the Father is like, and he only did what he saw the Father doing. If the Father were the one making people sick, the Son spent his whole ministry undoing his Father’s work. Jesus said a house divided against itself can’t stand (Matthew 12:25-26). He said it about Satan. It holds from the other side too.

God does not afflict from his heart. That is the Hebrew of Lamentations 3:33, written in the rubble of Jerusalem. He takes no pleasure in anyone’s death (Ezekiel 33:11). James warns people not to say God is tempting them, then says every good gift comes from the Father, who doesn’t change like shifting shadows (James 1:13-17).

The hard texts stay on the page. Revelation 2 is Jesus speaking, and it has its own drawer: who Jezebel was, who her children were, and who was left alone (below). We hold our reading with that text in view, not by leaving it out.

Whatever the cause, the New Testament’s instruction for a sick believer is the same: call for prayer, and expect healing and forgiveness together (James 5:14-16). Not everyone was healed at once. Epaphroditus nearly died before God had mercy on him (Philippians 2:27), and Paul’s thorn, which he calls a messenger of Satan, stayed until he learned that grace was enough (2 Corinthians 12:7-9). But the instruction didn’t change.

Faith and doctors are on the same side. Paul told Timothy to take some wine for his stomach (1 Timothy 5:23), and Luke, who wrote a Gospel and Acts, was a doctor (Colossians 4:14). How much healing comes now and how much waits for the resurrection is honestly debated (Romans 8:23). And if healing hasn’t come, that is not a verdict on your faith. There is no condemnation (Romans 8:1). The Thread makes the same point.

“That is why many among you are weak and sick”TextDisputedReadingNot discerning the body. Which body, who did the judging, and why the answer is to come and eat.
The text

The setting first. When the Corinthians met for the Lord’s Supper, those who had plenty ate first. The poor went hungry, some got drunk, and Paul asks whether they despise the church by humiliating those who have nothing (1 Corinthians 11:20-22).

He repeats the words of the meal: this is my body, which is for you (11:23-26). Then he warns against eating in an unworthy manner (11:27). In Greek that is an adverb, anaxiōs. It describes how they were eating, not who they were. Then: let each person examine themselves, and so let them eat (11:28). The point of looking is to come to the table.

For those who eat and drink without discerning the body of Christ eat and drink judgment on themselves. 1 Corinthians 11:29 “On themselves” is heautō: they eat the judgment into themselves.

The oldest manuscripts say only “the body.” Later ones add “of the Lord,” and the NIV adds “of Christ.” The verb is diakrinō, to tell apart, to see a thing for what it is. Two verses later Paul uses it again: if we discerned ourselves, we would not be judged (11:31). “The body” and “ourselves” take the same verb, and in the next chapter he says it outright: you are the body of Christ (12:27). In four verses he uses four forms of one root, discern, judgment, judged and condemned (11:29-32). Judgment traces that family.

Then: many among you are weak and sick, and a number are sleeping (11:30), Paul’s word for believers who have died (1 Corinthians 15:6, 18). And: when we are judged, we are disciplined, so that we will not be condemned with the world (11:32). In Greek “by the Lord” sits between the two verbs and can go with either. The King James takes it with the second: when we are judged, we are chastened of the Lord.

His remedy is one line: when you gather to eat, wait for one another (11:33-34). The verb, ekdechomai, means to wait for, or to welcome.

The frame on both sides: the one loaf makes them one body (10:16-17), and the body with many parts comes next (12:12-27). A chapter earlier he names who killed the grumblers in the wilderness: the destroying angel (10:10).

Where Christians differ
  • The Lord’s body in the bread. They ate the holy meal as if it were ordinary food, and God sent sickness and death to correct them. This is the older reading, and the Catholic, Orthodox and Lutheran one, tied to their teaching on the sacrament.
  • The church as his body. They failed to recognize Christ’s body in each other, and left the poor hungry at his own table. Many recent commentators read it this way, because the passage is about how they treated each other, and chapters 10 and 12 frame it with the one body.
  • The body broken for your healing. His body was given for you (11:24), and by his wounds you are healed (Isaiah 53:5; 1 Peter 2:24). Those who eat without recognizing what the body carries miss what it gives, and stay weak and sick. Joseph Prince teaches it in Health and Wholeness Through the Holy Communion. Paul Ellis reads “unworthy manner” as eating without valuing what the cross did, and the Lord’s part as training, not punishment. Paul Ellis on 11:26-32 Paul Ellis on 11:31-32
Our reading

We hold the second and third as one reading. The body Paul means is the body given for you and the body you belong to. Christ’s body was broken for your wholeness, and it is gathered as your brothers and sisters. Corinth missed both at once: they ate his body while they shamed his body.

Look where the judgment comes from in Paul’s own words. They eat it on themselves (11:29). If they discerned themselves, it wouldn’t come (11:31). And the Lord’s part, in the King James order, is the training (11:32): he turns what they brought on themselves into a lesson, so that they won’t be condemned with the world.

So the cure isn’t a harder look inward. It’s a truer look at the body: take the bread as his body given for your healing, and eat it together, with the poor fed and everyone welcomed. Communion and healing belong together.

The Passover draws the same line. The LORD guards the door with the blood on it, and the destroyer is what stays outside (Exodus 12:23). A church that despised the body had stepped out from under the protection the body gives. Those who fell asleep went home early. They were not lost (1 Thessalonians 4:13-14).

And no one is too unworthy to come. The warning was about the manner of eating, and the command that follows the self-examination is: and so eat.

The letter to Thyatira comes from the Son of God, whose eyes are like blazing fire (Revelation 2:18). It praises the church first: their love, faith, service and perseverance are growing (2:19). She named herself a prophet. Her namesake is Ahab’s queen, who brought Baal worship to Israel and killed the LORD’s prophets (1 Kings 16:31; 18:4). Then: I have given her time to repent of her immorality, but she is unwilling. Revelation 2:21

What follows sorts people into four groups. She is thrown onto a bed. Those who commit adultery with her will suffer greatly, unless they repent of her ways. Her children are struck dead. And the rest in Thyatira, who don’t hold her teaching, get no other burden, only this: hold on to what you have (2:22-25).

Three phrases have a meaning in Scripture’s own usage. “Thrown on a bed” is how Matthew describes the sick: the servant lying paralyzed, Peter’s mother-in-law lying with fever, and the paralytic on his bed are all, in Greek, “thrown” (Matthew 8:6, 14; 9:2). “Kill with death” is the phrase Revelation uses a few chapters later for plague (Revelation 6:8). And “children” is John’s word for followers (2 John 1, 4; 3 John 4).

The reason given: so all the churches will know that he searches hearts and minds, and repays each one by their deeds (2:23; Jeremiah 17:10).

Where Christians differ
  • Jesus himself brings sickness and death on the unrepentant. Read plainly, he says “I” four times: I gave her time, I am throwing her, I will strike, I will repay. On this reading the Lord of the church judges evil inside the church, and those who follow it share its end.
  • Revelation’s pictures. The book speaks in symbols. The couch of her feasts and her adultery becomes her sickbed, and the teaching that promised freedom ends in death. The letter shows where her way leads, and that Jesus sees all of it.
  • Not God’s children. Jezebel is a false prophet, not a believer being disciplined, and “her children” are what her teaching produced, not the Christians she misled. On this reading Jesus rescues the believers she seduced. He doesn’t kill them. Paul Ellis on Revelation 2:18-29 Paul Ellis on Revelation 2:23
Our reading

We hold the third, with the second. Nothing here is a Father correcting a child who is trying. It is a self-appointed prophet who had been given time and refused it, leading his servants into sin. Her children are her own following, what her teaching produced. They aren’t infants, and they aren’t the believers she misled: those are called to repent (2:22), and the rest are left alone (2:24).

Even the threat has a door in it: unless they repent (2:22). Jesus’ weapon in these letters is the sword of his mouth, his word (2:16; 1:16). His blazing eyes are the eyes that see what’s hidden (2:18, 23), the same eye that counsels in Psalm 32:8.

Scripture’s pattern for judging evil that people insist on is handing over. God gives people over to what they want (Romans 1:24, 26, 28), and Paul hands a man over so that his spirit will be saved in the end (1 Corinthians 5:5). We read the bed the same way: sickbed and death are where her own teaching was leading, and the one who searches hearts lets it arrive, after holding it back for a long time.

Jesus does say “I,” and we don’t soften it. We read it as the Lord of the church judging evil held onto, never as a Father disciplining a child. And for the rest in Thyatira, the only burden is to hold on (2:24-25).

What is repentance?

The Greek is metanoia, a change of mind. The Hebrew is shuv (שׁוּב), to turn around, to come home.

Neither word means groveling, and neither means paying something back. Our reading: repentance is the turn itself, from trusting yourself to trusting him.

And Paul says what produces it: Or do you show contempt for the riches of his kindness, forbearance and patience, not realizing that God’s kindness is intended to lead you to repentance? Romans 2:4 Not his threats. His kindness.

Do I have to keep asking for forgiveness?

The forgiveness that saves you was given once, for everything, at the cross. Past sins and future ones.

Telling God the truth about your sin afterward is honesty between a child and a Father. It isn’t a fresh application for a pardon you already have.

What about the hard cases?

A believer who died by suicide. One who died in the middle of a relapse. The verses that seem to say one lie shuts the door, that you’ll be judged by your works, or that Jesus will say “I never knew you.” Some of these are funeral questions, and some keep people up at night. They get the same answer as the rest of this page: you’re kept by him, not by how your last day went.

They also get the same “by no means” Paul gave in Romans 6. Grace that covers a sin doesn’t make the sin a good idea. Both halves are true at once, and this section holds them together.

The text

Paul’s answer to “shall we sin, since grace covers it?” carries straight over. Your life isn’t yours to end: you were bought at a price. Therefore honor God with your bodies. 1 Corinthians 6:20

And it takes a lie to get there. Jesus called the enemy a murderer from the beginning and, in the same breath, the father of lies (John 8:44). And he set himself against that thief: The thief comes only to steal and kill and destroy; I have come that they may have life, and have it to the full. John 10:10

Then the promise that carves out no exception: nothing in all creation, death included, can separate a child of God from his love (Romans 8:38-39).

Scripture records several people who took their own lives, among them Saul, Ahithophel and Judas. In none of them does it rule on a person’s eternity by the way they died. Samson’s death is often counted too, and Hebrews lists him among the faithful (Hebrews 11:32), though most read his last act as a soldier’s, not despair.

Where Christians differ

For most of church history, suicide was treated as the one sin you can’t repent of afterward, and so as final despair. Some still hold a version of that, or say a true believer wouldn’t do it, so that those who do show they never believed. Others hold that a mind overtaken by illness, pain or torment isn’t a verdict on a person’s faith, and that the one who died was overcome, not unbelieving.

From the tradition
  • Augustine called taking one’s own life a form of killing, forbidden by the commandment not to kill. His view shaped the church for centuries. City of God I.20
  • Church councils refused funeral rites to people who had died this way, first at Braga in 561. Adamiak and Dohnalik, 2023
  • Thomas Aquinas gave three reasons it is wrong: it goes against the love a person owes themselves, it wounds the community, and life is God’s gift. Summa Theologiae II-II, q.64, a.5
  • Martin Luther refused to say such people are certainly damned. They are overcome by the devil, he said, like a traveler murdered in the woods by a robber. In the same breath he said this should not be taught to ordinary people, so as not to give the devil an opening. Table Talk
  • The Catechism of the Catholic Church says grave psychological disturbance, anguish or fear can diminish a person’s responsibility, and that “we should not despair of the eternal salvation of persons who have taken their own lives.” §2282-2283 Its 1983 canon law no longer lists suicide as a reason to refuse a funeral. 1983 Code, canon 1184
  • The Orthodox bishops of the Americas wrote that church burial can be granted where a person’s mind was compromised, and that the state of the one who died must remain in the hands of God. SCOBA letter, May 2007
Our reading

Should a believer take their own life, since grace would cover it? By no means, for the reasons Paul gave about any sin: you were bought, you belong to him, and the voice that says otherwise is a liar. Whatever the lie sounds like, “they’d be better off without me,” “this will never change,” it’s a lie.

But salvation doesn’t hang on the last act of a life, any more than it hangs on the last prayer (question 10). A believer who dies this way was overcome, not unsaved. Their standing rested on Christ, and he doesn’t lose what he holds (John 10:28).

If you’ve lost someone this way: their suffering was real, and so is the one who kept them. It wasn’t your failure, and it isn’t the last word about them.

Paul Ellis makes the case from the verb’s other uses. Paul Ellis on John 15:2
Our reading

We hold the second. The Father is a gardener, not a lumberjack. A branch in the vine that isn’t bearing yet gets lifted out of the dirt and propped up. A fruitful branch gets pruned, and Jesus says the pruning is his word (15:3). For a branch that is in him, there is no cutting and no fire.

Verse 6 is about someone who does not remain, and three verses later remaining is defined: remaining in his love (15:9-10). On our reading of 1 John 2:19, those who don’t remain show they were never truly his (question 10). And the tree cut down in Matthew 7:19 is the false prophet of verse 15, known by his fruit, not a branch in the vine.

Jesus told the same kind of story with a fig tree. The owner wants it cut down. The gardener asks for one more year, and digs around it and feeds it (Luke 13:6-9). The story still ends with an “if,” and it was told to a nation that wouldn’t turn. But notice who asks for more time, and what he does with it.

Fruit takes seasons (Mark 4:28), and it grows from staying attached, not from fear of the knife.

What to do when you sinTextReadingLook up, not in. Then get up.
The text
  • My dear children, I write this to you so that you will not sin. But if anybody does sin, we have an advocate with the Father—Jesus Christ, the Righteous One. 1 John 2:1
  • Let us then approach God’s throne of grace with confidence, so that we may receive mercy and find grace to help us in our time of need. Hebrews 4:16
  • for though the righteous fall seven times, they rise again, but the wicked stumble when calamity strikes. Proverbs 24:16
Our reading

Come close, with confidence, the verse says, at exactly the moment you feel least able to. Tell him the truth about it. Receive what’s already yours. Get up, and keep walking.

Don’t stay down to prove you’re sorry. Staying down is the flesh’s favorite kind of religion.

The text

And we all, who with unveiled faces contemplate the Lord’s glory, are being transformed into his image with ever-increasing glory, which comes from the Lord, who is the Spirit. 2 Corinthians 3:18

Our reading

Change comes from looking at him, not from inspecting your progress.

How what you hear becomes belief, and then fruit, is traced in The Word.

One old way to look is to mutter. The Hebrew word for meditate, hagah, means to murmur under your breath, and Joshua is told to keep the word on his lips (Joshua 1:8; Psalm 1:2). Take one verse, not five chapters. Say it aloud, slowly, several times. Say it again on a walk, and again falling asleep. The Thread has more.

Feelings run on sleep, stress and weather, and they are an unreliable narrator. A lot of faithful prayer has felt like nothing at all at the time. Show up dry. It counts. And when you need to decide, Paul gives you an umpire: let the peace of Christ rule in your hearts. The word for rule, brabeuō, is the referee’s word for making the call (Colossians 3:15).

Why it is slow has a physical side too: repeated thought wears its own paths. Spirit · Soul · Body traces the research.

Growing upTextReadingMilk first, then solid food, then fathers who know him.
The text

Peter writes to new believers: Like newborn babies, crave pure spiritual milk, so that by it you may grow up in your salvation, 1 Peter 2:2

Hebrews says solid food is for the mature, who have trained themselves by practice to tell good from evil (Hebrews 5:12-14). Paul told Corinth he could only give them milk (1 Corinthians 3:1-3), and he names the goal: growing up into Christ, no longer tossed around by every wind of teaching (Ephesians 4:13-15).

John writes to three ages in one church. Children, whose sins are forgiven and who know the Father. Young men, who are strong, who have the word living in them and have overcome the evil one. Fathers, who know him who is from the beginning (1 John 2:12-14).

Our reading

Growing doesn’t change your standing. It changes how much of it you live in. A baby is as much a son as a grown man. He just can’t eat steak yet.

Effort isn’t the enemy. Paul worked harder than any of the apostles, and said it wasn’t him but the grace of God with him (1 Corinthians 15:10). Law presses from outside. Grace grows from inside.

Look at John’s first stage: forgiven, and knowing the Father. You don’t grow into sonship. You start there. The young are strong because the word lives in them. And the last stage is simply knowing him who is from the beginning. Growing up ends where the page began: with the heart that was there from the beginning.

The stages, and how to tell where you are, are in Where Are You? and Soundings.

When life is hardTextReadingHe works in all things. That doesn’t make him the author of all things.
The text

James says to count trials as joy, because testing produces perseverance (James 1:2-4). A few lines later he is careful about where things come from. No one should say God is tempting them (James 1:13). Don’t be deceived, he says (James 1:16), and then:

Every good and perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of the heavenly lights, who does not change like shifting shadows. James 1:17

Paul says suffering produces perseverance, character and hope (Romans 5:3-5), and that in all things God works for the good of those who love him (Romans 8:28). Peter says trials prove faith genuine, the way fire proves gold (1 Peter 1:6-7).

Our reading

God works in all things. That doesn’t make him the author of all things. James puts the two side by side on purpose: trials can grow you, and it is every good gift, not every hard thing, that comes down from the Father.

So a hard season isn’t a verdict or a punishment. It’s a place where the vine keeps feeding the branch. What grows you there is what grows you anywhere: his word, his presence, and staying attached.

A dry season isn’t backsliding either, and hunting for a hidden sin to explain it usually does more harm than the dryness. Where Are You? and Soundings map those seasons.

What it looks like from the outsideTextReadingFruit, singular, and it belongs to the Spirit.
The text

But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, forbearance, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness and self-control. Against such things there is no law. Galatians 5:22-23

Our reading

It’s one fruit, not nine, and it’s the Spirit’s, not yours. The list describes someone who is safe. It isn’t the exam you pass to become safe.

Set it beside the list just before it (Galatians 5:19-21). More than half of the flesh’s list is about people: hatred, discord, jealousy, fits of rage, selfish ambition, dissensions, factions and envy. The flesh fractures. The Spirit gathers. Spirit · Soul · Body draws the same contrast.

Can you see it in a crowd?ResearchReadingLarge studies say yes for people who show up. None of them can measure belief.
The research
  • Among 89,708 women nurses in the US, followed from 1996 to 2010, those who attended religious services at least weekly had about a sixth of the suicide rate of those who never attended. VanderWeele et al., JAMA Psychiatry, 2016
  • In two large studies of health professionals, weekly attenders had a far lower risk of “deaths of despair,” from suicide, drugs and alcohol: a 68% lower risk among women and 33% lower among men. Chen et al., JAMA Psychiatry, 2020
  • 36% of actively religious American adults called themselves very happy, against about a quarter of inactive and unaffiliated adults. Pew Research Center, January 31, 2019
  • The claim that Christians divorce as often as everyone else holds only for people who call themselves Christians and rarely attend. About 60% of them had been divorced, against 38% of regular attenders. Bradley Wright, cited by Glenn Stanton, 2012

Every one of these measures attendance or a label, not belief. None can rule out that steadier people are simply more likely to attend, and their authors say so.

Our reading

Fruit is visible, over time, and mostly in community. That fits what the studies find. But no study can see into a heart, and none of these numbers is a test of anyone’s faith, yours included. The fruit is the Spirit’s, and it grows at his pace (question 03).
