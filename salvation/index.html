Salvation

What actually happens when you believe.

Salvation is a rescue, and it was finished before you got there. Every sin was paid for. The door is open. The only thing that keeps anyone out is not walking through. Walking through is called believing. Not agreeing. Trusting. When you believe, God doesn’t improve you. He gives you a new birth, a new standing and a new name: child. From that day he sees you in his Son. That isn’t a feeling. It’s where you stand. You’ll still stumble. The standing doesn’t move. That’s what grace is.

This page shows the verses, says what we take them to mean, and marks every place where Christians read them differently. You’ll be able to tell the three apart the whole way down.

01

What is salvation?

The Greek word is sōzō: to rescue, to make safe, to heal. It’s the word for pulling someone out of the sea.

The name Jesus is the Hebrew Yeshua, a short form of Yehoshua: “the LORD saves.” The angel said what from: She will give birth to a son, and you are to give him the name Jesus, because he will save his people from their sins.” Matthew 1:21 Salvation isn’t a status upgrade, or a ticket. It’s being pulled out of something you couldn’t get out of, by someone who could.

Three tenses of one rescueTextReadingSaved, being saved, will be saved. Most of the worry lives in mixing them up.
The text

You have been saved: For it is by grace you have been saved, through faith—and this is not from yourselves, it is the gift of God— Ephesians 2:8 In Greek that’s a perfect tense: done, and still standing.

Our reading

The past tense is the one that answers “am I saved?” The present tense is your soul being renewed, which is unfinished in everyone. The future tense is your body raised. Most people who doubt their salvation are looking at the middle tense and asking it a question only the first one can answer.

These map onto spirit, soul and body, laid out in full in Spirit · Soul · Body.

Churches differ on baptism’s part. Some read Acts 2:38 and 1 Peter 3:21 as making baptism the moment of new birth. Others read Ephesians 2:8-9 and the thief as placing that moment at faith, with baptism as the public sign that follows.

Our reading

We hold the second. The new birth happens when you believe, and baptism is its commanded, public witness. Both sides agree baptism isn’t optional for a believer. The question is only whether it saves, or shows.

03

Is it really not by works?

Yes. And the reason matters: if you could earn it, it would be wages, and wages can be docked.

Paul puts it bluntly: However, to the one who does not work but trusts God who justifies the ungodly, their faith is credited as righteousness. Romans 4:5

But “believe” is where people slip. The word is pisteuō: to trust, to rely on, to lean your weight on. You believe that there is one God. Good! Even the demons believe that—and shudder. James 2:19 Agreeing that God exists isn’t the door. Trusting him is.

Some read James as saying works complete your justification before God. Works are the fruit on the tree. They’re never its root.

What trust looks likeTextReadingTrust is when you stop checking.
The text

A Roman officer sent word rather than come himself: The centurion replied, “Lord, I do not deserve to have you come under my roof. But just say the word, and my servant will be healed. Matthew 8:8 Then he went home, and the servant was well Matthew 8:13

Our reading

Believing, in the New Testament’s sense, is resting your whole weight on something that’s already true. You sit in a chair without testing it first. That’s the posture.

It doesn’t need to be loud, and it doesn’t need to feel strong. A weak grip on a strong rope still holds you up.

Believing, in John, is always a verbTextReadingHe never uses the noun “faith.” He uses “believe” about a hundred times.
The text

John wrote his Gospel so that people would believe (John 20:31). Yet the noun pistis, faith, never appears in it. The verb pisteuō appears about a hundred times, and over thirty of those are “believe into him,” pisteuō eis: the way you move your weight onto something.

He also shows believing that isn’t trust yet. In Jerusalem, many believed in his name when they saw his signs (John 2:23). Then: But Jesus would not entrust himself to them, for he knew all people. John 2:24 In Greek it’s the same verb both times. They believed in him, and he didn’t entrust himself to them.

It happens again in John 8. A crowd that believed him at verse 30 is picking up stones by verse 59.

Our reading

John isn’t describing two strengths of faith. He’s describing two objects. The crowd in Jerusalem trusted what he could do. The people he calls his own trust who he is.

Being impressed by Jesus isn’t the same as leaning on him. You can admire a bridge from the bank. Believing is walking out onto it.

That should settle a fear, not start one. The question was never whether your belief is strong enough. It’s whether it’s resting on him.

Trying to be a better personTextReadingThe most respectable man in Jerusalem was told he needed to be born.
The text

Nicodemus was a Pharisee, a member of the ruling council, and in Jesus’ own words Israel’s teacher (John 3:10). If anyone could have improved his way in, he could. Jesus didn’t offer him a better record: Jesus replied, “Very truly I tell you, no one can see the kingdom of God unless they are born again.” John 3:3

Paul had the best religious record of his generation, and he lists it (Philippians 3:4-6). Then he writes it off: But whatever were gains to me I now consider loss for the sake of Christ. Philippians 3:7

And he says rules can’t even do what people hope they’ll do: Such regulations indeed have an appearance of wisdom, with their self-imposed worship, their false humility and their harsh treatment of the body, but they lack any value in restraining sensual indulgence. Colossians 2:23

Our reading

Wanting to be a better person isn’t wrong. It’s the wrong door. Self-improvement renovates a house. The gospel is a birth, and you can’t renovate your way into being born.

Most self-help, and a good deal of religion, runs on one engine: try harder, keep the rules, and you’ll become someone worth accepting. Paul ran that engine harder than anyone. It made him impressive. It couldn’t make him new, and it couldn’t even hold back what it was aimed at.

So the order flips. You’re made new first, and the better person grows out of that. It’s the same arrow as become, belong, behave, pointed the right way.

Works of the flesh, fruit of the SpiritTextReadingPaul never calls the good list “works.” Works are made. Fruit grows.
The text

Galatians 5 sets two lists side by side and names them differently. The first is the erga of the flesh, its works (Galatians 5:19). The second is the karpos of the Spirit, its fruit (Galatians 5:22). Works is plural. Fruit is singular. One list is produced. The other grows.

Jesus used the same picture: “Make a tree good and its fruit will be good, or make a tree bad and its fruit will be bad, for a tree is recognized by its fruit. Matthew 12:33 And he said where fruit comes from: “I am the vine; you are the branches. If you remain in me and I in you, you will bear much fruit; apart from me you can do nothing. John 15:5

He told a parable about how slowly, and how unforced, the growing is: All by itself the soil produces grain—first the stalk, then the head, then the full kernel in the head. Mark 4:28

Our reading

Works are what you produce to become something. Fruit is what shows up because you already are something. An apple tree doesn’t strain to be an apple tree. You could staple apples to a fence post, and it would still be a fence post.

That’s the difference between trying to prove you’re saved and being saved. The fruit is real, and people can see it. But it’s the Spirit’s, it takes seasons, and it grows from staying attached, not from effort aimed at the fruit. What it looks like from the outside is in question 12.

“Fallen from grace”TextDisputedReadingPaul said it about the people doing more, not less.
The text

In English, someone “falls from grace” when they’re caught in a scandal. Paul used the phrase once, and he meant nearly the opposite: You who are trying to be justified by the law have been alienated from Christ; you have fallen away from grace. Galatians 5:4

The Galatians hadn’t drifted into immorality. They were adding circumcision to faith, to be sure (Galatians 5:2-3). Paul’s alarm was about the ones being most careful. Just before, he told them: It is for freedom that Christ has set us free. Stand firm, then, and do not let yourselves be burdened again by a yoke of slavery. Galatians 5:1

And he had already asked the question the whole letter turns on: I would like to learn just one thing from you: Did you receive the Spirit by the works of the law, or by believing what you heard? Galatians 3:2

Where Christians differ
  • Lost. “Alienated from Christ” means what it says: a believer who turns to the law for their standing can be cut off. Notice that even on this reading, what cuts them off is adding works, not sinning.
  • Never had it. Anyone who finally trusts the law instead of Christ shows they were never trusting Christ.
  • Off the ground, not out of the family. They stepped off grace as the ground they stood on, onto law. They lost the freedom, not the sonship. Paul still calls them brothers and sisters, and says he’s confident they’ll come around (Galatians 5:10).
Our reading

We hold the third. But all three readings agree on which way the fall goes. The danger Galatians names isn’t sinning too much. It’s trusting something added to Christ.

That’s why working for it and believing are opposites, not partners. Paul puts them on two sides of one sentence: Now to the one who works, wages are not credited as a gift but as an obligation. Romans 4:4 And he says why Israel missed what the Gentiles found: Why not? Because they pursued it not by faith but as if it were by works. They stumbled over the stumbling stone. Romans 9:32

So if you’re working to be accepted, you aren’t trusting yet. You’re trusting yourself, and the work is the tell. But the same good deed done because you’re already accepted is something else entirely. Paul follows “not by works” with this, in the next breath: For we are God’s handiwork, created in Christ Jesus to do good works, which God prepared in advance for us to do. Ephesians 2:10 Not by works. For works. The deed can look identical. What it’s for is the whole difference.

Hebrews says it about rest: for anyone who enters God’s rest also rests from their works, just as God did from his. Hebrews 4:10 The unbelief Paul fought hardest didn’t look like atheism. It looked like religion that wouldn’t stop working.

The fear that grace makes people careless is real, and Paul answers it (question 08). But look at how he answered the one church that did get careless. Not with more law. With identity: you were bought, you’re his, you’re one spirit with him. Grace had its own answer to license, and it wasn’t a rulebook.

04

What is the sin that isn’t forgiven?

Our reading: the one thing that can’t be forgiven is refusing the forgiveness.

Jesus named what the Spirit convicts the world of, and gave the reason: about sin, because people do not believe in me; John 16:9 Every other sin has a remedy. This one is the rejection of the remedy. If you push away the one who brings you to the door, there’s no other door.

That’s why unbelief isn’t one sin among many. It’s the sin that keeps all the others.

If you’re afraid you’ve committed it, you haven’t.The fear is the Spirit still at work in you. A hardened heart doesn’t lie awake worrying that it’s hard.

Why we read it as unbeliefReadingTextNot a word said by accident. A refusal held to the end.
Our reading

The sin against the Spirit isn’t a phrase you can blurt out, or a thought that crosses your mind. It’s a settled refusal of what the Spirit says about Jesus, kept to the end.

So “unbelief is the unforgivable sin” is true in this sense: not that God refuses to forgive it, but that it’s the one place forgiveness can’t be received. It’s why belief is the only way. Not because God is narrow. Because there is one door, and this is standing outside it with your arms folded.

The text

The Spirit and the bride say, “Come!” And let the one who hears say, “Come!” Let the one who is thirsty come; and let the one who wishes take the free gift of the water of life. Revelation 22:17 The only thing between anyone and the water is the taking.

The church has said this beforeHistoryReadingAugustine, Aquinas, and a modern catechism.
From the tradition
  • Augustine preached a whole sermon on Matthew 12:32 and landed here: the unforgivable blasphemy is impenitence, a heart that refuses forgiveness to the end of life. Sermon 71, §20 He says it again, and names the sermon, in his Enchiridion, ch. 83
  • Thomas Aquinas, following Peter Lombard, lists six sins against the Holy Spirit. Two of them are final impenitence and obstinacy. Summa Theologiae II-II, q.14, a.2
  • The Catechism of the Catholic Church says there are no limits to God’s mercy, but whoever deliberately refuses it by refusing to repent rejects the forgiveness of sins, and that such hardness of heart can lead to final impenitence. §1864
Our reading

This isn’t a new reading invented to comfort people. It’s one of the oldest there is.

Where Christians differ
  • A specific act. Seeing the Spirit’s work plainly and deliberately calling it Satan’s, as those Pharisees did. Some hold it was only possible while Jesus was on earth.
  • Final unbelief. A refusal of the Spirit’s witness to Jesus, held until death. This is our reading.

All three agree on one thing, and it matters more than which is right: the person who is afraid they’ve committed it hasn’t. The worry itself is evidence that the Spirit is still speaking, and still being heard.

Our reading

We read it through Paul, writing after the cross: Be kind and compassionate to one another, forgiving each other, just as in Christ God forgave you. Ephesians 4:32 Forgiven, therefore forgive. Not forgive, in order to be forgiven.

An unforgiving believer is a real problem. But it’s a symptom of forgetting the debt, not a trapdoor.

Where Christians differ

Whole traditions, Wesleyan, Catholic and Orthodox among them, hold that a believer can finally turn away and be lost. Reformed and many Baptist churches hold that the born again are kept to the end.

Our reading

We hold that those born of God are kept. The warning passages describe the refusal question 04 is about: a deliberate, final turning from Christ himself, not a believer’s bad week, or bad year.

But these are serious texts, taken seriously by serious Christians, and they aren’t shortened here.

11

What about the hard cases?

A believer who died by suicide. One who died in the middle of a relapse. The verses that seem to say one lie shuts the door. These are funeral questions, and they get the same answer as the rest of this page: you’re kept by him, not by how your last day went.

They also get the same “by no means” Paul gave in Romans 6. Grace that covers a sin doesn’t make the sin a good idea. Both halves are true at once, and this section holds them together.

If you’re thinking about ending your life, please tell someone today. In the US, call or text 988, any hour. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free, confidential lines by country. Help works. The thought that nothing will ever change is one of the lies this section is about.

A believer who died by suicideTextDisputedHistoryReadingShould anyone? By no means. Were they lost? Not by the way they died.
The text

Paul’s answer to “shall we sin, since grace covers it?” carries straight over. Your life isn’t yours to end: you were bought at a price. Therefore honor God with your bodies. 1 Corinthians 6:20

And it takes a lie to get there. Jesus called the enemy a murderer from the beginning and, in the same breath, the father of lies (John 8:44). And he set himself against that thief: The thief comes only to steal and kill and destroy; I have come that they may have life, and have it to the full. John 10:10

Then the promise that carves out no exception: nothing in all creation, death included, can separate a child of God from his love (Romans 8:38-39).

Scripture records several people who took their own lives, among them Saul, Ahithophel and Judas. In none of them does it rule on a person’s eternity by the way they died. Samson’s death is often counted too, and Hebrews lists him among the faithful (Hebrews 11:32), though most read his last act as a soldier’s, not despair.

Where Christians differ

For most of church history, suicide was treated as the one sin you can’t repent of afterward, and so as final despair. Some still hold a version of that, or say a true believer wouldn’t do it, so that those who do show they never believed. Others hold that a mind overtaken by illness, pain or torment isn’t a verdict on a person’s faith, and that the one who died was overcome, not unbelieving.

From the tradition
  • Augustine called taking one’s own life a form of killing, forbidden by the commandment not to kill. His view shaped the church for centuries. City of God I.20
  • Church councils refused funeral rites to people who had died this way, first at Braga in 561. Adamiak and Dohnalik, 2023
  • Thomas Aquinas gave three reasons it is wrong: it goes against the love a person owes themselves, it wounds the community, and life is God’s gift. Summa Theologiae II-II, q.64, a.5
  • Martin Luther refused to say such people are certainly damned. They are overcome by the devil, he said, like a traveler murdered in the woods by a robber. In the same breath he said this should not be taught to ordinary people, so as not to give the devil an opening. Table Talk
  • The Catechism of the Catholic Church says grave psychological disturbance, anguish or fear can diminish a person’s responsibility, and that “we should not despair of the eternal salvation of persons who have taken their own lives.” §2282-2283 Its 1983 canon law no longer lists suicide as a reason to refuse a funeral. 1983 Code, canon 1184
  • The Orthodox bishops of the Americas wrote that church burial can be granted where a person’s mind was compromised, and that the state of the one who died must remain in the hands of God. SCOBA letter, May 2007
Our reading

Should a believer take their own life, since grace would cover it? By no means, for the reasons Paul gave about any sin: you were bought, you belong to him, and the voice that says otherwise is a liar. Whatever the lie sounds like, “they’d be better off without me,” “this will never change,” it’s a lie.

But salvation doesn’t hang on the last act of a life, any more than it hangs on the last prayer (question 10). A believer who dies this way was overcome, not unsaved. Their standing rested on Christ, and he doesn’t lose what he holds (John 10:28).

If you’ve lost someone this way: their suffering was real, and so is the one who kept them. It wasn’t your failure, and it isn’t the last word about them.

Where Christians differ

Those who hold that salvation can be lost draw the line at practicing sin rather than struggling with it, and addiction sits right on that line. Even so, Vlad Savchuk, who teaches that salvation can be lost, told his church about a young man who beat a drug habit and died two months later after one slip. His point was that unconfessed sin at the moment of death isn’t what disqualifies anyone from heaven. Sermon, June 8, 2020, at 46:01

Our reading

The New Testament has believers who died because of how they sinned, and in the same sentence says they weren’t condemned with the world. Death can be a consequence. It can even be discipline. It isn’t the verdict.

Addiction repeats almost by definition, which is why it frightens people most. But you aren’t kept by your streak (question 10). If you’ve lost someone this way, the question isn’t what was found in their system. It’s who they had trusted.

“If anyone destroys God’s temple”TextReadingOften quoted about suicide. The “you” is plural, and the temple is the church.
The text

Don’t you know that you yourselves are God’s temple and that God’s Spirit dwells in your midst? If anyone destroys God’s temple, God will destroy that person; for God’s temple is sacred, and you together are that temple. 1 Corinthians 3:16-17

Every “you” in those two verses is plural in Greek: you, all of you together, are God’s temple. The chapter is about a church splitting into camps, “I follow Paul,” “I follow Apollos” (1 Corinthians 3:4) and about builders whose work will be tested by fire. The temple is the congregation. The one who destroys it is whoever tears it apart.

The verse about your own body as a temple comes three chapters later (1 Corinthians 6:19-20). It carries no threat at all. It says you were bought, so honor God with your body.

Our reading

Reading 3:17 as a sentence on someone who died by suicide takes a warning about church division and aims it at a grieving family. None of the standard commentaries we checked read it that way, and the grammar won’t let it. Two verses earlier, the same passage says a believer whose work burns up will still be saved (1 Corinthians 3:15).

“Will not inherit the kingdom,” and “all liars”TextDisputedReadingIf one lie shut the door, Peter would be standing outside it.
The text

Paul lists those who will not inherit the kingdom of God (1 Corinthians 6:9-10; Galatians 5:19-21; Ephesians 5:5). Revelation’s list ends with all liars (Revelation 21:8).

Three things sit right beside them. Straight after the Corinthian list: And that is what some of you were. But you were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ and by the Spirit of our God. 1 Corinthians 6:11 Two chapters before the Galatian list: For if the inheritance depends on the law, then it no longer depends on the promise; but God in his grace gave it to Abraham through a promise. Galatians 3:18 And one verse before the liars: Those who are victorious will inherit all this, and I will be their God and they will be my children. Revelation 21:7

The grammar points the same way. Galatians describes them with a present participle, hoi ta toiauta prassontes, “those who practice such things”: a way of life, not a single act. Revelation’s last list says the same of everyone who loves and practices falsehood (Revelation 22:15).

Where Christians differ
  • They describe unbelievers. The lists name who people are, not what a believer did on a bad day. A life defined by these things shows there was no faith.
  • Inheriting isn’t entering. Some Free Grace teachers read “inherit” as reward and reigning with Christ, not getting in. Paul calls the inheritance a reward in one place (Colossians 3:24) and ties being a co-heir with Christ to sharing his sufferings (Romans 8:17).
  • A real warning to believers. Paul says “do not be deceived” before the Corinthian list. Those who hold that salvation can be lost read these as warnings that a believer who goes on living this way can be shut out.
Our reading

We hold the first, and take the second seriously. The lists describe who a person is. Believers still sin, lying included. Peter lied three times in one night, and Jesus restored him. If one lie shut the door, everyone would be standing outside it, Peter first.

You inherit because you’re a child, not because you behaved. Paul says it outright: since you are his child, God has made you also an heir (Galatians 4:7). And John defines the heirs of Revelation 21 himself. The heir there is, in Greek, ho nikōn, the one who overcomes, and his first letter says who that is: Who is it that overcomes the world? Only the one who believes that Jesus is the Son of God. 1 John 5:5 The list in Revelation 21:8 even names the unbelieving before it names the liars.

Five questions for a frightening verseReadingBefore a verse keeps you up at night, ask it these.
Our reading
  1. Who is “you”? One person, or the whole church? 1 Corinthians 3:17 is the whole church.
  2. One act, or a way of life? Watch for present tenses and words like “practice.” Galatians 5:21 and 1 John 3:9 are both about a way of life.
  3. What is at stake? Your standing, your reward, or an earthly consequence? In 1 Corinthians 3:15 the work burns and the person is saved. In 1 Corinthians 11:32 the judgment is discipline, not condemnation.
  4. What does the same writer say a few lines away? 1 Corinthians 6:11. Galatians 3:18. Revelation 21:7.
  5. What were the first readers facing? “Fallen from grace” was about adding circumcision, not moral failure (question 03). Hebrews was written to believers tempted to go back to the temple sacrifices.

Some verses stay hard after all five. Hebrews 6:4-6 and 10:26 are the hardest for the view this page holds, and they’re set out at full strength in question 10.

How do I live it out?

Not by trying harder. By believing what’s already true, and moving from there.

In the same way, count yourselves dead to sin but alive to God in Christ Jesus. Romans 6:11 Count it so.

So I say, walk by the Spirit, and you will not gratify the desires of the flesh. Galatians 5:16 That’s a promise, not a threat.

Therefore, my dear friends, as you have always obeyed—not only in my presence, but now much more in my absence—continue to work out your salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in you to will and to act in order to fulfill his good purpose. Philippians 2:12-13 Out, not for.

You aren’t becoming God’s child by behaving. You’re learning to behave like the child you already are.

Can you see it in a crowd?ResearchReadingLarge studies say yes for people who show up. None of them can measure belief.
The research
  • Among 89,708 women nurses in the US, followed from 1996 to 2010, those who attended religious services at least weekly had about a sixth of the suicide rate of those who never attended. VanderWeele et al., JAMA Psychiatry, 2016
  • In two large studies of health professionals, weekly attenders had a far lower risk of “deaths of despair,” from suicide, drugs and alcohol: a 68% lower risk among women and 33% lower among men. Chen et al., JAMA Psychiatry, 2020
  • 36% of actively religious American adults called themselves very happy, against about a quarter of inactive and unaffiliated adults. Pew Research Center, January 31, 2019
  • The claim that Christians divorce as often as everyone else holds only for people who call themselves Christians and rarely attend. About 60% of them had been divorced, against 38% of regular attenders. Bradley Wright, cited by Glenn Stanton, 2012

Every one of these measures attendance or a label, not belief. None can rule out that steadier people are simply more likely to attend, and their authors say so.

Our reading

Fruit is visible, over time, and mostly in community. That fits what the studies find. But no study can see into a heart, and none of these numbers is a test of anyone’s faith, yours included. The fruit is the Spirit’s, and it grows at his pace (question 03).
