Salvation

What actually happens when you believe.

Salvation is a rescue, and it was finished before you got there. Every sin was paid for. The door is open. The only thing that keeps anyone out is not walking through. Walking through is called believing. Not agreeing. Trusting. When you believe, God doesn’t improve you. He gives you a new birth, a new standing and a new name: child. From that day he sees you in his Son. That isn’t a feeling. It’s where you stand. You’ll still stumble. The standing doesn’t move. That’s what grace is.

This page shows the verses, says what we take them to mean, and marks every place where Christians read them differently. You’ll be able to tell the three apart the whole way down.

01

What is salvation?

The Greek word is sōzō: to rescue, to make safe, to heal. It’s the word for pulling someone out of the sea.

The name Jesus is the Hebrew Yeshua, a short form of Yehoshua: “the LORD saves.” The angel said what from: he shall save his people from their sins (Matthew 1:21). Salvation isn’t a status upgrade, or a ticket. It’s being pulled out of something you couldn’t get out of, by someone who could.

Rescued from what, to whatTextSalvation has a direction. Two lists, side by side.
The text

Rescued from

  • The debt of sin Colossians 2:14
  • The power of sin Romans 6:6, 6:14
  • Death Romans 6:23
  • Wrath Romans 5:9

Brought into

  • The family Galatians 4:6-7
  • The kingdom of his Son Colossians 1:13

Both lists come from a handful of letters. “Saved” isn’t a mood. It has a from and a to.

Three tenses of one rescueTextReadingSaved, being saved, will be saved. Most of the worry lives in mixing them up.
The text

You have been saved: by grace are ye saved through faith (Ephesians 2:8). In Greek that’s a perfect tense: done, and still standing.

You are being saved: Paul speaks of us which are saved (1 Corinthians 1:18), and the Greek is a present participle, “us who are being saved.”

You will be saved: we shall be saved from wrath through him (Romans 5:9).

Our reading

The past tense is the one that answers “am I saved?” The present tense is your soul being renewed, which is unfinished in everyone. The future tense is your body raised. Most people who doubt their salvation are looking at the middle tense and asking it a question only the first one can answer.

These map onto spirit, soul and body, laid out in full in Spirit · Soul · Body.

The word in Jesus’ mouthTextReadingHe used it for things that arrived that day.
The text

To a woman who had brought nothing but tears and ointment: Thy faith hath saved thee; go in peace (Luke 7:50). To a tax collector who had just climbed down from a tree: This day is salvation come to this house (Luke 19:9). And his own summary of why he came: to seek and to save that which was lost (Luke 19:10).

Our reading

The same verb heals a blind man (Mark 10:52) and forgives a sinner. In his mouth, salvation wasn’t a course you enrolled in. It arrived.

Was it instant?TextDisputedReadingA thief with no time, no baptism, and one sentence.
The text

A dying thief said Lord, remember me when thou comest into thy kingdom, and heard To day shalt thou be with me in paradise (Luke 23:42-43). No baptism, no works, no time to ask twice.

At Cornelius’s house the Holy Ghost fell on all them which heard the word, before anyone had been baptised (Acts 10:44-48).

Where Christians differ

Churches differ on baptism’s part. Some read Acts 2:38 and 1 Peter 3:21 as making baptism the moment of new birth. Others read Ephesians 2:8-9 and the thief as placing that moment at faith, with baptism as the public sign that follows.

Our reading

We hold the second. The new birth happens when you believe, and baptism is its commanded, public witness. Both sides agree baptism isn’t optional for a believer. The question is only whether it saves, or shows.

03

Is it really not by works?

Yes. And the reason matters: if you could earn it, it would be wages, and wages can be docked.

Paul puts it bluntly: to him that worketh not, but believeth on him that justifieth the ungodly, his faith is counted for righteousness (Romans 4:5).

But “believe” is where people slip. The word is pisteuō: to trust, to rely on, to lean your weight on. the devils also believe, and tremble (James 2:19). Agreeing that God exists isn’t the door. Trusting him is.

The text

  • a man is justified by faith without the deeds of the law Romans 3:28
  • if by grace, then is it no more of works: otherwise grace is no more grace Romans 11:6
  • a man is not justified by the works of the law, but by the faith of Jesus Christ Galatians 2:16
  • if righteousness come by the law, then Christ is dead in vain Galatians 2:21
  • having begun in the Spirit, are ye now made perfect by the flesh? Galatians 3:3
  • Not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy he saved us Titus 3:5
  • not having mine own righteousness, which is of the law, but that which is through the faith of Christ Philippians 3:9
  • this man went down to his house justified rather than the other Luke 18:14
Our reading

The last one is a story. The man with a list of good deeds went home unchanged. The man who could only say God be merciful to me a sinner went home justified.

“This is the work of God”TextReadingThey asked what works to do. He named one.
The text

They asked him, What shall we do, that we might work the works of God? He answered, This is the work of God, that ye believe on him whom he hath sent (John 6:28-29).

Our reading

The only work God asks for, to be saved, is to stop working for it and trust. How faith itself works, where it comes from and what the Greek says, is the whole subject of FAITH, so this page won’t repeat it.

Then what about James?TextDisputedReading“Faith without works is dead.” The first objection anyone raises.
The text

Even so faith, if it hath not works, is dead, being alone (James 2:17). And James says Abraham was justified by works, when he had offered Isaac (James 2:21).

Where Christians differ

Some read James as saying works complete your justification before God. Others read him using “justified” the way Jesus did in wisdom is justified of her children (Matthew 11:19): shown to be right, proven by what it produces.

Our reading

We hold the second, and the order of events settles it. Paul quotes Genesis 15:6, he believed in the LORD; and he counted it to him for righteousness. James points to Genesis 22, the offering of Isaac, which comes years later. Abraham was declared righteous when he trusted. The offering showed what kind of trust it was.

Works are the fruit on the tree. They’re never its root.

What trust looks likeTextReadingTrust is when you stop checking.
The text

A Roman officer told Jesus, speak the word only, and my servant shall be healed, and went home (Matthew 8:8-13).

if thou shalt confess with thy mouth the Lord Jesus, and shalt believe in thine heart that God hath raised him from the dead, thou shalt be saved (Romans 10:9).

Our reading

Believing, in the New Testament’s sense, is resting your whole weight on something that’s already true. You sit in a chair without testing it first. That’s the posture.

It doesn’t need to be loud, and it doesn’t need to feel strong. A weak grip on a strong rope still holds you up.

04

What is the sin that isn’t forgiven?

Our reading: the one thing that can’t be forgiven is refusing the forgiveness.

Jesus said the Spirit would convict the world Of sin, because they believe not on me (John 16:8-9). Every other sin has a remedy. This one is the rejection of the remedy. If you push away the one who brings you to the door, there’s no other door.

That’s why unbelief isn’t one sin among many. It’s the sin that keeps all the others.

If you’re afraid you’ve committed it, you haven’t.The fear is the Spirit still at work in you. A hardened heart doesn’t lie awake worrying that it’s hard.

What he said, and why he said itTextMark tells you what the Pharisees had just done.
The text

Mark gives the same saying, and then the reason for it: he that shall blaspheme against the Holy Ghost hath never forgiveness, Because they said, He hath an unclean spirit (Mark 3:28-30). They had watched the Spirit set a man free, and called it the devil’s work.

Hebrews uses different words for the same shape: they could not enter in because of unbelief (Hebrews 3:19). And John: he that believeth not is condemned already, because he hath not believed in the name of the only begotten Son of God (John 3:18).

Why we read it as unbeliefReadingTextNot a word said by accident. A refusal held to the end.
Our reading

The sin against the Spirit isn’t a phrase you can blurt out, or a thought that crosses your mind. It’s a settled refusal of what the Spirit says about Jesus, kept to the end.

So “unbelief is the unforgivable sin” is true in this sense: not that God refuses to forgive it, but that it’s the one place forgiveness can’t be received. It’s why belief is the only way. Not because God is narrow. Because there is one door, and this is standing outside it with your arms folded.

The text

whosoever will, let him take the water of life freely (Revelation 22:17). The only thing between anyone and the water is the taking.

The church has said this beforeHistoryReadingAugustine, Aquinas, and a modern catechism.
From the tradition
  • Augustine preached a whole sermon on Matthew 12:32 and landed here: the unforgivable blasphemy is impenitence, a heart that refuses forgiveness to the end of life. Sermon 71, §20 He says it again, and names the sermon, in his Enchiridion, ch. 83
  • Thomas Aquinas, following Peter Lombard, lists six sins against the Holy Spirit. Two of them are final impenitence and obstinacy. Summa Theologiae II-II, q.14, a.2
  • The Catechism of the Catholic Church says there are no limits to God’s mercy, but whoever deliberately refuses it by refusing to repent rejects the forgiveness of sins, and that such hardness of heart can lead to final impenitence. §1864
Our reading

This isn’t a new reading invented to comfort people. It’s one of the oldest there is.

Where Christians read it differentlyDisputedReadingThree readings, and the one thing all of them agree on.
Where Christians differ
  • A specific act. Seeing the Spirit’s work plainly and deliberately calling it Satan’s, as those Pharisees did. Some hold it was only possible while Jesus was on earth.
  • Final unbelief. A refusal of the Spirit’s witness to Jesus, held until death. This is our reading.
  • Turning away with full knowledge. Walking away after tasting the truth, read from Hebrews 6:4-6 and 10:26-29.
Our reading

All three agree on one thing, and it matters more than which is right: the person who is afraid they’ve committed it hasn’t. The worry itself is evidence that the Spirit is still speaking, and still being heard.

Does this make God harsh?ReadingNo. It makes him consistent.
Our reading

He paid for everything. He won’t force anyone to take it. A gift you can be made to take isn’t a gift. The door stays open for as long as you live, and he keeps saying come.

05

Does God love me? Am I his child?

Does he love you before you believe? Yes. Does he love you more after? No. The love doesn’t grow. The relationship changes.

God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us (Romans 5:8). Before, you were loved as his creature, even as his enemy (Romans 5:10), the runaway watched for from the road. After, you’re loved as his child: that we should be called the sons of God (1 John 3:1).

Same love. New name.

Everyone is his offspring. Not everyone is his child.TextReadingMade by him isn’t the same as born of him.
The text

Paul told a crowd of pagans in Athens, For we are also his offspring (Acts 17:28).

John writes about becoming: But as many as received him, to them gave he power to become the sons of God, even to them that believe on his name, Which were born, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God (John 1:12-13).

And Paul again: ye are all the children of God by faith in Christ Jesus (Galatians 3:26).

Our reading

By creation, everyone belongs to God. By birth, only the born again are his children. Being a child is something you become, and John says how: by receiving him.

Nobody is outside his love. Not everyone is inside his family yet.

The verses people use to say God hatesTextDisputedReading“Esau have I hated.” “Children of wrath.” Read with the idiom.
The text
  • thou hatest all workers of iniquity Psalm 5:5
  • Jacob have I loved, but Esau have I hated Romans 9:13, quoting Malachi 1:2-3
  • and were by nature the children of wrath, even as others Ephesians 2:3
Where Christians differ

Reformed readers take some of these at face value: God’s saving love is particular, and not everyone is inside it. Others read them as covenant language and Hebrew idiom.

Our reading

We hold the second, and the idiom is easy to see. In Hebrew, and in the Greek that borrows from it, “son of” something means “marked by” it. James and John are The sons of thunder (Mark 3:17). Barnabas is The son of consolation (Acts 4:36). The disobedient are, in the Greek of Ephesians 2:2, “the sons of disobedience.”

So “children of wrath” names a condition, not a father, and the very next verse says so: But God, who is rich in mercy, for his great love wherewith he loved us (Ephesians 2:4). Jacob and Esau in Malachi are two nations and a covenant choice. Psalm 5 is what God feels about the doing of evil. None of them cancel John 3:16.

  • and hast loved them, as thou hast loved me John 17:23

Our reading

This isn’t God pretending. He sees you in his Son because you are in his Son. And the Father loves a believer the way he loves Jesus, because Jesus asked him to, in those words, the night before the cross.

06

If he reconciled the whole world, is everyone saved?

Both halves are true. The reconciling is done, from God’s side, for everyone. The receiving isn’t automatic, or Paul wouldn’t be pleading.

A pardon with your name on it is yours. It still has to be taken. they which receive abundance of grace and of the gift of righteousness shall reign in life (Romans 5:17). The gift is given. The ones who reign are the ones who receive.

The world-sized verses, at full strengthTextDon’t water them down. The good news needs them.
The text
  • he is the propitiation for our sins: and not for ours only, but also for the sins of the whole world 1 John 2:2
  • the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all Isaiah 53:6
  • that he by the grace of God should taste death for every man Hebrews 2:9
  • the free gift came upon all men unto justification of life Romans 5:18
  • by him to reconcile all things unto himself Colossians 1:20
Three ways to read themDisputedReadingEveryone saved, only some paid for, or paid for all and received by faith.
Where Christians differ
  • Everyone will be saved in the end. Universalism reads “all” as the final outcome for every person.
  • Christ died to save the elect. Limited atonement, held in the Canons of Dort and the Westminster Confession, reads “world” as people from every nation rather than every person.
  • Paid for all, received by faith. The provision is universal, and it becomes yours when you believe. This is our reading.
Our reading

The same letters that say “the whole world” also say “be ye reconciled,” “whosoever believeth,” and “condemned already, because he hath not believed.” A reading that makes any of those sentences pointless is the wrong reading.

Universalism empties the plea. Limited atonement shrinks the world. Provision received by faith lets every sentence mean what it says.

So who is a child of God?ReadingLoved, paid for, invited: everyone. Born: those who receive.
Our reading

Everyone is loved. Everyone is paid for. Everyone is invited. Those who receive are born again, and the born are children.

Provision is a fact about God. Sonship is a fact about you, and it starts when you believe. The offspring and children verses are in question 05.

Do I really look like Jesus?

In the part of you that was born again, you are as righteous, as accepted and as alive as he is, because that part of you is joined to him (1 Corinthians 6:17, Galatians 2:20).

Paul wrote to the worst-behaved church in the New Testament and opened by calling them saints. Five chapters later he wrote such were some of you: but ye are washed, but ye are sanctified, but ye are justified (1 Corinthians 6:11). He didn’t call them saints because of how they behaved. He called them saints, and argued from there.

08

Then why do I still sin?

Because the rescue changed your nature and your standing. It hasn’t yet changed your habits, your body, or the world you wake up in.

Paul, who wrote There is therefore now no condemnation, also wrote what I hate, that do I, and put them a few verses apart (Romans 7:15, 8:1).

Sin is no longer who you are. It’s still what you practised for years. A freed prisoner still flinches at the sound of keys.

Romans 6, 7 and 8 are one argumentTextReadingThe “I still sin” chapter sits between two “you are free” chapters.
The text

Romans 6: you died with him, so sin isn’t your master

For sin shall not have dominion over you: for ye are not under the law, but under grace

Romans 7: the flesh is still there, and willpower loses to it

O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death?

Romans 8: the Spirit does what willpower couldn’t

the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus hath made me free from the law of sin and death

Our reading

Romans 7 isn’t the end of the story. It sits between two chapters that say you’re free, on purpose. And the wretched man gets his answer in the very next verse: I thank God through Jesus Christ our Lord.

Flesh and SpiritTextReadingThe fight is real. Notice which side your wanting is on.
The text

For the flesh lusteth against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh: and these are contrary the one to the other: so that ye cannot do the things that ye would (Galatians 5:17).

Our reading

The fight is between what you now want and what you used to practise. It isn’t two equal selves. Read the verse again: the flesh stops you doing what you would. A believer’s wanting has already changed sides.

Perfected, and being made holy, in one sentenceTextHebrews 10:14 holds both, on purpose.
The text

In Greek, “perfected” is teteleiōken, a perfect tense: finished, and it stays finished. “Them that are sanctified” is tous hagiazomenous, a present participle: those who are being made holy.

One offering, one group of people, one sentence. You are complete and in progress at the same time, and neither cancels the other.

Consequences are not condemnationTextReadingA child who breaks a window is still a child.
The text
  • whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap Galatians 6:7
  • God dealeth with you as with sons Hebrews 12:7
  • grieve not the holy Spirit of God, whereby ye are sealed unto the day of redemption Ephesians 4:30
Our reading

Sin still costs. It still hurts you and the people near you, and the Father still corrects his children. None of that is wrath, and none of it touches your standing.

Look at Ephesians 4:30 again: even while you grieve him, you are sealed. The difference between judgment and correction is what Judgment is about. A child who breaks a window isn’t un-adopted. He’s grounded.

Does sinning prove I was never saved?TextDisputedReading1 John 3 read three ways.
The text

Whosoever is born of God doth not commit sin (1 John 3:9).

And earlier in the same letter: If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves (1 John 1:8), and if any man sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous (1 John 2:1).

Where Christians differ
  • Strictly. Ongoing sin shows the faith was never real.
  • A way of life. The Greek present tense describes the direction someone walks, not every stumble.
  • The new nature. What is born of God in you doesn’t sin, even while the flesh still does.
Our reading

The same letter says believers sin, and that they have an advocate when they do. You can’t have an advocate for a case that ends your adoption. John wrote so that his readers may know that ye have eternal life (1 John 5:13), not so they’d spend it wondering.

09

What is repentance?

The Greek is metanoia, a change of mind. The Hebrew is shuv (שׁוּב), to turn around, to come home.

Neither word means grovelling, and neither means paying something back. Repentance is the turn itself: from trusting yourself to trusting him.

And Paul says what produces it: the goodness of God leadeth thee to repentance (Romans 2:4). Not his threats. His kindness.

The word, and what it isn’tTextReadingJudas regretted. That’s a different word.
The text

repent ye, and believe the gospel (Mark 1:15).

When Judas repented himself (Matthew 27:3), Matthew uses a different verb, metamelomai: to regret, to feel sorry afterwards. It changed nothing.

Paul draws the line: godly sorrow worketh repentance to salvation not to be repented of: but the sorrow of the world worketh death (2 Corinthians 7:10).

Our reading

Sorrow can lead to repentance. It isn’t repentance. Repentance is the turning, and it can happen with dry eyes.

Repentance from what, to whatTextReadingThe first turn is away from rescuing yourself.
The text

Paul preached repentance toward God, and faith toward our Lord Jesus Christ (Acts 20:21). Hebrews calls it repentance from dead works, and of faith toward God (Hebrews 6:1).

Our reading

Repentance and faith are one movement with two names: turning from, and trusting to. The first repentance isn’t swapping a list of bad things for a list of good ones. It’s turning from dead works, the attempt to save yourself, to faith in the one who already has.

The cross deals with the sins. Repentance is the turn that receives the cross. The whole coming-home story is told in Out of the Spiral.

The prodigal, as a picture of itTextReadingHe came to himself, he got up, and his father ran.
Our reading

he came to himself is the change of mind. he arose, and came to his father is the turn.

He had rehearsed a speech that ended make me as one of thy hired servants (Luke 15:19), which is the works road in one sentence. He never gets to that line. The father talks over it.

The robe, the ring and the shoes are question 02’s list, told as a story.

Repenting after you believeTextReadingA child turning back toward a Father who never left.
The text

Jesus told Peter, on the night Peter would deny him, when thou art converted, strengthen thy brethren (Luke 22:32). And of the seven churches in Revelation, he told five to repent (Revelation 2-3).

Our reading

Believers keep turning. The direction is set, and the small corrections go on. But it’s a child turning back toward a Father who never left, not a stranger applying to get in again.

fruits meet for repentance (Matthew 3:8) is what the turn looks like from the outside.

10

Do I have to keep asking for forgiveness?

The forgiveness that saves you was given once, for everything, at the cross. Past sins and future ones.

Telling God the truth about your sin afterwards is honesty between a child and a Father. It isn’t a fresh application for a pardon you already have.

So if you sin and die before you get to say sorry, you go home. There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus (Romans 8:1) has no footnote about timing. He died for us that, whether we wake or sleep, we should live together with him (1 Thessalonians 5:10).

What’s actually bothering you?

Pick the one that’s closest.

Your answer will show here, with the verse it rests on.

I sinned and died before I asked

You go home. You aren’t kept by the speed of your apologies. You’re kept by him: they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand (John 10:28). The thief on the cross had no time to confess anything after “remember me,” and he was in paradise that day.

If I sin and die before I ask

I don’t feel saved

Feelings report on your soul, which is still being renewed. Your standing is a fact about your spirit. John wrote a whole letter so that believers may know that ye have eternal life (1 John 5:13), from what God said, not from how today felt.

Three tenses of one rescue

I keep doing the same thing

Then you’re in Romans 7, which sits between Romans 6 and 8 on purpose. The flesh is well practised, but your wanting has changed sides. Look up, not in: we have an advocate with the Father (1 John 2:1).

Romans 6, 7 and 8

I’m afraid I committed the unforgivable sin

You haven’t. That sin is a settled refusal of the Spirit’s witness to Jesus, and the person committing it isn’t afraid of it. Your fear is the Spirit still speaking, and you still listening. him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out (John 6:37).

Three readings, one agreement

Do I have to confess every day?

Confess, yes. It’s honest, it’s good, and it keeps you close. But confession isn’t what keeps you saved. The forgiveness was given once, for all of it: where remission of these is, there is no more offering for sin (Hebrews 10:18).

What 1 John 1:9 means

Can I lose it?

This is the most disputed question on the page, and both sides are shown below. We hold that those born of God are kept: he which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ (Philippians 1:6).

Our reading

“Having forgiven you all trespasses” is past tense, and it says all. Every sin you have committed since you believed was still in the future when he died for it. He paid for the whole life, not only the part you had lived by then.

He sat down because the work was finished.

1 John 1:9, the verse the daily-asking view rests onTextDisputedReadingThree honest readings of one well-loved verse.
Where Christians differ
  • Fellowship. Your salvation is settled, but confessing each sin restores closeness with God and receives fresh forgiveness for it. This is the most common evangelical reading, and many Christians live it well.
  • An invitation in. John is writing to a mixed room that includes people who say we have no sin (1 John 1:8). Verse 9 is the door in for them. To believers he writes your sins are forgiven you for his name's sake (1 John 2:12).
  • Agreement. The Greek for confess, homologeō, means to say the same thing. Confessing is agreeing with God about what happened, not paying for it.
Our reading

Forgiveness is complete, and confession is telling the truth to someone who already knows and has already paid. It’s good, it’s right, it clears the air, and it keeps you honest. It isn’t what keeps you saved.

Whichever of the three readings you hold, none of them says a believer who dies mid-sentence is lost.

The Lord’s Prayer, and the servant who wouldn’t forgiveTextDisputedReading“Forgive us, as we forgive.” Before the cross, and after it.
The text

forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors, and then if ye forgive not men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses (Matthew 6:12, 6:15). The parable of the unforgiving servant ends the same way (Matthew 18:23-35).

Where Christians differ

Some read Matthew 6:15 as a standing condition: God’s forgiveness depends on your forgiving others. Others note Jesus taught it before the cross, and read it as describing what a forgiven person becomes.

Our reading

We read it through Paul, writing after the cross: forgiving one another, even as God for Christ's sake hath forgiven you (Ephesians 4:32). Forgiven, therefore forgive. Not forgive, in order to be forgiven.

An unforgiving believer is a real problem. But it’s a symptom of forgetting the debt, not a trapdoor.

Where Christians differ

Whole traditions, Wesleyan, Catholic and Orthodox among them, hold that a believer can finally turn away and be lost. Reformed and many Baptist churches hold that the born again are kept to the end.

Our reading

We hold that those born of God are kept. The warning passages describe the refusal question 04 is about: a deliberate, final turning from Christ himself, not a believer’s bad week, or bad year.

But these are serious texts, taken seriously by serious Christians, and they aren’t shortened here.

11

How do I live it out?

Not by trying harder. By believing what’s already true, and moving from there.

reckon ye also yourselves to be dead indeed unto sin, but alive unto God (Romans 6:11): count it so.

Walk in the Spirit, and ye shall not fulfil the lust of the flesh (Galatians 5:16): that’s a promise, not a threat.

work out your own salvation, For it is God which worketh in you (Philippians 2:12-13): out, not for.

You aren’t becoming God’s child by behaving. You’re learning to behave like the child you already are.

Which way the arrow pointsReadingBehave, belong, become. Or the other way round.
Our reading

Religion

BehaveBelongBecome

Grace

BecomeBelongBehave

Religion says behave well enough, and you’ll belong, and one day you’ll become someone God accepts. Grace runs it the other way: you become his child when you believe, you belong from that moment, and the behaviour grows out of it.

It’s the same order as every letter Paul wrote.

Four verbsTextReadingReckon, yield, walk, abide.
The text, and what each verb asks
  • Reckon. reckon ye also yourselves to be dead indeed unto sin Romans 6:11
    Count what God says as true, before you feel it.
  • Yield. yield yourselves unto God, as those that are alive from the dead Romans 6:13
    Report to your new master, like a freed man turning up for his first day.
  • Walk. If we live in the Spirit, let us also walk in the Spirit Galatians 5:25
    One step, then another. Walking is ordinary.
  • Abide. As the branch cannot bear fruit of itself, except it abide in the vine; no more can ye, except ye abide in me John 15:4
    Fruit is what a branch does when it stays attached.

Don’t stay down to prove you’re sorry. Staying down is the flesh’s favourite kind of religion.
