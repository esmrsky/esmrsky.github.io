Calling · gifts · purpose

Given

You did not choose your gifts and you cannot earn them. This page reads what Scripture actually says about them, slowly, and helps you notice yours.

What this page will and won't do

  • It will put the four gift lists side by side, so you can see what they share and where they differ.
  • It will show people Scripture names as gifted, including where they struggled.
  • It will show how each gift bends when the person using it is bound, and let you trace a struggle back to the gift underneath.
  • It will give you six honest questions and a way to test the answers with people who know you.
  • It won't score you or assign you a type. The New Testament never does either, and the tests that try are weaker than they look (see the disputes).
01

Three words, untangled

Calling, gifts and purpose get used as if they meant the same thing. They don't, and most of the anxiety around "finding my calling" comes from asking one of them to do the work of the other two.

The outer ring

Purpose

What every life in Christ is for. It is not hidden and it is not unique to you: love God, love your neighbor, and be made like Jesus along the way. Your gifts and your calling serve this; they never replace it.

The middle ring

Calling

In the New Testament the word is almost always about being called to Christ: into his people, his life, his way. Paul also speaks of the situation God has assigned each person, a job, a household, a town. Os Guinness names these the primary and the secondary calling: first to Someone, then to somewhere.

The inner ring

Gifts

Abilities the Spirit hands out, as he decides, so that other people are served. The Greek word Paul uses most, charisma, is built on charis, grace. A gift is grace taking a shape in you for someone else's sake.

No list is complete.

Twenty-one distinct gifts across four passages (1 Corinthians 12 has two lists), and none appears in every list; prophecy comes closest, in four of the five. Paul is giving samples, not a menu. Elsewhere he calls staying single a gift (1 Cor 7:7), and Peter puts hospitality right beside the gift passage (1 Pet 4:9).

Ordinary and startling sit together.

Showing mercy sits next to prophecy; helping sits next to miracles. Nothing in the lists ranks the spectacular above the practical, and 1 Corinthians 12 argues against doing so.

Some are people, some are abilities.

Romans 12 and 1 Corinthians 12:8–10 name what people do. Ephesians 4 names people Christ gives to the church. Both are gifts; they are not the same kind of thing.

Romans 12

Humility in one body

The list comes right after "do not think of yourself more highly than you ought" (12:3). The point is sober self-assessment and getting on with it: if it is serving, serve.

1 Corinthians 12

Unity, against ranking

Written to a church proud of tongues. One Spirit, many gifts, each "for the common good" (12:7); the parts that seem weaker are indispensable (12:22).

Ephesians 4

People given to equip

The ascended Christ gives these people "to equip his people for works of service" (4:12). The goal is maturity and unity, not a title.

1 Peter 4

Stewards, under pressure

A suffering church, told the end is near. Two broad kinds of gift, speaking and serving, both done "so that in all things God may be praised" (4:11).

03

What gifts are for

Paul's longest teaching on gifts runs across three chapters, and the middle one is not about gifts at all. Chapter divisions came later, but the shape is his: the gifts, then love, then the gifts again, now measured by love.

Ch. 12Many gifts, one Spirit, one body. Every part needed.
Ch. 13Without love, the most impressive gift is noise.
Ch. 14Prefer what builds others up. Everything done in order.

A working test that falls out of this: a gift shows itself less in how it feels to you than in what it does for the people on the receiving end.

04

The Old Testament: precedent, not personality

Long before Pentecost, God fills particular people for particular work. These stories are precedents for gifting. What they are not is a set of personality types to sort yourself into.

A real precedent

Bezalel, the craftsman

The first person the Bible describes as filled with the Spirit of God is an artisan, not a priest or a prophet. The gift is skill with metal, stone and wood, and later the ability to teach it (Exod 35:34).

A real precedent

The Twelve, called to be with him

When Jesus appoints the twelve apostles, the first purpose given is presence, and only then mission. The order is worth keeping: the calling is to him before it is to a task.

Often misread

The blessings of the twelve tribes

Jacob's blessing (Genesis 49) and Moses' (Deuteronomy 33) speak about the tribes' futures: land, history, the line of kings coming from Judah. They are prophecies about peoples, not descriptions of temperaments. Joseph, for instance, is "a fruitful vine" in Genesis 49:22 and a bull only in Deuteronomy 33:17. An earlier project on this site, Twelve, built a typology from them; it now sits under the other projects for that reason.

05

Case files

People whose gifting the text itself names. Watch three things in each: what the gift was, who recognized it, and where it strained. Almost none of them discovered their gift alone, and none of them were free of failure.

Bezalel

Exodus 31 and 35–38 · craftsman of the tabernacle

The gift
Artistic skill, named as the Spirit's filling: designs in gold, silver and bronze, cutting stones, carving wood (Exod 31:3–5).
Recognized by
God names him to Moses, and Moses announces it to the whole community (Exod 35:30).
Multiplied
He and Oholiab are given "the ability to teach others" (Exod 35:34). The gift did not stay in one pair of hands.
Worth noticing
Nothing about this work is "spiritual" in the narrow sense. Making things well, for others, is gifted work.

Moses

Exodus 3–4 · the reluctant spokesman

The calling
To bring Israel out of Egypt (Exod 3:10). The calling came before any sense of fitness for it.
His objection
"I am slow of speech and tongue" (Exod 4:10). God answers that he made human mouths, and then gives him Aaron to speak (4:11–16).
Recognized by
God directly, and the signs given for the elders of Israel (4:29–31).
Worth noticing
What Moses felt he lacked did not cancel the call; it was met with another person. Feeling unsuited is not, on its own, evidence of the wrong path.

Deborah

Judges 4–5 · prophet who was leading Israel

The gift
Prophecy and judgment: Israelites came to her under the palm to have disputes decided (Judg 4:4–5).
Recognized by
The people, who kept coming; and Barak, who would not go to battle without her (4:8).
Used for
She passes on God's command to Barak rather than taking the army herself, and then goes with him (4:6–9).
Worth noticing
Her song afterwards (Judges 5) gives the credit to God and to the tribes who showed up. The gift points away from itself.

Stephen

Acts 6–7 · one of the seven

The task
Chosen to make sure widows got their share of the daily food (Acts 6:1–5), a practical job.
Recognized by
The whole community chose him as "full of faith and of the Holy Spirit" (6:5), and the apostles laid hands on them (6:6).
Then
He also did "great wonders and signs" and argued with a wisdom his opponents could not stand up to (6:8–10).
Worth noticing
Faithfulness in serving tables is where his other gifts showed. Small assignments are not a detour from calling.

Barnabas

Acts 4, 9, 11, 13, 15 · "son of encouragement"

The gift
Encouragement, so marked that the apostles renamed him for it (Acts 4:36). He vouches for Saul when no one trusts him (9:27).
Recognized by
The church at Antioch, where the Spirit says "Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul" while they worship and fast (13:2).
Used for
Seeing grace in others: at Antioch "he saw what the grace of God had done" and was glad (11:23).
Where it strained
He and Paul split so sharply over giving Mark a second chance that they parted ways (15:37–39). The same instinct that made him an encourager cost him a partnership. Later Paul calls Mark useful (2 Tim 4:11).

Priscilla and Aquila

Acts 18 · Romans 16 · tentmakers

The station
Tentmakers by trade, like Paul, who stayed and worked with them (Acts 18:2–3).
The gift
When they heard Apollos, they "invited him to their home and explained to him the way of God more adequately" (18:26). Teaching, done privately and well.
Recognized by
Paul, who calls them co-workers who risked their lives for him and hosted a church in their house (Rom 16:3–5).
Worth noticing
Their gift ran through their ordinary life: a trade, a home, a table. The secondary calling was the vehicle for the gift.

Apollos

Acts 18 · 1 Corinthians 1–4

The gift
"A learned man, with a thorough knowledge of the Scriptures", who spoke with great fervor (Acts 18:24–25).
The gap
He "knew only the baptism of John" (18:25). Real gifting, incomplete understanding.
What happened
He accepted correction from two tentmakers, then "was a great help to those who by grace had believed" (18:27).
Where it strained
His gifts drew a fan club in Corinth ("I follow Apollos", 1 Cor 1:12), which Paul has to dismantle: "I planted the seed, Apollos watered it, but God has been making it grow" (3:6).

Timothy

1 and 2 Timothy

The gift
Given "through prophecy when the body of elders laid their hands on you" (1 Tim 4:14).
Recognized by
The elders, and Paul, who had watched him for years (Phil 2:22).
The instruction
"Do not neglect your gift" (1 Tim 4:14) and "fan into flame the gift of God" (2 Tim 1:6). A gift can be received and still left cold.
Where it strained
Young and, it seems, timid: "Don't let anyone look down on you because you are young" (1 Tim 4:12); "the Spirit God gave us does not make us timid" (2 Tim 1:7).
06

The same gift, turned

Every gift has a free form and a bound form. The gift is the same in both; what changes is what it is being used to get. Read the map from the gift to see where it goes wrong, or start from a struggle and trace it back to the gift underneath.

A gift does not check your motives before it works. Paul says God's "gifts and his call are irrevocable" (Rom 11:29). So a gift can keep working in someone whose heart has drifted, and the fact that it works proves nothing about them.

The reverse holds too. When a gifted person falls, the fall does not prove the gift was fake. Usually the gift was real and the person carrying it had changed. That is why the case files above each have a row for where it strained.

The rock, twiceMoses strikes the rock at God's word and water comes (Exod 17:6). Later he strikes it again in anger, against the instruction, and water still comes. That time it costs him the land (Num 20:8–12).
"I never knew you"People who prophesied and drove out demons in Jesus' name are still turned away (Matt 7:22–23). The gifts worked. The relationship wasn't there.
Without loveProphecy, knowledge and mountain-moving faith, without love, add up to nothing (1 Cor 13:2).
The way backNot guilt. "Godly sorrow brings repentance" (2 Cor 7:10). The gift stays; the motive is what gets cleaned.

Apostle

FreeStarts what God asks for, builds the structure that lets it last, and hands it on.

BoundStarts things to prove something. Leaves before the thing is done, again and again, or stays long after God said go and won't let anyone else hold it.

Welch tells this from his own life: after losing something he built, he started quitting new things while they were going well, before they could be lost. When he forced himself to stay, he started controlling everyone instead. Both, he says, were "the same unhealth."

Prophet

FreeHears what God is saying and says it, including the hard parts, in love, and stays for what follows.

BoundRuns and hides from what they were sent to say. Or swings the other way and gives everyone good words, to be accepted.

Scripture's prophets run more than any other kind of person: Jonah heads the other way (Jonah 1:3), Elijah flees after Carmel (1 Kgs 19:3), Jeremiah tries to stop speaking and can't (Jer 20:9). The flattering side is in Jeremiah too: "'Peace, peace,' they say, when there is no peace" (Jer 6:14).

Training

How you use it

Skill. A real gift used clumsily still needs practice, feedback and correction. Priscilla and Aquila explained the way to Apollos "more adequately," and he got better (Acts 18:26).

Healing

Why you use it

No amount of skill fixes a motive. If a gift is being used to be seen, to stay safe or to stay in control, more training only makes it a sharper tool. Welch's four places to look:

  • The past: how you carry what already happened.
  • The future: what you expect is coming.
  • Permission: whether you let yourself succeed.
  • Armor: how much you protect yourself from being known.
A thirty-second check

Before you use it

Welch's practice, at the moment an opportunity shows up: remember Christ crucified (1 Cor 2:2), and that he chose it (John 10:18). Then ask, "How does he want me to use this?" He says the motives start falling off when you do it often.

Forms marked Episode come from Taylor Welch, "Discovering your Spiritual Gifts (dormant gifts, domains & callings)," The Deep End. They are one teacher's observations, not Scripture, and his ten gifts are his own merge of the Romans 12 and Ephesians 4 lists. Scripture means the text cited carries it; This page's reading means neither did, so hold it loosely. The Convergence Atlas maps the same idea, a core frequency and its inverse, through Welch's twelve tribal archetypes; this page stays with the gift lists, for the reason in 04.

07

Your path: six questions, no score

The case files share a pattern: a need, someone stepping into it, fruit other people could see, and a community that named what it saw. These questions follow that pattern. Your answers stay in this browser only; nothing is sent anywhere.

Where has fruit shown up that other people noticed?

Not what you enjoy, and not what you hope you are good at: what has actually helped someone, by their account. Barnabas was renamed by others, not by himself.

What need keeps catching your attention?

Nehemiah heard that Jerusalem's wall was broken down and wept for days (Neh 1:3–4). A need that keeps returning to you is worth taking seriously. It is a signal, not a proof.

What do people keep asking of you?

Requests are a form of recognition. If people bring you their spreadsheets, their sick relatives or their questions about Romans, they are telling you something.

Where does serving leave you tired but more yourself?

A weak signal, held lightly: Moses did not want his calling, and Timothy found his hard. Still, many people notice that some kinds of service drain them in a good way and others hollow them out.

What is your station right now?

Paul tells the Corinthians to live out their calling in the situation they are in (1 Cor 7:17–24). Before asking what you should be doing in ten years, ask who is in front of you this week.

What do you use your gift to get?

Section 06 in one question. Under pressure, a gift starts working for you instead of for others: to be seen, to stay safe, to stay in control. Paul opens the Romans 12 list with "sober judgment" for this reason (Rom 12:3).

Next 90 days

Serve in a real need

Pick one need from your answers and serve in it regularly, somewhere real. Gifts are discovered mostly in use, not in reflection.

Along the way

Ask three people

Ask three people who have watched you serve, ideally family, a close friend and someone who has seen you at work: "What did you see? Where did it help? Where did it miss?" Timothy's gift was named by people who knew him. Then compare. What they see and you see too is confirmed. What they see and you don't is hidden. What you sense and they haven't seen yet is unexpressed, and often waiting on courage.

Before and after

Ask God to describe you

Welch's closing advice, and the simplest step here: ask God how he would describe you to a friend, and write down what comes. Then test it like anything else (1 Thess 5:21). No profile, including this page, gets the last word on who you are.

At the end

Review it with your church

Sit down with a pastor or a mature friend and look at the fruit together, with "sober judgment" (Rom 12:3). Then keep going, adjust, or try another need.

07

Where Christians honestly disagree

Faithful readers of the same texts land in different places on these. Each is set out with the strongest case on both sides and what nearly everyone agrees on. This page does not settle them for you.

Do the miraculous gifts continue today?

Divides traditions

Prophecy, tongues, healing and miracles. The standard map is Are Miraculous Gifts for Today? Four Views (ed. Wayne Grudem, 1996): Richard Gaffin (cessationist), Robert Saucy (open but cautious), Sam Storms (third wave) and Douglas Oss (Pentecostal), each answering the others.

They ceased with the apostles

  • The church is "built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets" (Eph 2:20): a foundation is laid once.
  • Signs confirmed the first witnesses' message (Heb 2:3–4); with Scripture complete, new revelation is not needed.
  • Thomas Schreiner, once a continuationist, now argues a careful version of this, and says he could be wrong.

They continue until Christ returns

  • "When completeness comes" (1 Cor 13:10) most naturally means Christ's return, when we see "face to face" (13:12).
  • Paul tells the church to "eagerly desire gifts of the Spirit, especially prophecy" and not to forbid tongues (1 Cor 14:1, 39). No text says they will stop sooner.
  • Grudem and Storms argue this; D. A. Carson's Showing the Spirit (1987) is a careful, measured reading of 1 Corinthians 12–14 in this direction.

Common ground: every gift must be tested against Scripture (1 Thess 5:20–21), used for building others up, and done in order. Both sides agree the ordinary gifts on the lists (teaching, serving, mercy, giving) are very much for today.

Is "fivefold ministry" a model for every church?

Live debate

Ephesians 4:11 names apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors and teachers. Alan Hirsch's APEST framework reads these as five functions every church needs and many believers share.

Yes, as functions

  • They are given "until we all reach unity in the faith" (4:13), a goal not yet reached, so the giving is ongoing.
  • Churches led only by teachers and shepherds can lose their outward, pioneering edge; naming all five helps.
  • Hirsch himself warns against turning the fivefold into a hierarchy of powerful offices, as some "apostolic" movements have.

Read it more narrowly

  • Apostles in the strict sense were eyewitnesses of the risen Christ (1 Cor 9:1; 15:8) and part of the one-time foundation (Eph 2:20).
  • The list may be four, not five: the Greek joins "pastors and teachers" in a way many read as one role, though grammarians disagree.
  • The passage names leaders given to the church to equip everyone else, not five personality types for every believer (Ty Grigg's critique).

Common ground: the point of 4:11 is 4:12, equipping "his people for works of service". Whatever you call the roles, ministry is meant to spread out to everyone, not concentrate in a few.

Do spiritual gift tests work?

Evidence is thin

The case for them

  • They start a conversation many people never have, and give language to things they had not noticed.
  • Used as a first guess and then tested in real service, they can point somewhere worth trying.

What the research found

  • Psychometric studies in the Journal of Psychology and Theology (1989, 1994) found poor to moderate reliability for most subscales.
  • Factor analysis did not support the fourteen or seven separate gifts the tests claim; the answers clustered into about two or three groups.
  • No inventory appears to have been checked against real-world outcomes, like whether high "teaching" scorers actually teach well. Most measure preference and self-image.

Common ground: the New Testament never asks anyone to identify their gift before serving. People find their gifts by serving and having others confirm the fruit, which is what the path above is built on.

Are the Romans 12 gifts personality types?

Speculative

Popular teaching since the late twentieth century calls the seven gifts of Romans 12 "motivational" or "redemptive" gifts: a built-in drive that shapes how a person sees everything. Typologies based on the twelve tribes work similarly.

Why people find it helpful

  • It explains why people respond so differently to the same situation, which is a real observation.
  • It gives people a way to value temperaments unlike their own.

Why it outruns the text

  • Paul introduces them as "different gifts, according to the grace given to each of us" (Rom 12:6): grace given, not wiring at birth.
  • The passage gives each gift one line of instruction, not a profile of personality, strengths and weaknesses.
  • Nothing in Romans 12 says each person has exactly one, and the lists elsewhere mix in gifts no temperament explains, like healing or faith.

Common ground: people really are different, and the body needs the differences (1 Cor 12:17–20). Hold any typology as a lens, never as a verdict on who you are.

Is my calling my job?

Mostly agreement

Every station is a calling

  • The Reformers recovered the idea that a farmer or a mother serves God as truly as a monk. Paul tells slaves and free people alike to live out their calling where they are (1 Cor 7:20–24).
  • "Whatever you do, work at it with all your heart, as working for the Lord" (Col 3:23).

Some callings are particular

  • The Spirit sets apart Barnabas and Saul for a specific work (Acts 13:2), and Paul calls it good to aspire to be an overseer (1 Tim 3:1).
  • Some people do sense a summons to a particular work, and the church is right to test it.

Common ground: the primary calling is to Christ and belongs to every believer. Jobs and roles are where it is lived out, and they can change without the calling changing.
