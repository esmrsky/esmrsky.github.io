Four questions that sort any of these

When two terms feel interchangeable, one of these usually separates them.

  1. OneWhat's being harvested? Status, sympathy, engagement, an argument won, control, or access. Pickme harvests status. Sadfishing harvests sympathy. Rage bait harvests engagement. Sealioning harvests an argument. Love bombing harvests control. Simping harvests access.
  2. TwoWho's the target — a person or a crowd? The same act renames itself depending on the answer. Self-deprecation to a friend is fishing; to a timeline it's sadfishing. Devotion to one person is simping; to an audience it's clout chasing.
  3. ThreeDoes the target lose anything? This separates the annoying from the harmful. Humblebragging costs you nothing. Gaslighting costs you your read on reality. Both are on this page; they are not the same order of thing.
  4. FourIs it repeated, or a moment? Most of these describe a one-off move. The intimate-control family describes a pattern — and a single instance genuinely doesn't qualify. One overenthusiastic week is not love bombing.
Three things this page can't do for you

Limits worth stating plainly

Almost all of these describe intent, which nobody can observe. The behavior is visible; the motive is inferred. That inference is wrong often enough that these words work far better as self-audit than as verdict. "Pickme," "virtue signaling," and "playing the victim" are the three most reliably misfired.

Clinical terms are not clinical here. Gaslighting, love bombing, DARVO, and trauma dumping came out of abuse and therapy literature and have been badly diluted by casual use. Someone disagreeing with your memory is not gaslighting you. Real patterns of coercive control are a different conversation than a slang glossary, and worth taking to someone qualified.

Origins vary in solidity. Where a coinage is documented I've named it. Where the term is descriptive or the origin is contested, the row says so — I'd rather flag a gap than invent an etymology.
