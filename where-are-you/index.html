01

Maturity isn't a ladder.

Most church maps go up. Learn more, serve more, lead more.

But every framework that lasted had to add a room for the part where it all stops working — and that room is where maturity actually happens.

The longer version

Scripture does sometimes talk like a ladder — Peter stacks eight rungs in three verses. Churches love that, because a ladder is teachable, printable and measurable.

The trouble is what happens around year fifteen. A mature, obedient, genuinely converted person finds the practices that worked for a decade have stopped working. Prayer flattens. Certainty drains. Nothing obviously sinful has happened, and yet the machinery has stopped.

Every tradition that lasted built a room for this. John of the Cross called it the dark night. Hagberg and Guelich drew it as a literal wall. Teresa put it in the middle of the castle, not outside it. Rohr calls the fall necessary. Scazzero built a whole discipleship model around it.

Most churches have no name for it, so they reach for the nearest one they've got: backsliding. That single mislabel is one of the most reliable ways to lose a person who was about to grow.

The six positions.

Read across, not down. Every card carries the same six rows in the same order — the tell, what it feels like, where it goes wrong, the doorway out, and what to do and not do if you're walking with someone there.

Position 04

The Wall

Eleven maps, eighteen centuries, no shared vocabulary — and they disagree about nearly everything except this. There is a place where the machinery stops, and it is not a malfunction. Here is the same spot on every one of them.

c. AD 90

1 John 2:12–14

no room for it

Three groups, sorted by what they know. John sets “young men” beside “fathers” and never describes the crossing — the oldest map here has the gap in it and no name for it.

3rd–16th c.

The Three Ways · Interior Castle

The passive purifications

Mansions four to six, where prayer stops being something you do and becomes something done to you. The only tradition that treats losing control of your own prayer as the mechanism rather than the obstacle.

c. 1578

John of the Cross — Dark Night

The night of sense, then of spirit

Two nights, not one, and they need different answers: the first strips the sweetness off prayer, the second takes apart the self that was praying. Still the most exact vocabulary anyone has produced, written from a prison cell.

1930s

The Navigators' Wheel

no room for it

A hub and four spokes with no time axis at all. It can tell you which spoke is slack. It has nothing to say about the year the wheel will not turn.

1981

Fowler — Stages of Faith

Stage 4 · individuative-reflective

The only one that frames this as authority relocating rather than faith failing — you stop holding the group's beliefs and start holding your own. Fowler is candid about the price: costly, and often lonely.

1987

Peck — The Different Drum

Stage 3 · skeptic, individual

A psychiatrist's addition, and the most useful sentence on this page: from outside, stage 4 is indistinguishable from stage 2. The people furthest through get mistaken for the people who never left.

1989

Hagberg & Guelich

The Wall

The only map that refuses to make it a stage — it is drawn as a passage cutting across 3 into 4. Their verb is the giveaway. The stages humble, ground, reward, unsettle, transform and transcend us; the Wall unmasks us.

2006

Scazzero — Emotionally Healthy

Stage 4 · the wall and the inward journey

Welds this to emotional maturity — the argument that ordinary discipleship leaves the deep parts of a life untouched — and then does the unglamorous thing, prescribing a rule of life sturdy enough to survive the crossing.

2007

REVEAL — Willow Creek

no room for it

A four-point continuum with no crisis anywhere on it — and then its own survey walked into one. Church activity predicts growth early and stops predicting it later, at about the point every other map here puts the Wall.

2011

Rohr — Falling Upward

The necessary fall

The strongest form of the claim: the container is not damaged by breaking, it is finished by it. The first half of life builds the thing; the second half finds out what it was for.

2014

Spader — 4 Chair Discipling

no room for it

Four chairs, straight up, built to answer what you do on Tuesday. It is the best next-step tool here, and it has no chair for the person who has stopped being able to sit in any of them.

Reading the gap

Three of the four with no room for this were built for the first half of the journey — a maintenance diagram, a growth survey and a next-step ladder. Each is very good at the job it took, and each stops being about you at exactly the place where you are. The fourth is two verses long, and it was written before anyone thought the question needed a diagram.

Before you self-diagnoseThe difference between a dark night and clinical depression is a medical question and you should not guess at it. They look identical from the inside and are treated completely differently. Spiritual direction and therapy aren't competitors here — get both, and tell each one you have the other.

04

It's a person, not a programme.

Asked to choose between a thought-provoking sermon and building strong relationships with other Christians, 57% of Gen Z and Millennial churchgoers pick the relationships. Across all Christians it's closer — 48% to 42%, with 10% unsure. Every framework on this page arrives somewhere near the same place from a different direction, and almost none of what they describe can be delivered from a stage.

Who, specifically · 01

Someone half a step ahead

Not a mentor assigned by a programme. Someone close enough to your situation to answer a second question, and unimpressive enough that you'll actually ask it. Spader's four chairs make this the entire engine — you learn it working alongside the teacher before you're ever released to do it without them.

Who, specifically · 02

Someone level with you

One person who gets the unedited version — not the version with the ending already attached to make it bearable to say. Hagberg and Guelich built their six stages out of interviews rather than theory, which is to say out of people who had somewhere to say the unflattering part out loud.

Who, specifically · 03

Someone behind you

The fastest way to find out what you actually believe is to be asked by somebody who started last month. It's also the standing correction to Position 05's occupational hazard — contempt for the stage you have just come out of, which is the reliable proof you haven't.

How this gets ruinedThe moment a church accepts that relationships matter, it builds a programme called Relationships — assigned partners, group placement, a term of hospitality events — and gets the shape of the thing without the thing. What the number describes isn't an activity you can schedule on somebody else's behalf. It's a small number of people with standing to say hard things and the patience to still be there in a year. REVEAL's awkward finding is the invoice for getting this backwards: past the early stages, more church activity stopped predicting growth.

06

Where to go next.

Twenty-two sources, each with its bias named. Filter by what you actually want.

Caveat 01

Not a ranking of God's love

Hagberg and Guelich are emphatic: later positions don't mean greater closeness to God, only a different experience of the same access.

Caveat 02

Not linear, and not once

You'll cycle. Most people re-enter the wall two or three times in a life, and people move back and forth between positions rather than graduating out of them.

Caveat 03

Not uniform across your life

It's very common to be at 05 in suffering and 02 in money, or 06 with strangers and 01 with your own family. Locate yourself per domain.

Caveat 04

Not a diagnosis of anybody else

Used on another person without their invitation, this becomes a very efficient instrument of condescension.
