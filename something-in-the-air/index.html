Vibes, auras, and the powers over a place

Something in the air

Everyone reads a room before anybody speaks. Almost nobody agrees on what they are reading.

A friend, about a coffee shop “I don’t know. There’s just something in the air in there.”
Paul, about the same noun “…the ruler of the authority of the air” — tōn archōnta tēs exousias tou aēros

Two thousand years apart, the same three words. One of them is a shrug and the other is a claim about government. This page is about how to tell which one you are actually looking at — because the honest answer is usually neither of the things people reach for first.

01 Everybody does this

You knew before anyone spoke

Walk into a house you are thinking of renting and you will know in about four seconds. Walk into a family kitchen ten minutes after an argument and you will know before you have looked at a face.

This is not a fringe experience or a spiritual gift. It is the most ordinary thing in the world, and every language on earth has words for it.

Where people report it

A city, read blindfolded before the first sign. Open sea, flat and indifferent. A lake at dusk. Water you cannot see the bottom of. Buildings. Hospitals at 3am.

And the two identical branches of one chain coffee shop — one you go back to, one you do not, for reasons you have never managed to put in a sentence.

And the newest slang has made the whole thing quantitative. A generation that grew up online now measures charisma in aura. It is not a spiritual claim. But notice its shape: a field around a person, of variable strength, which other people can read at a distance and which the person can cultivate or squander. That is a folk theory, and it is remarkably specific for something everyone insists is just a joke.

Every one of them is on loan from the physical world. Except one.

Nobody says “I detected a moderate anomaly on entry.” They say heavy. They say thick, cold, off, close. The report arrives in the body, in the language of physics, before the mind has any account of it — and that is the single most important fact on this page, because it is exactly what makes the next step so easy to get wrong. A reading you receive as a body sensation is a reading you will explain with whatever cause is nearest to hand. If you are a materialist, that will be the air conditioning. If you are charismatic, that will be a spirit. Neither of you checked.

Aura, taken literally

The slang is a joke. There is a non-joking version, and it makes a different kind of claim: a coloured field around the body, of variable strength, readable by a sensitive, indicating personality or health. That is not a metaphor. It is a physical claim about a detectable field — which means, unlike almost everything else on this page, it can be tested directly.

It was, by a nine-year-old. Emily Rosa sat twenty-one experienced therapeutic-touch practitioners — people who work professionally with what they describe as the human energy field — on the far side of a cardboard screen with their hands through two holes. She held her own hand a few inches above one of theirs, at random, and asked which. They could not tell above chance, and the journal’s editor told patients to save their money.

The one claim anybody tested 21practitioners · no better than chance

A cardboard screen, two holes, a hand held a few inches above one of theirs. Designed as a fourth-grade science project.

Rosa, Rosa, Sarner & Barrett, JAMA 279 (1998).

Be precise about what that settles, because it is routinely over-claimed in both directions. It tests one specific, physical, well-specified claim, and that claim failed cleanly. It says nothing whatever about discernment, about the unseen realm, or about whether you can read a room — those are not detectable-field claims and were never on the screen. Anyone using this result to close the larger question is making the same filing error as everyone else, just quickly.

The more interesting point is that Scripture does not have this category either. There is no doctrine of coloured human energy fields anywhere in it. What it has instead is a completely different vocabulary — and not one word of it is a field. They are relations and moral conditions, things that exist between parties rather than radiating from one.

What Scripture says instead

presence · glory · spirit · peace · fear · holiness · defilement · oppression · light · darkness · aroma

Eleven words for what surrounds a person and fills a place. None of them is a field.

So the useful question was never “is aura real?” It is: what are you trying to describe, and does one of those words fit it better?

Usually one does, and usually it is more specific than the word it replaces.

And the same move, in newer clothes

Watch for four words that now turn up constantly in Christian teaching about atmosphere and perception — including in the title of a popular podcast episode about hearing God. Sometimes they are plainly metaphors. Sometimes they appear to be meant literally. The two are almost never distinguished, and the distinction is the whole thing, because it decides what would count as evidence.

Borrowed authority

frequency · vibration · resonance · energy

In physics each of these names a measurable quantity with units. Used of a room or a spirit, the units come along for the ride and nothing is being measured.

In physics these words name measurable quantities with units. Nothing in the biblical vocabulary above maps onto them, and no experiment of the Emily Rosa kind has established the ones being invoked. A metaphor cannot be wrong, which is a fine thing for a metaphor to be. A physics claim can be wrong, and ought to be phrased so that it could be. The word does borrowed work either way — it arrives carrying the authority of a laboratory — and noticing that is most of what you need.

02 What you were probably doing

Most of it is the room, and you

Before anything else gets a hearing, the boring explanations have to be given their turn — not because they are the truth, but because skipping them is how people end up certain about things they have never tested.

Here are the mechanisms that are actually known, with an honest mark on each for how well it is known. Two of these are much weaker than the way they usually get quoted.

Thin-slicing

Solid

You read people fast, unconsciously, and better than chance. Shown thirty seconds of silent video of a teacher they had never met, strangers predicted that teacher’s end-of-term student ratings. No words, no context, half a minute.

So when you walk into a room of people, you are running a very fast, very old instrument — and it does not tell you it is running.

Ambady & Rosenthal, Half a Minute, J. Personality & Social Psychology, 1993. Effect is robust; its size across domains is still argued over.

Emotional contagion

Solid

You catch affect. Faces, posture and voice tone move your own physiology within a fraction of a second, below any decision to be moved. Walk into a room where six people are tense and you will be tense, and you will not experience it as having been handed to you.

You will experience it as a property of the room.

Hatfield, Cacioppo & Rapson, Emotional Contagion, Current Directions in Psychological Science, 1993.

Misattributed arousal

Mixed

Body state arrives first; the explanation is fitted afterwards, from whatever cause happens to be nearby. The classic demonstration put men on a high swaying footbridge and found they read their own racing heart as attraction to the woman who met them at the end.

The bridge study has had a rough time in replication. The underlying point has not: you are the one supplying the cause, and you are doing it after the fact.

Schachter & Singer, 1962; Dutton & Aron, 1974. Treat the specific studies with care and the general mechanism as well supported.

The room’s own physics

Mixed

Nearly 700 volunteers walked through Hampton Court Palace and the South Bridge Vaults in Edinburgh, reporting anything strange. Reports clustered in the places that already had a haunted reputation — and also tracked plain physical variables: air movement, the size of the space, lighting, local magnetic field variance.

Which is genuinely two findings, and the second one is doing less work than people think.

Wiseman, Watt, Stevens, Greening & O’Keeffe, An investigation into alleged ‘hauntings’, British Journal of Psychology 94 (2003), 195–211.

Infrasound at 19 Hz

Mixed

Vic Tandy felt dread and cold sweat in his lab and saw a grey shape at the edge of vision that vanished when he turned. The next day a fencing blade clamped in a vice was visibly vibrating. A newly fitted extractor fan was producing a standing wave at around 19 Hz — close to a resonance of the human eyeball and chest.

He later measured the same frequency in a fourteenth-century cellar in Coventry with a long-standing reputation.

Tandy & Lawrence, Journal of the Society for Psychical Research, late 1990s. A vivid case study, not a controlled result — which matters enormously, for the reason below.

Neuroception

Mixed

Stephen Porges’ name for the nervous system’s continuous, pre-conscious appraisal of a place for safety or threat — reading faces, voice tone, posture, spacing and sound before any of it reaches thought, and shifting your physiological state accordingly. It is the closest thing anyone has to a mechanism for “I walked in and something was off.”

Which is why the caveat matters: the wider framework it belongs to, Polyvagal Theory, is seriously contested. Grossman and thirty-eight co-authors have argued its core claims about vagal anatomy, heart-rate measurement and evolutionary history are untenable; Porges says they are attacking a version of the theory he does not hold. Unresolved.

The narrow observation — that you appraise a room below awareness and feel the result in your body — is not what is in dispute. The specific machinery is. Take the first and hold the second loosely.

Expectancy

Solid

Being told a place is haunted reliably increases what people report there. This is the least glamorous finding in the whole literature and by some distance the strongest, and it applies just as hard to the believer as to the sceptic.

If someone tells you on the way in that the building has a history, your instrument has already been tampered with.

Consistent across the Wiseman work and the study below. The reputation of a place predicts experience in it.

This cuts both ways, and almost everyone only notices the edge pointed at their opponent.

If you were planning to say “it’s just infrasound” — that was tested directly, under controls, and it failed. The confident naturalistic explanation is not sitting on firmer ground than the thing it was dismissing; it is sitting on a case study and a plausible mechanism, which is roughly what the other side has.

But if you were planning to say “see, the physical explanations don’t work, so it must be spiritual” — read the result again. What predicted the experience was the person. Not the room, and not the realm. Whatever instrument you think you are carrying, it has a documented tendency to produce a reading whether or not there is anything to read.

That is not a reason to stop reading. It is a reason to stop trusting a single reading, from a single person, taken once, with no way of being wrong. Which, as it happens, is more or less what Scripture says about it too.

03 Why both answers are wrong in the same way

You were handed a shelf short

In 1982 a missiologist named Paul Hiebert published a short essay that explains almost everything about how this conversation goes, and it is still the most useful thing written on the subject.

Hiebert had been a missionary in India. A man came to him about a sick child, having already been to a local magician. Hiebert could talk about God — prayer, providence, the world to come. He could talk about medicine — infection, treatment, what the clinic was for. What he found he could not do was say anything at all about the thing the man had actually come to ask about, which sat squarely between those two.

The frame 13pages · Missiology, 1982

If you read one thing off this entire page, read that one. It explains why this argument keeps happening between people who cannot hear each other.

He went looking for the gap in his own head and found it, and it is drawn below. The Western picture of reality has two shelves where most of the world and most of history have three — and the missing one is the shelf every experience on this page actually lives on.

Hiebert called what happened to it the flaw of the excluded middle. Not denied — excluded. It was never argued away. There was simply no longer anywhere to put it.

A thing with no shelf does not stay unfiled. It gets forced onto one of the two shelves you do have.

That is the whole mechanism, and once you see it you will see it every time. Somebody walks out of a room and reports a middle-shelf experience. There is no middle shelf. So it goes down — “it smells weird in there, you’re tired” — or it goes up — “there is a principality over this city.” The two answers sound like opposite worldviews having an argument. They are the same filing error, made in opposite directions, by two people who were handed the same shelf short.

What Hiebert did not claim

That everything on the middle shelf is real. The evil eye and a territorial spirit are not established by having a shelf to sit on.

He spent the rest of his career warning against swapping a rationalist blind spot for a credulous one.

And notice what the missing shelf costs you: it is not mainly that you believe the wrong thing. It is that you cannot investigate. A category you do not have is a category you can neither affirm nor test nor rule out. The reason this subject is so full of people who are certain in both directions is that almost nobody has anywhere to put a maybe.

One more thing worth saying quickly. The two-tier picture is not human nature — it is local, recent and unusual. The Bible does not carve reality that way, the church did not for most of its history, and most of the world does not now. If the framework feels obvious to you, that is worth noticing about the framework.

04 The two cheap exits

Both of these are the same move

Here are the two exits the missing shelf produces. They look like opposite worldviews. They are the same refusal, facing different directions.

Exit one · the flat one

It’s the air conditioning

“You’re tired. You didn’t eat. It smells weird in there. That’s all it is.”

Sometimes that is exactly all it is, and the person saying it is right. The trouble is that they were going to say it either way — there is no room, no reading, no circumstance that would move them off it. A position that cannot lose is not a strong position. It is an unused one.

What it costs: a whole faculty, dismissed on principle. And a New Testament in which one of Paul’s longest and most concrete passages — a list of offices, a description of an active hostile authority, an instruction to armour up — quietly becomes a metaphor for having a difficult week.

Exit two · the spooky one

There’s something over this city

“I felt it the moment we crossed the county line. There’s a spirit of —— over this whole region.”

Sometimes there is something, and the person saying it is not making it up. The trouble is identical: nothing could show them otherwise. The reading confirms itself, the absence of confirmation is called warfare, and the one thing never on the table is that the feeling had an ordinary cause.

What it costs: more, actually. It makes people afraid of buildings. It relabels a treatable illness, an unforgiven grudge or a plain sin as an assignment from outside. And it flatters the reader that they are a precision instrument, at exactly the moment they have stopped calibrating.

An unfalsifiable reading is not a spiritual one. It is a lazy one.

That is the working rule of the rest of this page, and it is not a sceptic’s rule — it is the Bible’s. “Do not despise prophecies, but test everything; hold fast what is good” is a single instruction with two halves, and almost everybody keeps one of them.

05 What the text actually claims

There are offices up there

The intuition that the unseen world is organised the way the seen one is — jurisdictions, seniority, a chain of command, someone in charge of a region — is not a modern invention borrowed from fantasy novels. It is in the vocabulary, and once you see it you cannot unsee it.

First, the allotment

Deuteronomy 32:8–9 says that when the Most High gave the nations their inheritance and divided humanity, he fixed their boundaries “according to the number of” — and here the manuscripts part company. Most modern critical translations follow the scrolls, on the straightforward reasoning that a scribe might well soften “sons of God” into something safer, and would have no motive at all to go the other way.

Read that way, the verse is doing something specific: at the scattering, the nations were apportioned — and the next line says the LORD’s own portion was Jacob. Israel is the exception in a system where everyone else got assigned to somebody.

And then it is audited

Psalm 82. God stands in the divine assembly and passes judgment among the gods, for judging unjustly and showing partiality to the wicked. The sentence is that they will die like men.

Delegated authority, abused, and held to account.

Whatever these beings are, the accounting is the point. They held a delegated responsibility over a region’s justice, they abused it, and they are answerable for it. The office is real enough to be audited, which is a stranger claim than either of the two cheap exits has room for.

Then, the clearest text in the Bible on this

Daniel 10. An angel arrives three weeks late and explains why: “the prince of the kingdom of Persia withstood me twenty-one days, but Michael, one of the chief princes, came to help me.” He then says he is going back to fight the prince of Persia, and that the prince of Greece will come.

Four things in one chapter

A named spiritual authority. Attached to a political territory. In conflict with another such authority. On a timetable that tracks the succession of empires.

There is nothing metaphorical available here.

If you want a biblical basis for the idea that something stands over a place, this is it — and it is one chapter.

The thing to notice

Almost none of these are monster words. They are civil service words — office, jurisdiction, dominion, throne, magistracy. Paul reached for the vocabulary of provincial government to describe the unseen world, which is exactly the instinct that says there must be mayors up there and someone above the mayors.

And every single time he uses the list, he uses it to say the same thing. Christ made them. Christ disarmed them. Christ is seated above them. Not one item on the list can separate you from the love of God. The vocabulary is administrative and the verdict is settled.

06 And it is located

Places carry what is done in them

The other half of the intuition — that this attaches to somewhere, that a city or a room or a stretch of water can hold a character — is thinner in the text than the first half, but it is not absent. Six places where Scripture treats a location as having a spiritual weather.

Persia, then Greece

A prince withstands an angel for twenty-one days; Michael comes to help; and the next one is already named before he arrives. Territorial, political, and on a schedule.

Daniel 10:13, 20
Pergamum

“I know where you dwell, where Satan’s throne is.” Whatever that phrase means precisely — the altar of Zeus, the imperial cult, the healing shrine — it is said of one city and not the six others in the same letter.

Revelation 2:13
Philippi

A slave girl follows Paul with a “python spirit” — the Greek word is the serpent of Delphi, the oracle’s own name. Luke uses the local religious vocabulary because it is a local phenomenon.

Acts 16:16
Ephesus

Artemis is not a private opinion there; she is the economy. When the gospel spreads, the people who riot are the silversmiths, and the people who repent burn their own books.

Acts 19:19, 23–27
Canaan

The spies call it “a land that devours its inhabitants.” Leviticus says the land itself vomits out those who defile it. Land is treated as morally implicated in what happens on it.

Numbers 13:32 · Leviticus 18:25
Bethel

“Surely the LORD is in this place, and I did not know it.” The good version of the same phenomenon — and note the failure mode. Jacob slept straight through it.

Genesis 28:16–17

Notice what these have in common. In every case the spiritual character of the place is tied to what is being done there — worship, trade, oppression, bloodshed, an encounter with God. It is not a property of the soil. Ephesus feels like Artemis because Ephesus is full of people serving Artemis and getting paid for it. That is not a deflation of the claim. It is the shape of the claim.

The rule these six share

Not the soil — the practice. Worship, trade, oppression, bloodshed, an encounter with God.

Which is why the ordinary version needs no gift: a workplace organised around fear feels like fear.

And then there is the sea

Water is the one people ask about most — the flat indifference of open ocean, the hush over a lake at dusk, the specific unease of water you cannot see the bottom of. It is also where Scripture has the most to say and where the modern literalisation has travelled furthest, so it is worth doing properly.

What the text does. From the third sentence of the Bible the sea is not scenery. The earth is formless and void, darkness is over the face of tehom — the deep — and the Spirit hovers over the waters. Creation then proceeds by God ordering and bounding that. Israel’s neighbours told the same story as a fight. Scripture keeps the imagery and drops the struggle: God shatters the heads of Leviathan in the waters (Psalm 74:14), pierces the fleeing serpent (Isaiah 27:1), and in Job 41 Leviathan is simply a creature — unmasterable by men, entirely unremarkable to God.

The neighbours’ version

Baal against Yamm at Ugarit.
Marduk splitting Tiamat in the Enuma Elish.

Scripture borrows the imagery and drops the combat. Nothing is wrestled; the deep is bounded.

And the sea keeps meaning that. Daniel’s four beasts come up out of the sea. Revelation’s beast rises from it. When Jesus rebukes the wind and tells the sea to be still (Mark 4:39) and walks across it (Matthew 14:25), he is doing in a boat what the Psalms say God does to the deep — which is precisely why the disciples’ response is not gratitude but “who then is this?” Then the last word on it: “the sea was no more” (Revelation 21:1). That is not hydrology. It is the announcement that chaos is finished — and what replaces it is a river, clear as crystal.

So yes, water carries something in Scripture. What it carries is a meaning.

Now watch the same move happen again. Out of all that, contemporary deliverance teaching has grown a taxonomy: “marine spirits” and a “marine kingdom”, a distinct rank of demons resident in physical bodies of water.

A symbol became a species

Held responsible for lust, poverty, infertility and marital breakdown, anchored through ancestral covenants, and mapped onto Mami Wata and the water deities of Yoruba and Vodou tradition.

John Eckhardt, Marine Demons. Anchor verse: Job 41:34.

That is exactly what happened with territorial spirits in section 07, performed on different material. In Scripture the sea is what chaos and death and rebellion are like; it is not a habitat with a population and a management structure. And this version carries an extra cost the territorial version does not: it takes another culture’s water deities and files them as a demonic sub-rank, which is syncretism running in the opposite direction from the one it warns everybody else about.

The page’s own rule holds here. Place matters, and it matters because of what is done there and what it means. A lake is not a jurisdiction. And if you feel something standing at the edge of open water at dusk, you are not making it up — there are simply several ordinary reasons queuing ahead of any spirit.

Queuing ahead of a spirit

The scale. The horizon. The sound. And the very old, entirely sensible knowledge that you cannot see the bottom.

Which means the ordinary version is available to you constantly, with no gift required. A workplace organised around fear feels like fear. A house where something is being denied feels like the denial. You are not imagining that, and you do not need a vision to read it — you need to be willing to say out loud what the place is actually for.

07 What the text conspicuously does not do

Nobody in the Bible ever maps one

This is the part that gets skipped, and it is the part that decides whether any of the above is safe to hold. Four passages that everybody quotes, and the thing not one of them contains.

Daniel 10

Daniel never confronts the prince of Persia

He does not name him, bind him, rebuke him, or research him. He mourns and fasts for three weeks without knowing anything is happening, an angel handles it, and Daniel is told about the fight afterwards, as background. The clearest territorial-spirit passage in Scripture contains no territorial-spirit technique at all.

Jude 9

Michael would not do it either

Disputing with the devil over the body of Moses, the archangel “did not presume to pronounce a blasphemous judgment, but said, ‘The Lord rebuke you.’” A great deal of contemporary warfare teaching is more forward with a principality than Michael was with Satan.

2 Corinthians 10:4–5

The strongholds verse is about arguments

Quoted over cities constantly — and the sentence finishes: “we destroy arguments and every lofty opinion raised against the knowledge of God, and take every thought captive to obey Christ.” The strongholds are logismoi, reasonings. The most-cited text in this whole field is about what people believe, not where they live.

Acts 19

In the most loaded city in Acts, Paul holds lectures

Two years of daily reasoning in the hall of Tyrannus. He does not survey Ephesus, name Artemis’ principality or hold a march. The city changes because the word spreads and people burn their own books — and the cautionary tale about borrowed authority, the sons of Sceva, sits twenty verses earlier in the same chapter, apparently on purpose.

The movement that went the other way

From the mid-1980s a serious missiological programme was built on these texts. Its central practice was spiritual mapping — researching a place’s history and religious past in order to identify the spirit holding it.

How it was built

1986 — C. Peter Wagner, Spiritual Power and Church Growth, launching “strategic-level spiritual warfare”.
George Otis Jr. develops spiritual mapping.
Cindy Jacobs and Ed Silvoso build prayer campaigns on it.
1996 — Wagner answers his critics in Confronting the Powers.

It was answered at length. Chuck Lowe’s Territorial Spirits and World Evangelisation? is a biblical, historical and missiological critique of the whole programme. And the Lausanne Movement published its own assessment, written by A. Scott Moreau, which is worth reading precisely because it is not a hatchet job.

Moreau credits the movement with real gains before he says anything hard — and the list of gains is not short. Then: requiring practitioners to discover and name territorial spirits comes uncomfortably close to “Christian animism”. Spiritual mapping is too subjective to identify anything reliably. And the core strategy is not found in Scripture or church history “at least not without some serious stretching.” It closes on Clinton Arnold’s summary: Christians have no biblical warrant to bind or expel a territorial spirit directly, though they may certainly ask God to restrain its influence so the gospel can run.

What Moreau credits it with

Taking the powers seriously in a discipline that had gone managerial. Putting prayer above strategy. Making church unity a spiritual issue. Naming the domination systems inside cultures. Recovering corporate repentance.

Five gains, stated first. The balanced source is the one worth quoting in both directions.

Worth knowing that a quite different reading exists: Walter Wink’s Naming the Powers takes the powers as the interiority of institutions — a regime, a corporation or a school really does have a spirit, which is neither merely a figure of speech nor a separate personal being. It is a contested reading and it does not have the last word, but it accounts for something the other two readings keep tripping over: why the powers language in Paul is so relentlessly political.

Where that leaves the three claims

The unseen realm is real, populated, and organised into offices and jurisdictions. Angels, princes, thrones, dominions, a council that deliberates and is held to account.
Well attested
Some of that authority is attached to territory, and a place can carry the character of what is done in it. Daniel 10 is explicit; Pergamum, Philippi, Ephesus and the land laws point the same way.
Attested, thinly
You can identify a given city’s spirit, name it, and evict it — and doing so is a precondition for the gospel spreading there.
Not attested

Those three lines are the whole argument of this page compressed. Holding the first two while refusing the third is the narrow and slightly uncomfortable place where the evidence actually sits.

08 The people who say they can see it

Scripture is oddly uninterested in how much you saw

Some people report seeing the realm directly rather than sensing it. The Bible has this category, treats it as real, and hedges it far more carefully than its modern admirers do.

Elisha’s servant panics at an army surrounding the city; Elisha prays, “LORD, open his eyes,” and the hills are full of horses and chariots of fire. Read the mechanics of that: the reality was there the whole time, the servant was a believer standing next to a prophet, and he still could not see it until someone asked God to let him. Sight is granted, in a moment, for a purpose. It is not equipment you own.

The others it records

Micaiah, hauled before two kings, reports a council scene — the LORD on his throne, the host of heaven to his right and left, deliberating.
Balaam’s eyes are uncovered. Ezekiel is taken. John is in the Spirit on the Lord’s Day.

And Paul lists diakriseis pneumatōn, “discernings of spirits”, among the gifts distributed “as he wills” — plural, given out unevenly, and sitting in a chapter whose entire argument is that no one member gets to be the body.

Who is actually in this conversation

The claim is not historical. It is being made now, at scale, by people with real audiences — and it is being answered, refined and disciplined by other people with real audiences. Here is the map, sorted by what each one is actually for. Nobody on it is endorsed and nobody is dismissed; the lane and the caution are the useful part.

Lane 1

They say they see it

First-person reports of direct sight. The primary material, and the least testable.

Micah Turnbo
Behold Wonder · formerly Vineyard Church Northwest, Cincinnati

A seer prophet who said he perceived the spirit realm much as he perceived the physical one, and had since childhood — dreams, visions, angels, demons. Collected in The Invitation, and carried across the podcasts below: The Deep End ep. 020, Blurry Creatures ep. 89 “I See Angels”.

Then the tests in this section ran, in public. In 2025 the apologist Mike Winger published a detailed warning, objecting less to the fact of the visions than to their content — casual, domestic depictions of God and of angels that he argued could not be squared with how Scripture describes such encounters. Concerns spread through the wider Vineyard movement. His senior pastor defended him, then withdrew the defence.

How it ended in May 2025 Turnbo, Vineyard Northwest and Vineyard USA “together decided that Micah will take an extended leave from public ministry to rest, reflect, and seek guidance.” His accounts went dark. His own statement: “I recognize that some of my writings and interview comments have included statements that do not align with Scripture.” Read that as what it is — a body weighed it, and the man conceded rather than doubling down, which is rarer than either the accusation or the defence.
Phoenix Steinfeldt
@phoenixfromphoenix · Redeem Eden, with Cody Parker

Posts as a prophetic seer on what stands over places. Redeem Eden states the claim about as plainly as it can be stated: he is described as having the gift to distinguish between spirits and to see the spirit realm through sight and visions, and they “pray against principalities that are spiritually oppressing different geographical territories all over the Earth.” They also sell a 1.5–2 hour Christ-centred life-coaching and deliverance session.

Their own framing is more careful than either their followers or their critics tend to be: “We are not theologians. We are not pastors or preachers. We never went to seminary. We are not psychics. We are not mediums. We are not fortune tellers.” That disclaims the clerical category and the occult one in the same breath, and leaves them standing on the gift alone.

Watch for that is precisely the third claim in section 07 — the one marked not attested — offered as a service. Note too that the platform’s own related searches include the word debunked: the material travels fast and so does the pushback, and neither travels with a way of settling it.
The wider seer-ministry world
Doug Overmyer / SeersSee · Jennifer LeClaire on the “seer anointing”

An established body of teaching, training and books aimed at people who believe they have this gift — and at their families, pastors and therapists, which is a more sober framing than outsiders tend to expect.

Take from it a distinction it draws that most of its critics do not: a seer sees and declares; a discerner perceives the source of a thing. Different gifts, different jobs — and conflating them is most of the confusion.
Lane 2

They take the unseen seriously, at length

Long-form audio is where this subject actually lives now. These differ enormously in rigour, and the difference is the thing to track.

The Deep End
Taylor Welch · ~1,000 episodes · with NOVOS Network

“Deep conversations on taboo & controversial topics that will challenge the beliefs you’ve grown up with” — a wide-ranging show running at the seam where business, psychology and spirituality meet. Micah Turnbo’s episode 020 is the seeing-into-the-spirit-realm one. Welch’s companion platform NOVOS Network describes itself as blending “neuroscience and cognitive behavioral training with integrated spiritual awakening.”

His actual thesis deserves stating properly, because it is the most sophisticated version of the position this page is arguing with. Welch holds that vibes, atmospheres and resonance are not New Age imports at all — they are real created mechanics that the occult counterfeited and the church abandoned, so that reclaiming them is recovering stolen property rather than importing anything. On that much, this page substantially agrees: sections 01 to 03 say the faculty is real and that dismissing it is its own error.

Where it parts company is the appeal to laboratory evidence. Newberg’s neuroimaging of glossolalia is real published work; “the brain has circuitry designed to yield control to the Holy Spirit” is an interpretation the scans cannot carry, and heart-coherence claims are further out still. The thesis does not need them — it is a theological claim and it stands or falls as one. Borrowing a laboratory’s authority is exactly the move section 01 flags, and it is the one thing here worth resisting.
Blurry Creatures
Luke Rodgers & Nate Henry

The Nephilim, the Watchers, the sons of God, the divine council and cosmic geography — alongside cryptids, ancient giants and UFO lore, all through a biblical lens. It takes the strange seriously instead of laughing at it, which is rarer than it should be.

Watch for a very wide aperture. Signal-to-speculation varies sharply by episode and by guest, and the show does not always mark the join.
The Lord of Spirits
Fr. Andrew Stephen Damick & Fr. Stephen De Young · Ancient Faith, since 2020

Two Orthodox priests on gods, angels, demons and the union of the seen and unseen, worked out from Scripture and the Fathers. The most rooted thing on this list by a distance: it is not rediscovering the unseen realm, because that tradition never filed it away. There is a book of the same name.

Read it for the shock of finding that nearly everything treated as fringe here has been ordinary, carefully governed doctrine somewhere, for a very long time.
Naked Bible · and BibleProject
Michael S. Heiser (458 eps, ended Jan 2023) · Tim Mackie, Spiritual Beings series

The scholarly end of the podcast lane. Heiser worked the divine council from the Hebrew and the Second Temple literature until weeks before his death in February 2023; the archive stands. BibleProject’s Spiritual Beings series is the gentlest on-ramp there is — elohim as a title rather than a name, the council as God’s staff, and Deuteronomy 32:8–9 laid out slowly.

Start here if the ledger in section 05 was the part that interested you.
Lane 3

They did the scholarship

Slower, duller, and the reason anything on this page can be checked at all.

Paul Hiebert
“The Flaw of the Excluded Middle”, 1982

Section 03 is his. Thirteen pages, and it explains why this argument keeps happening between people who cannot hear each other. If you read one thing off this entire page, read that one.

Michael S. Heiser
The Unseen Realm, 2015

The book that put the divine council in front of a general readership. His doctorate was on precisely this — the council in late canonical and Second Temple Jewish literature — so the popular book has real machinery underneath it.

Watch for how fast it gets stretched. A careful argument about ancient cosmology is routinely cited downstream as a licence for things Heiser himself declined to claim.
The powers debate
Clinton Arnold · Chuck Lowe · A. Scott Moreau · Walter Wink

Four positions worth knowing, disagreeing productively: Arnold on what Paul’s vocabulary meant, Lowe’s critique of strategic-level warfare, Moreau’s balanced Lausanne assessment, and Wink reading the powers as the interiority of institutions. Section 07 is built out of these four.

Lane 4

They apply the brakes

The most useful lane and the emptiest. Correction from inside a practice is worth more than correction from outside it.

The Remnant Radio
Joshua Lewis, Michael Miller & Michael Rowntree

Charismatic, practising, unembarrassed about the gifts — and in an episode called Deliverance Ministry Without the Weird they take on territorial spirits and spiritual mapping directly, cautioning against speculative demonic taxonomy and against a focus on the demonic that displaces the gospel.

Why it matters this is the rarest position available: inside the practice, with the brakes on. It also lands almost exactly on the three verdicts in section 07.
The Prophetic Standards Statement
April 2021 · drafted by Michael Brown & Joseph Mattera

After a conspicuous run of failed prophecies about the 2020 US election, around eighty-five charismatic leaders, academics, ministry heads and revivalists signed a four-page call for prophetic accountability — standards for how a word is given, weighed, and retracted when it fails.

Why it matters it is the movement disciplining itself in public. That is exactly what 1 Corinthians 14:29 asks for, and exactly what critics usually say never happens.
The cessationist critics
e.g. Justin Peters, and the discernment-ministry world

They hold that the revelatory gifts have ceased, so they do not weigh the claims in lane 1 — they reject the category. That is a real theological position with a real argument behind it, and it is not the same move as the flat exit in section 04.

Read them for accountability pressure, which they supply better than anyone. Not for a model of the realm, which they are not trying to give you — and be warned that traffic between these camps runs hot and yields far more heat than findings.

The question is not “is this person lying?” It is what test you would run if they weren’t.

This page is not going to hand out verdicts on the people in lane 1, because adjudicating a stranger’s inner life is not something a website can responsibly do. What it can do is hand over the tests — which are old, specific, and mostly ignored in both directions, by people who accept everything and by people who dismiss everything, who between them have skipped the same seven instructions.

And notice the shape of that map. The lanes are not ranked by how much they believe. Lane 4 believes as much as lane 1 — it simply has somewhere to put a maybe, which, per section 03, is the thing most people were never given.

1 John 4:1–3
Does it confess Jesus Christ come in the flesh?

The first test in the New Testament for a spirit is doctrinal, not experiential. Note the framing: “many false prophets have gone out into the world” is offered as the reason to test, not as a reason to stop listening.

Deuteronomy 18:22
Did the thing actually happen?

Brutally simple, and the one nobody keeps records for. A word about a city, a region or a year is checkable. If nobody wrote it down, nobody intends to check it.

Deuteronomy 13:1–3
And if it did happen — where is it taking you?

A sign that comes true does not validate the teacher. Accuracy is not the top of the hierarchy; direction is. This verse exists precisely because impressive things can be true and still be leading somewhere else.

Matthew 7:16
What is growing in their life, over years?

Fruit is a slow instrument on purpose. It cannot be produced for a camera, and it is the test that most cleanly separates a gift from a platform.

1 Corinthians 14:29
Who is allowed to weigh it?

“Let two or three prophets speak, and let the others weigh what is said.” Weighing is not optional and it is not self-administered. A seer accountable to no one has not been given a licence — they have skipped the instruction that comes with the gift.

1 Corinthians 14:32
Are they in control of themselves?

“The spirits of prophets are subject to prophets.” Being overwhelmed, unable to stop, or unable to wait is presented here as a mark against, not a mark of authenticity. This inverts most people’s intuition and it is worth sitting with.

1 Thessalonians 5:19–21
Are you keeping both halves?

“Do not quench the Spirit. Do not despise prophecies. But test everything; hold fast what is good.” One sentence, and it forbids both cheap exits at once. Almost everyone quotes the half that suits their temperament.

None of that is hypothetical. Between this page’s two named seers, one went through exactly this process while it was being written: a critic argued from doctrine rather than from vividness, a body weighed it, a denomination acted, and the man himself said in public that some of what he had taught did not align with Scripture. That is the machinery in section 08 running, in real time, with an outcome. It is worth holding on to, because the loudest voices on both sides insist it never happens.

Now count what is missing. Not one of those tests asks how vivid the vision was, how many beings were seen, how detailed the description is, or whether the seer’s account is internally consistent. Scripture is almost indifferent to the impressiveness of the experience and almost obsessive about the doctrine, the fruit and the accountability. That asymmetry is the single most useful thing in this section, because it is exactly backwards from how these claims are actually evaluated online, in either direction.

09 Somebody already built this

Two disciplines for the missing shelf

If section 03 is right — the middle shelf was excluded rather than refuted — then traditions that never lost it should have developed a way of working there. They did. Twice, in detail, and both are more careful than almost anything said on either side of the modern argument.

The desert: sort the thought by where it came from

Evagrius Ponticus (c. 346–399) went out to the Egyptian desert and produced a diagnostic scheme of eight generic logismoi — thoughts — from which he held all sin proceeds. John Cassian carried the list west and it eventually became the seven deadly sins, which is the only part most people have heard of.

The eight logismoi

gluttony · lust · avarice · sadness · anger · sloth · vainglory · pride

The list is the part that survived. The method attached to it is the part worth having.

Because the list is not the interesting part. The interesting part is the question he attached to it. Evagrius worked at discriminating thoughts that arise from the person, thoughts that come from demons, and thoughts that come from angels — not by how the thought felt, but by what it did, where it led, and what it left behind. That is exactly the modern question — is this me, or the room, or something else? — asked in the fourth century, with a working method attached and centuries of case notes.

And notice the word. When Paul says the weapons of our warfare demolish strongholds, the strongholds are logismous — reasonings. The same word. The single text most often stretched over cities is about the exact thing the desert tradition spent three centuries learning to read, one thought at a time, in a room by yourself.

A resonance, not a lineage

This is not a claim that Evagrius took the term from 2 Corinthians 10:5, and it should not be stretched into one. No source establishes dependence.

It is worth sitting with anyway.

Ignatius: read the direction, not the feeling

The second discipline arrives eleven hundred years later and is astonishingly practical. Ignatius of Loyola’s Rules for the Discernment of Spirits — fourteen of them for the First Week of the Exercises, a second set for the Second — give two names to what this page has been calling a vibe.

Consolation

Toward

Any increase of faith, hope and love. Interior joy that draws toward heavenly things. A quieting of the soul, giving it peace in its Creator.

Desolation

Away

Darkness of soul, disturbance, a movement toward what is low and earthly. The unquiet of agitations and temptations, want of confidence, hopelessness, lovelessness — finding oneself lazy and tepid and sad and as if separated from God.

Read those again and notice: they are directions, not sensations.

Ignatius is not asking whether you feel good. Plenty of desolation is comfortable and a fair amount of consolation is not. He is asking which way you are being moved. That single distinction would defuse most of the modern argument on its own, because almost everyone in it is reporting a sensation and calling it a diagnosis.

Then comes the fifth rule, which may be the most useful sentence ever written on this subject:

In time of desolation, never make a change.

Hold whatever you decided before it came. His reasoning is precise and worth having: in consolation it is chiefly the good spirit guiding and counselling, and in desolation the counsel is not one by which we can take a course to decide rightly. So the desolation may be telling you something true, and you are still not fit to act on it while you are inside it.

And immediately after comes the rule that stops this becoming passivity.

Change your effort, not your decisions — more prayer, more examination, more deliberate attention, aimed squarely at the thing sitting on you.

Rule 6, in his words

“Although we ought not to change our resolutions, it is very helpful to change ourselves intensely against the desolation.”

That is a five-hundred-year-old protocol for precisely what this page is about, and it beats most of what has been said since for one specific reason: it tells you what to do without requiring you to first name what you are feeling. You do not have to settle whether it was the room, your blood sugar or a principality. You have to not decide anything while it is on you.

And people keep rediscovering it

Here is the part that ought to settle the question of whether any of this is still live. Contemporary charismatic teaching on hearing God routinely arrives at a three-way sort — is this thought from God, from me, or from the enemy? — and at a set of tells for the third.

The tells, as taught now

urgency · anxiety · condemnation · confusion · a push to act right now

Against which it sets peace, clarity, and conviction without panic. Evagrius’ three origins and Ignatius’ fifth rule, arrived at without the lineage.

That is Evagrius’ three origins and Ignatius’ fifth rule, arrived at independently, apparently without the lineage. And the convergence is worth more than either party’s claim to it: two disciplines separated by eleven hundred years, and a podcast separated from both by another five, keep producing the same warning about urgency. Something is being noticed repeatedly, by people with no reason to agree.

Which cuts against the flat exit, and it is also why the desert and Ignatius are worth going back to rather than reinventing: they have the same insight with sixteen centuries of case notes attached, and they will not let you act on the mood you are currently in.

Five vocabularies, possibly one event

Line them up and the modern argument gets a great deal smaller.

The shrug“The energy in there is off.”
The charismatic“There’s no peace here. I sensed oppression.”
The Ignatian“I notice desolation. I will not decide anything today.”
The clinical“My nervous system moved toward threat.”
The environmental“Low ceiling, no daylight, my back to the door.”

These are not necessarily five claims. They may be five languages for one event, and a great deal of the heat between the people speaking them is about vocabulary rather than about what happened in the room.

That does not make them equally useful, and the difference is instructive. Four of the five stop at description. Only one of them tells you what to do next — and it is the oldest, and it was written by someone who took the unseen entirely seriously and still would not let you act on a mood.

10 The working tool

You walked in. Something is off. Now work down.

Seven rungs, in this order. The order is the whole point: the top rungs are boring and account for most of it, and the bottom one is only honest once the six above it have actually been asked.

1 Your bodySlept, eaten, ill, in pain, over-caffeinated
What it looks like when this is the answer

The feeling tracks your day rather than the place. It was there in the car. It lifts after you eat. It is worse at four in the afternoon and gone by seven. You get it in rooms that have nothing in common except that you were tired in all of them.

This is the most common answer by a wide margin and the one people are least willing to check, because checking it is humiliating and finding a principality is not.

Do: eat something, and come back. That is not a joke and it is not a deflection — it is the cheapest possible test, and a reading that survives a sandwich is worth more than one that does not.
2 The room itselfLight, air, sound, sightlines, your back to the door
What it looks like when this is the answer

Everyone feels it, including people with no interest in any of this. It is stronger in one corner. It changes when you move seats. Low ceiling, no daylight, a hum you stop hearing after ten minutes but never stop registering, stale air, an entrance behind you that you cannot see.

Humans read space for prospect and refuge — can I see, can I not be seen — long before they read it for anything else. A room that fails that test feels wrong to everybody and has done since long before anyone had a word for it.

Do: move. Change seat, open a door, step outside for ninety seconds. If it lifts at the threshold and returns at the threshold, you have your answer and it is architectural.
3 The peopleWhose tension did you just walk into the middle of
What it looks like when this is the answer

The room was fine last week. It went strange the moment two particular people were in it together. It clears when one of them leaves. You cannot name what is wrong, but you could point at where in the room it is coming from — and if you check later, you will usually turn out to have pointed correctly.

You are an excellent instrument for this and a terrible instrument for knowing that this is what you are reading. Thin-slicing and emotional contagion hand you a conclusion with the working scrubbed off, and the mind files it under the room because the room is what you can see.

Do: ask one person, plainly and privately, whether something happened before you got there. This resolves an enormous fraction of “heavy” rooms in a single sentence, and almost nobody asks.
4 YouWhat did this place remind you of
What it looks like when this is the answer

Nobody else feels it. It is specific to you and it is old. The smell of the carpet, the particular fluorescent tone, a voice with a cadence like someone’s. Shame and memory arrive dressed as the present tense and they do not announce themselves as memory.

The tell is disproportion — a reaction of a size the situation cannot account for. The other tell is that it is somehow always the same feeling, in unrelated places, which is a fact about the reader and not about the buildings.

Do: ask “when have I felt exactly this before?” and wait for the second answer, not the first. If a face arrives, the room is innocent.
5 What is being done herePresent tense — what is this place for
What it looks like when this is the answer

Everyone feels it, nobody says it, and it has a name that would be socially expensive to use. A workplace organised so that people are afraid feels like fear. A venue whose business model requires people to lose money feels like it. A house where something is not being said feels like the thing not being said.

This is the biblical pattern from the last section and it is the rung most likely to be true and least likely to be checked, because checking it means saying an uncomfortable sentence about people you have to keep seeing.

Do: finish the sentence “this place runs on ——.” If the honest ending is fear, debt, appetite, contempt or silence, you have almost certainly found it, and no further explanation is required.
6 Its historyWhat happened here, and is it being denied
What it looks like when this is the answer

Something real happened here and the place is arranged so as not to mention it. That is a genuine category — Scripture treats bloodshed and injustice as leaving a claim on land, and communities that have buried something behave in recognisable ways.

But handle this rung with both hands. It is the easiest one on the ladder to abuse, because you almost never actually know the history, a rumour will produce precisely the same feeling as a fact, and being told a building has a past is itself one of the strongest documented causes of feeling something in it. If you learned the history first and felt it second, you have not confirmed anything.

Do: notice the order of operations. Felt it, then learned the history — interesting. Learned the history, then felt it — discard, or at least do not build on it.
7 The realmOnly now, and held loosely
What it looks like in the reports

Consistently, and across traditions that are not talking to each other, the description is not fear. It is a withdrawal of peace — a check, a pressure, an unaccountable heaviness, a clear sense of not here or not now that is not attached to any danger you can point at. It does not track blood sugar. It lifts at the door and does not resume in the car. It shows up in places you had no expectations about, and fails to show up in places you did.

That last one is the closest thing to a real test available: a reading that surprises you is worth more than one that agrees with you. Everything on rungs one through six is capable of producing the feeling you already expected.

And then the response, which is deliberately boring

Pray, and do not announce. Stay if you are sent and leave if you are not. Bless the place — in Luke 10 the peace you carry either rests on a house or comes back to you, and both outcomes are treated as normal. Shake the dust off if it comes to that. Tell one person who is capable of telling you that you are wrong.

Do not name it, address it, rebuke it, or build a doctrine on one room. Daniel did none of those things about the one principality the Bible actually names, and Michael declined to do them to the devil. Do ask God to restrain it and get on with the thing you came to do — which, in Acts, is the entire method.

You do not skip rungs. And you do not stop early.

Stopping at one is the flat exit wearing a lab coat. Starting at seven is the spooky exit wearing a Bible. Both of them save you the work, and the work is the only part that was ever going to tell you anything.

11 The part nobody asks about

You are one of the vibes

This entire page has treated you as a receiver — an instrument walking into rooms, taking readings, working out what is in the air. That is half of it, and it is the flattering half.

Scripture spends at least as much attention on the other direction, and it says so three times, in three different registers. In each one somebody walking into a building is described as a source and not only a sensor.

Luke 10:5–6

Peace is portable

The disciples are sent out and told to say “peace be to this house” on entry. If a son of peace is there, the peace rests on him; if not, it returns to you. Read it as physics for a second: it is carried by a person, it lands on a place, and if the place will not hold it, it comes back — undiminished, still yours.

1 Samuel 16:23

One person changed the room

When the spirit tormented Saul, David played, and it lifted. One person, one instrument, and the atmosphere of a room changed — which is the smallest and least deniable version of everything this page has been describing.

2 Corinthians 2:15–16

You are an aroma

Reaching for a way to say what Christians are doing as they move through the world, Paul chooses the least abstract sense there is. “We are the aroma of Christ” — to some a fragrance from life to life, to others from death to death. The same smell. Two reactions. Nobody in that verse can name what they are smelling.

“That coffee shop is not a vibe” — and the smell they cannot place might be you.

Which loops back to where this started. Someone walks out of a room and says it smelled weird in there. Sometimes it did. Sometimes they were tired. Sometimes six people had been tense for an hour before they arrived, and sometimes the place runs on something they would rather not name out loud, and sometimes — rarely, and never on demand — there is something there that was there before any of them turned up, and Scripture is not embarrassed about saying so.

And sometimes the reading is running the other way entirely, and they are simply reacting to whoever else walked in.

The failure mode worth worrying about is not misreading the atmosphere. It is Jacob’s. He slept in the middle of the most significant thing that had ever happened to him, woke up, and said the sentence that ought to unsettle anybody who has ever dismissed a room: surely the LORD is in this place, and I did not know it.
